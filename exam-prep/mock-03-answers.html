1. B - At the beginning of the QRS complex.

Why: The PR interval measures from the onset of atrial depolarisation (P-wave onset) to the onset of ventricular depolarisation (QRS onset).

Not A: The start of T occurs after ventricular depolarisation.

Not C: Including all of QRS would extend beyond the PR interval.

Not D: The end of T is used to mark the end of the QT interval.

Source: lecture-7:Q19 | 6–7

2. A - Renin–angiotensin effects and renal salt retention.

Why: The rapid baroreflex stabilizes pressure within seconds, while the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system supports longer-term pressure and volume.

Not B: Aldosterone supports sodium retention rather than its suppression.

Not C: Baroreflexes remain available during sustained standing.

Not D: Loss of arteriolar responsiveness would worsen orthostatic control.

Source: circulation-3:Q9 | Circulation 3, slide 4; Chapter 19 §3

3. A - Cortical signals acting through the hypothalamus on autonomic centres.

Why: Emotions such as anger begin in the cortex. The cortex drives the hypothalamus, which excites the vasomotor centre, raising sympathetic output so heart rate and blood pressure rise.

Not B: Renal volume control is too slow and not the primary emotional route.

Not C: The CSF pathways do not directly account for this autonomic response.

Not D: Platelet serotonin is a local vascular constrictor, not the main emotional signal.

Source: circulation-2:Q57 | 30, 33

4. A - Retinal perfusion falls as blood shifts toward the feet.

Why: Positive g causes leg pooling and reduced pressure at eye level, so retinal perfusion can fail before cerebral consciousness.

Not B: Pressure at eye level decreases during positive g.

Not C: The mechanism is reduced eye-level arterial perfusion, not an inevitable ICP excess.

Not D: Headward blood movement occurs during negative g.

Source: circulation-3:Q16 | Circulation 3, slide 9; Chapter 19 §6

5. A - All valves closed, ventricular pressure rises, volume fixed.

Why: Both ventricles generate rising pressure with all inlet and outlet valves closed, so neither receives nor ejects blood.

Not B: Open valves permit volume exchange.

Not C: AV valves do not remain open once ventricular pressure rises above atrial.

Not D: Falling pressure describes isovolumetric relaxation, not contraction.

Source: circulation-1:Q19 | 14–15

6. D - Reduced plasma oncotic pressure promotes oedema.

Why: Proteinuria reduces circulating albumin and its inward oncotic force, favouring movement of fluid into interstitium.

Not A: Viscosity is not the principal mechanism of protein-loss oedema.

Not B: Capillary permeability elsewhere need not decrease.

Not C: Albumin loss lowers oncotic pressure.

Source: circulation-1:Q88 | 93

7. A - Ventricular contraction compresses intramural vessels.

Why: High left ventricular wall tension during systole squeezes intramyocardial coronary vessels; relaxation permits better flow.

Not B: Coronary ostia remain open through the cardiac cycle.

Not C: Aortic pressure persists during diastole and drives coronary flow.

Not D: Coronary flow increases during relaxation rather than disappearing.

Source: lecture-6:Q22 | 27; Chapter 23 §3

8. B - Increased cAMP/PKA signalling → greater calcium availability.

Why: Beta-1 signalling raises cAMP and PKA activity, enhancing calcium entry and cycling for stronger contraction.

Not A: Blocking release channels would reduce systolic calcium release.

Not C: Vagal slowing is not the main ventricular inotropic pathway.

Not D: Beta-1 stimulation raises, not lowers, cAMP.

Source: circulation-1:Q10 | 46–47; Chapter 2 §7

9. C - Deep vein compression with one-way valves.

Why: Calf contractions compress deep veins, while competent venous valves favour upward flow toward the heart instead of backflow.

Not A: Venous return requires directed pressure gradients, not dilation alone.

Not B: Capillaries do not function as contractile muscle pumps.

Not D: Arteries do not rely on venous-style one-way valves.

Source: circulation-3:Q6 | Circulation 3, slide 6; Chapter 19 §3

10. C - Direct vasomotor-centre excitation greatly increases sympathetic outflow.

Why: Hypoxic, hypercapnic medullary tissue drives a strong sympathetic pressor response to restore cerebral blood flow.

Not A: Inhibition would tend to lower vascular tone and pressure.

Not B: Vagal discharge slows the heart and is not the primary pressor mechanism.

Not D: ANP favours dilation and volume loss.

Source: circulation-2:Q54 | 31–32, 35–36

11. B - Several leads are calculated from combinations of the same electrode voltages.

Why: A lead is an electrical viewpoint calculated from one or more electrode potentials, so ten physical contacts can generate twelve different standard recordings.

Not A: Routine twelve-lead ECG does not require intracardiac electrodes.

Not C: All twelve are electrical leads; pressure sensors do not generate extra leads.

Not D: The limb contacts remain in place during a standard recording.

Source: lecture-7:Q14 | 3–5; Chapter 6 §3

12. A - It shortens the heart-to-brain hydrostatic column.

Why: A more horizontal posture decreases the vertical distance over which acceleration reduces pressure toward the head and brain.

Not B: Increasing the vertical distance would worsen the gradient.

Not C: Enhanced ankle pooling would reduce central venous return.

Not D: Greater dependent filtration would not protect brain perfusion.

Source: circulation-3:Q17 | Circulation 3, slide 11; Chapter 19 §6

13. A - A small or biphasic ventricular deflection.

Why: Only the component of the electrical vector along the lead axis contributes strongly to the deflection; a near-perpendicular vector has a small projection.

Not B: A strongly downward deflection needs a substantial vector component away from the positive pole.

Not C: A strongly upright deflection needs a substantial vector component towards the positive pole.

Not D: Vector orientation changes amplitude and polarity, not necessarily conduction duration.

Source: lecture-7:Q3 | 3–5; Chapter 6 §2

14. B - 4 L/min.

Why: Flow equals pressure difference divided by resistance: 80/20 = 4 L/min. Increasing resistance with the same pressure difference would lower flow.

Not A: Two L/min would correspond to resistance of 40.

Not C: Eight L/min would correspond to resistance of 10.

Not D: Three L/min does not fit the given ratio.

Source: circulation-1:Q50 | 61, 67–70

15. B - Intermittent cannon a waves.

Why: Unsynchronised atrial contraction against a shut AV valve can produce large intermittent cannon a waves.

Not A: Giant v waves primarily suggest tricuspid regurgitation.

Not C: Atrial contractions still cause pressure variations.

Not D: Venous pulsations can persist despite AV block.

Source: circulation-1:Q72 | 26; Chapter 11 §7

16. C - It falls by about 5 mmHg.

Why: The arterial–venous difference changes by −30 minus −25, or −5 mmHg. Falling venous and intracranial pressures therefore help protect cerebral perfusion.

Not A: That ignores the fall in cerebral venous pressure.

Not B: The difference becomes slightly smaller, not larger.

Not D: The changes cannot be added as two rises.

Source: circulation-3:Q7 | Circulation 3, slide 5; Chapter 19 §3

17. D - Antidromic impulses in branches of a sensory axon.

Why: The flare is an axon reflex: sensory-nerve branches release vasodilator neuropeptides in surrounding skin without a spinal reflex.

Not A: Lymphatic filtration does not produce this arteriolar flare.

Not B: The surrounding redness is functional vasodilation, not thrombosis.

Not C: A central synaptic reflex is not needed for the axon reflex.

Source: lecture-6:Q51 | 40

18. D - Raised capillary pressure increases outward filtration.

Why: The increased hydrostatic pressure in dependent leg capillaries pushes more fluid into the interstitium and promotes oedema.

Not A: A lower capillary pressure would oppose outward filtration.

Not B: Standing promotes dependent filtration; it does not stop capillary exchange.

Not C: Plasma oncotic pressure ordinarily favours fluid retention within blood.

Source: circulation-3:Q4 | Circulation 3, slide 3; Chapter 19 §2

19. C - Rapid passive ventricular filling.

Why: S3 occurs during rapid early-diastolic ventricular filling, as blood entering the chamber causes vibrations.

Not A: Atrial depolarisation precedes the atrial contribution later in diastole.

Not B: Isovolumetric contraction occurs near S1.

Not D: Aortic valve opening occurs during ventricular systole.

Source: circulation-1:Q29 | 28

20. B - A possible late ventricular repolarisation phenomenon.

Why: The lecture describes a U wave, when seen after T, as the final remnants of ventricular repolarisation. Its precise physiological origin is uncertain.

Not A: Atrial depolarisation is the P wave preceding QRS.

Not C: Rapid ventricular depolarisation is represented by QRS, not U.

Not D: The ventricular plateau is associated with the ST segment.

Source: lecture-7:Q29 | 10

21. C - To adjust body heat gain and loss.

Why: Cutaneous vessels dilate to increase heat loss and constrict to conserve heat; flow can far exceed nutritional needs.

Not A: Skin metabolic demand is relatively modest compared with variation in perfusion.

Not B: Heart pacemaking is not the main thermoregulatory role of skin circulation.

Not D: Pulmonary-systemic pressure equality is unrelated to thermoregulation.

Source: lecture-6:Q46 | 39–41

22. D - The ventricle must generate higher pressure for ejection.

Why: Higher arterial pressure increases ventricular pressure work and wall stress, requiring more oxygen for force generation.

Not A: Higher afterload generally increases wall stress.

Not B: The myocardium continues to contract against the higher load.

Not C: Coronary oxygen extraction remains substantial.

Source: circulation-1:Q48 | 54; Chapter 8 §7

23. C - Its small electrical signal is obscured by the ventricular QRS complex.

Why: Atrial repolarisation occurs around the time of ventricular depolarisation; the much larger QRS electrical signal masks it on the surface trace.

Not A: Repolarisation does generate voltage differences, although the signal is small.

Not B: Atrial repolarisation does not need to await the beginning of ventricular diastole.

Not D: That statement does not explain why the wave is not separately visible.

Source: lecture-7:Q25 | Chapter 6 §4 (approved-chapter explanation)

24. B - It lasts 0.14 s and is longer than the stated usual range.

Why: At 25 mm/s, 3.5 small squares equal 3.5 × 0.04 = 0.14 s, above the lecture’s usual QRS duration of 0.06–0.10 s.

Not A: 0.10 s corresponds to 2.5 small squares at the standard speed.

Not C: 0.07 s incorrectly halves the 0.14-s measured duration.

Not D: 0.35 s does not follow from the standard 0.04-s small-square duration.

Source: lecture-7:Q39 | 8, 13

25. D - Constriction of systemic arterioles and veins.

Why: Sympatholytic drugs blunt reflex arteriolar and venous constriction, so the circulation cannot adequately oppose blood pooling and falling arterial pressure on standing.

Not A: Walking uses a mechanical muscle pump, not this autonomic drug pathway.

Not B: Venous valve directionality is mechanical and not directly abolished by sympatholytic drugs.

Not C: Cerebral metabolic vasodilatation is not directly a sympathetic vasoconstrictor response.

Source: circulation-3:Q15 | Circulation 3, slide 8; Chapter 19 §5

26. D - Compression of brain vessels and reduced driving pressure.

Why: Rising intracranial pressure compresses intracranial vessels and lowers cerebral perfusion pressure, risking medullary ischaemia.

Not A: Raised intracranial pressure does not require systemic blood loss.

Not B: Dilation cannot prevent the loss of cerebral driving pressure as intracranial pressure rises.

Not C: Perfusion initially becomes harder as intracranial pressure rises.

Source: circulation-2:Q53 | 31–32, 35–36

27. C - Aortic pressure exceeds ventricular pressure.

Why: When ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure, brief reversal tendency closes the aortic valve and starts isovolumetric relaxation.

Not A: Venous pressure does not directly determine aortic valve closure.

Not B: Atrial pressure governs mitral opening, not aortic closure.

Not D: Ventricular pressure exceeding aortic pressure opens the valve.

Source: circulation-1:Q23 | 17

28. B - Red line, flare and wheal.

Why: The classical triple response consists of local redness, a spreading flare and an oedematous wheal.

Not A: A white line is a separate lighter-stroke reaction.

Not C: Wheal formation requires local fluid leak rather than none.

Not D: Thrombosis and scar are not normal features.

Source: lecture-6:Q52 | 40

29. A - Their operating range resets around the sustained level.

Why: When pressure stays high for days, the baroreceptors reset and treat the new level as normal, so they defend it instead of correcting it. Long-term pressure is set mainly by the kidneys’ control of salt and water.

Not B: Unbounded sensitivity would strongly oppose the persistent rise.

Not C: Sensory fibres do not become motor fibres.

Not D: Resetting does not require loss of every afferent signal.

Source: circulation-2:Q44 | 18, 24–27

30. D - A brief local vasoconstrictor response.

Why: The lecture describes a transient white reaction due to constriction; it is distinct from the classical red–flare–wheal response.

Not A: Wheal is raised local oedema from permeability.

Not B: The red line is a separate vasodilator response.

Not C: Flare is surrounding redness from arteriolar dilation.

Source: lecture-6:Q49 | 39–40

31. D - Overlapping endothelial flaps admit interstitial fluid.

Why: Initial lymphatic endothelial flaps allow interstitial fluid and proteins to enter when surrounding pressure exceeds lymphatic pressure.

Not A: Initial lymphatic capillaries lack a thick continuous smooth-muscle coat.

Not B: There is no single central lymphatic heart.

Not C: Initial lymphatics are relatively permeable to interstitial proteins.

Source: circulation-1:Q93 | 60; Chapter 15 §2

32. D - Other conducting tissues retain latent automaticity.

Why: Subsidiary pacemakers in the AV junction or Purkinje system can spontaneously depolarise, usually at slower rates.

Not A: Autonomic nerves modify pacemaking but do not directly activate every myocyte.

Not B: Coordinated contraction still requires electrical activation.

Not C: Valves are passive mechanical structures.

Source: circulation-1:Q13 | 7; Chapter 3 §4

33. C - The arterial baroreflex reacts to the sympathetic pressure rise.

Why: Medullary ischaemia first raises sympathetic pressure; arterial baroreceptors then increase vagal drive and slow the heart.

Not A: β₁ stimulation would usually accelerate, not slow, the heart.

Not B: Cardiac autonomic signalling remains central to the response.

Not D: Atrial volume receptors respond chiefly to filling, not this sequence.

Source: circulation-2:Q55 | 32–36

34. C - Hypoxia together with accumulating carbon dioxide.

Why: Poor medullary perfusion produces hypoxia and hypercapnia, which strongly stimulate the vasomotor centre to raise pressure.

Not A: High oxygen and low CO₂ do not represent medullary ischaemia.

Not B: Low acidity and abundant oxygen oppose the described stimulus.

Not D: Adequate oxygen delivery would not produce the ischaemic response.

Source: circulation-2:Q52 | 30–32

35. C - Pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary veins.

Why: The right ventricle pumps blood through pulmonary arteries to lung capillaries; pulmonary veins return it to the left atrium.

Not A: The aorta receives systemic output from the left ventricle.

Not B: That is mainly the systemic circulation after left ventricular ejection.

Not D: Pulmonary veins carry blood into the left atrium rather than away from the right ventricle.

Source: circulation-1:Q1 | 2–6

36. A - NTS activation increases depressor and vagal signalling.

Why: More stretch → faster baroreceptor firing → more input to the NTS. The NTS excites the depressor area (which inhibits the pressor area) and the vagal neurons, so sympathetic output falls and vagal output rises.

Not B: The heart remains under autonomic regulation during the reflex.

Not C: The reflex suppresses rather than increases sympathetic pressor drive.

Not D: Increased wall stretch increases, rather than decreases, afferent traffic.

Source: circulation-2:Q40 | 22–24

37. D - Venoconstriction shifts stored blood centrally.

Why: Sympathetic venoconstriction reduces the capacity of the venous reservoir and shifts stored blood toward the heart, supporting venous return and end-diastolic filling.

Not A: Systemic arterioles have no valves that close with sympathetic stimulation.

Not B: Venodilation generally increases peripheral capacitance and pooling.

Not C: Venoconstriction redistributes existing blood rather than rapidly generating plasma.

Source: circulation-1:Q73 | 73–75, 97–98; Chapter 12 §2

38. A - Prolonged ischaemia can produce myocyte necrosis.

Why: Prolonged severe ischaemia can cause irreversible myocardial cell death; transient angina does not necessarily cause necrosis.

Not B: Infarction can occur across a range of heart rates.

Not C: Transient angina ordinarily reflects reversible myocardial ischaemia.

Not D: Stable angina may result from fixed stenosis without rupture.

Source: lecture-6:Q28 | 29

39. B - The elastic recoil of large arteries sustains diastolic flow.

Why: Elastic arteries stretch during systole and recoil during diastole, smoothing pulsatile ejection into continuous downstream flow.

Not A: Venous valves prevent backflow but do not create aortic pressure.

Not C: Respiratory movement mainly assists venous return.

Not D: Capillaries lack the pumping muscle required to drive systemic flow.

Source: circulation-1:Q49 | 55–57

40. B - Restricted coronary flow reserve cannot meet increased demand.

Why: A narrowed vessel may deliver adequate resting flow but cannot dilate enough to meet exercise demand, causing reversible ischaemia.

Not A: Plaque does not disappear with exertion.

Not C: Exercise raises myocardial work and oxygen requirements.

Not D: Resting coronary flow may be sufficient despite reduced reserve.

Source: lecture-6:Q27 | 29; Chapter 23 §6
