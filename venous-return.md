# Veins and Venous Return

## Venous return determines how much is available to pump
**Venous return (VR)** is blood flowing back to the right atrium per unit time. In a steady state, **venous return equals cardiac output**. When venous return falls abruptly, right ventricular filling drops and—after a delay—left ventricular preload and stroke volume fall.

Veins hold a major share of blood volume because they are compliant. Increased sympathetic venous tone reduces venous capacitance and mobilizes blood toward the heart. The skeletal-muscle pump, valves and respiratory pressure changes facilitate return from dependent limbs.

## Pressure gradients, posture and muscle pumping
Blood flows toward the right atrium along a driving pressure gradient. A useful conceptual Guyton relationship is **VR = (mean systemic filling pressure − right atrial pressure)/resistance to venous return**, interpreted with attention to vascular collapse and physiological limitations.

When standing still, gravity promotes venous pooling in the legs. Walking or subtle calf contractions compress veins; valves promote one-way movement. This explains why prolonged motionless standing can cause light-headedness and why soldiers subtly contract their leg muscles while standing.

Inspiration usually lowers intrathoracic pressure, facilitating systemic venous return to the right heart; effects on left-heart filling and BP are more complex and may be delayed.

## Pressure, compliance and venous reservoir
Veins are **capacitance vessels**: they contain a large proportion of resting circulating blood because they can accommodate substantial volume at low pressure. Venoconstriction decreases venous capacity and tends to shift blood toward the central circulation, raising **mean systemic filling pressure** and venous return when other conditions permit.

Venous return depends on the pressure difference between peripheral veins/mean systemic filling pressure and the right atrium, divided by resistance to venous return. In steady state **venous return = cardiac output** because blood cannot accumulate continuously in either side of a closed system. A normal right atrial/central venous pressure is generally low (often approximately 0–5 mmHg in a resting adult); avoid accepting every old slide's absolute value without the measurement context.

## Pumps and valves
Leg venous valves prevent backflow when the **skeletal-muscle pump** compresses deep veins, including during subtle contractions while standing. Inspiration reduces intrathoracic pressure, helping draw blood toward the thorax, although abdominal pressure and mechanical ventilation modify the effect. Arterial pulsation can aid nearby venous movement. Prolonged immobility permits pooling and may impair venous return.

In the head, collapsible extracranial veins can narrow under low pressure, but **dural venous sinuses** are held open by dural attachments. An injured/open venous sinus may admit air, causing venous air embolism.
