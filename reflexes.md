# Cardiovascular Reflexes and the Cushing Response

## Arterial baroreflex: a negative-feedback loop
Stretch-sensitive endings in the **carotid sinus** and **aortic arch** report pressure changes. Carotid afferents travel via **Hering's nerve**, a branch of glossopharyngeal nerve **IX**; aortic afferents largely via vagus **X**. They project to the medullary **nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS)**, which modifies autonomic outflow.

When BP rises: **more baroreceptor firing → less sympathetic and more vagal cardiac activity → lower HR, contractility and peripheral resistance → pressure tends to fall**. When BP falls, the reflex changes in the opposite direction. It is especially useful for rapid BP buffering, such as standing up.

## Atrial stretch and volume reflexes
Low-pressure cardiopulmonary receptors respond to changes in central blood volume. Atrial stretch can contribute to reflex tachycardia (**Bainbridge reflex**) and influence water/sodium regulation, although the actual HR response depends on competing reflexes and physiological context.

## Cushing response
Markedly raised **intracranial pressure (ICP)** reduces **cerebral perfusion pressure ≈ MAP − ICP** when venous pressure is not higher. Brainstem ischaemia can trigger intense sympathetic vasoconstriction and systemic hypertension to defend cerebral perfusion. That high arterial pressure then activates baroreceptors, contributing to **reflex bradycardia**. Altered respiration may occur; hypertension, bradycardia and abnormal respiration form the classic **Cushing triad**, a late warning sign of dangerous intracranial pressure elevation.

The stimulus for hypertension is threatened brain perfusion, **not** the baroreceptor reflex itself.

## Afferent nerves and feedback direction
Stretch at the **carotid sinus** raises firing through **Hering's nerve → glossopharyngeal nerve IX → NTS**. Stretch at the **aortic arch** sends signals through **vagus X → NTS**. A sudden rise in arterial pressure therefore increases afferent firing, promoting **vagal slowing** of the heart and decreased sympathetic resistance-vessel tone. A pressure fall reduces afferent firing, disinhibiting sympathetic activity and reducing vagal restraint. The baroreceptors sense **arterial wall stretch**, not blood oxygen directly.

## Atrial receptors and Bainbridge response
Low-pressure receptors at atrial and pulmonary venous junctions respond to filling. The lecturer describes atrial **type A** discharges around atrial contraction and **type B** discharges during filling. Increased atrial stretch can promote a **Bainbridge reflex** increase in HR, helping the heart handle greater venous return, and engage hormonal responses that limit volume overload. Whether HR rises in an intact person depends on simultaneous baroreflex activity, making simplistic 'atrial stretch always causes tachycardia' unreliable.

## Raised intracranial pressure: connect both reflexes
When increased intracranial pressure compromises cerebral perfusion, medullary ischaemia may drive intense sympathetic vasoconstriction and **hypertension**. Elevated arterial pressure then triggers **baroreflex bradycardia**; respiratory irregularity may emerge from brainstem dysfunction. This is the **Cushing response/triad**. It does *not* mean raised intracranial pressure always creates high BP/low HR in every case; the triad is an ominous late pattern.

**Practice:** Why can BP rise while HR falls? Two linked reflex processes operate: threatened brain perfusion drives sympathetic BP elevation, and arterial baroreceptors then favour bradycardia.
