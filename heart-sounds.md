# Heart Sounds and Murmurs

## Sounds reflect vibration around valve events
**S₁** is associated chiefly with closure of mitral and tricuspid valves as ventricular systole begins. **S₂** arises chiefly from aortic and pulmonary valve closure near its end. The sounds come from vibration of cardiac structures and blood associated with these events—not simply leaflets slapping together.

An **S₃** occurs during rapid early diastolic filling; it may be physiological in younger people, but in an older symptomatic patient can reflect volume overload or ventricular dysfunction. An **S₄** is associated with atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle; it is absent in atrial fibrillation.

## How to reason through a murmur
A murmur reflects audible turbulent or otherwise disturbed flow. Ask **which valve**, **whether flow is forward or backward**, and **whether that flow happens during systole or diastole**.

| Lesion | Flow producing sound | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Aortic stenosis | Ejection through narrowed aortic valve | Systolic |
| Pulmonary stenosis | Ejection through narrowed pulmonary valve | Systolic |
| Mitral regurgitation | LV to LA when LV contracts | Systolic |
| Tricuspid regurgitation | RV to RA during systole | Systolic |
| Aortic regurgitation | Aorta back into LV after systole | Diastolic |
| Mitral stenosis | LA to LV through narrowed valve during filling | Diastolic |

Not every murmur means structural valve disease: high-flow states such as anaemia may produce functional murmurs. **Bruits** are analogous vascular sounds produced by disturbed blood flow.

## Inspiration and the second sound
Inspiration increases systemic venous return to the right ventricle and can delay P₂. This explains normal inspiratory widening of the aortic–pulmonary components of S₂.

**Check:** Why can mitral regurgitation produce a systolic murmur, but mitral stenosis a diastolic one?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>During systole the LV normally contracts with the mitral valve closed, so incompetence permits backward flow. During diastole blood normally flows from LA into LV, so a narrowed mitral valve obstructs forward filling then.</details>

## Four heart sounds and the physiological split
**S1** chiefly follows mitral and tricuspid closure as ventricular contraction begins. **S2** chiefly follows aortic and pulmonary valve closure as ejection ends. **S3** occurs in early rapid filling and can be physiological in young people; in older adults it may suggest volume overload or heart failure. **S4** is associated with atrial contraction into a relatively stiff ventricle and is generally pathological in adults; it cannot occur without effective atrial contraction.

During inspiration, increased systemic venous return tends to prolong right ventricular ejection, while changes in pulmonary venous return can advance left-sided closure. Therefore **A2 precedes P2 by a larger interval**: physiological splitting of S2. The two components are not guaranteed to close simultaneously during expiration.

## Predict murmur timing from pressure gradients
A stenotic **aortic or pulmonary valve** obstructs forward ejection during **systole**, producing a systolic ejection murmur. A regurgitant **mitral or tricuspid valve** leaks backward during ventricular **systole**, producing a systolic regurgitant murmur. A stenotic **mitral or tricuspid valve** obstructs ventricular filling during **diastole**; a regurgitant **aortic or pulmonary valve** leaks back into the ventricle during **diastole**. Murmur intensity alone does not consistently measure lesion severity.

Standard auscultation areas: **aortic** second right intercostal space parasternal; **pulmonary** second left intercostal space; **tricuspid** left lower sternal border; **mitral** apex around fifth left intercostal space near the midclavicular line. These are optimal listening areas, **not the anatomical positions of the valves**.

## Why bruits arise beyond heart valves
Turbulent blood flow can generate vascular sounds over stenotic arteries, arteriovenous fistulas and aneurysms. Higher flow or lower viscosity (e.g. anaemia) can increase turbulence even without valve damage. A patent ductus arteriosus may produce a continuous murmur because aortic pressure exceeds pulmonary arterial pressure throughout much of the cardiac cycle.

**Practice:** Which valve lesion can cause a diastolic murmur with backward blood flow into the left ventricle? **Aortic regurgitation**, because the closed aortic valve fails to prevent aortic-to-LV flow during diastole.
