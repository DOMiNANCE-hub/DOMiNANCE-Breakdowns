# Heart Sounds and Murmurs

## 1. Why does the heart produce sounds?

During the cardiac cycle, pressure changes make heart valves close, and blood accelerates or decelerates inside the heart. Sudden changes in blood motion set the valve apparatus, surrounding tissues and blood into **vibration**. Some of these vibrations travel through the chest wall and can be heard with a stethoscope.

Heart sounds are therefore **not simply leaflets hitting one another**. The two principal sounds normally heard are **S1** and **S2**.

| Sound | Main valve event | When it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| **S1 — “lub”** | Mitral and tricuspid closure | Beginning of ventricular systole |
| **S2 — “dub”** | Aortic and pulmonary closure | Beginning of ventricular diastole |

## 2. Why does S1 occur before S2?

### S1: the start of ventricular systole

Ventricular depolarisation (QRS) is followed by myocardial contraction, raising ventricular pressure. Once ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure, the **AV valves close**. Their closure and the associated sudden changes in blood motion produce **S1**, around the onset of <span data-term="isovolumetric">isovolumetric contraction</span>.

### S2: the end of ventricular ejection

Later, ventricular force and pressure decline. When aortic and pulmonary arterial pressures exceed the corresponding ventricular pressures, the **semilunar valves close**. This produces **S2**, around the beginning of isovolumetric relaxation.

The important pressure logic is: **S1 follows reversal of the atrial-to-ventricular pressure gradient; S2 follows reversal of the ventricular-to-arterial pressure gradient.** Closure generates audible vibrations; a valve does not make a sound simply because it is open.

Illustrative sound durations are approximately **0.15 s for S1** and **0.12 s for S2**, but duration and intensity vary.

## 3. Why does S2 sometimes split with inspiration?

S2 has two components: **A2** (aortic valve closure) and **P2** (pulmonary valve closure). Usually **A2 precedes P2** by a short interval.

During inspiration, more negative intrathoracic pressure promotes **systemic venous return to the right heart**. Right ventricular filling rises, and right ventricular ejection may last slightly longer. Because the right ventricle takes slightly longer to finish ejecting, **P2 occurs later relative to A2**. The gap between the two closing sounds widens, so a single-sounding S2 may become audibly split. Respiratory changes in pulmonary venous return and left ventricular timing can contribute as well.

<div class="sound-split" role="group" aria-label="Aortic and pulmonary valve closure timing"><div><strong>Expiration</strong><span>A2 <b>·</b> P2</span><small>Short separation; may sound single</small></div><div><strong>Inspiration</strong><span>A2 <b>····</b> P2</span><small>Longer separation: physiological splitting</small></div></div>

**The important point is widening of the A2–P2 interval during inspiration**, not that the two valves necessarily close at precisely the same time during expiration. More complex patterns of splitting are advanced clinical material beyond this lecturer's primary scope.

## 4. What causes S3 and S4?

Both are additional low-frequency sounds in **diastole**, but they correspond to *different phases of filling*.

### S3 — Early diastole: rapid ventricular filling

**S3 occurs shortly after S2**, when blood enters the ventricle rapidly. Rapid filling and sudden deceleration of blood can set ventricular structures vibrating. S3 can be physiological in children and younger adults, and during pregnancy. In an older or symptomatic adult, it can indicate volume overload or ventricular dysfunction, including heart failure.

### S4 — Late diastole: atrial contraction against resistance

**S4 occurs shortly before S1**, during atrial contraction. If the ventricle is relatively stiff and noncompliant, the additional blood delivered by atrial systole encounters greater resistance to filling, generating low-frequency vibrations.

S4 is usually abnormal in adults. A typical atrial S4 is **absent in atrial fibrillation** because there is no effective coordinated atrial contraction to produce it.

| Feature | **S3** | **S4** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Early diastole, just after S2 | Late diastole, just before S1 |
| Trigger | Rapid ventricular filling | Atrial contraction |
| Interpretation | Sometimes normal in younger people; may indicate overload/dysfunction in older adults | Often suggests a stiff ventricle in adults |
| Needs atrial contraction? | No | Yes |

**Audio interpretation:** A new low-pitched sound just after S2 suggests **S3**; one just before S1 suggests **S4**. Our normal recording above demonstrates S1/S2, **not** a verified S3 or S4 example. Separate recordings should be added only after checking their identity and rights.

## 5. Why do heart sounds change in intensity?

Sound intensity depends on valve motion, pressure changes, the rate at which blood flow is stopped, and how well the vibrations travel to the stethoscope.

For example, **A2 may be accentuated in systemic hypertension**, and **P2 in pulmonary hypertension**, because higher arterial pressure can produce more energetic closing events. However, severe valve immobility may make a closure sound softer despite disease.

Thus, **a loud or soft sound alone cannot reliably establish disease severity**.

## 6. What makes a murmur?

A **heart murmur** is a relatively sustained sound produced when blood flow and cardiac structures generate audible vibrations, often in association with disturbed or turbulent flow.

Examples of conditions that change the flow include:

- **Stenosis:** A valve narrows, so blood has difficulty passing *forward* through it.
- **Regurgitation (incompetence):** A valve fails to close properly and allows blood to leak *backward*.
- **High-flow states:** For example, anaemia can increase flow or reduce blood viscosity enough to produce a murmur despite structurally normal valves.

To predict murmur timing, ask three questions: **(1)** Is the ventricle contracting (systole) or filling (diastole)? **(2)** Should the affected valve normally be open or closed then? **(3)** Is blood being forced through a narrowed opening or leaking backward through a valve that should be shut? The answers explain the timing without memorising four disconnected lists.

## 7. Predict a murmur's timing without memorising a list

Remember the normal valve pattern:

| During | AV valves (mitral/tricuspid) | Semilunar valves (aortic/pulmonary) |
|---|---|---|
| **Ventricular systole** | Should be **closed** | Should be **open** for ejection |
| **Ventricular diastole** | Should be **open** for filling | Should be **closed** |

### Systolic murmurs

**Aortic or pulmonary stenosis:** These valves should be open during ventricular ejection. A narrowed opening obstructs **forward systolic flow**, so turbulent high-velocity flow can produce a systolic ejection murmur.

**Mitral or tricuspid regurgitation:** These AV valves should be closed during ventricular contraction. If they leak, blood moves **backward from ventricle to atrium during systole**, creating a systolic regurgitant murmur.

### Diastolic murmurs

**Mitral or tricuspid stenosis:** AV valves should open during ventricular filling. A narrowed opening obstructs **forward flow from atrium to ventricle during diastole**, causing a diastolic murmur.

**Aortic or pulmonary regurgitation:** Semilunar valves should remain closed during diastole. If they leak, arterial pressure drives **backward flow into the ventricle during diastole**, causing a diastolic murmur.

### Murmur timing at a glance

| Valve group | Stenosis | Regurgitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Aortic / pulmonary** | **Systolic** (forward outflow obstruction) | **Diastolic** (backflow into ventricle) |
| **Mitral / tricuspid** | **Diastolic** (forward filling obstruction) | **Systolic** (backflow into atrium) |

To solve a new question, ask **which valve should be open or shut during the abnormal flow?** This explains the timing. Murmur loudness alone is not a dependable measure of lesion severity; location, character, radiation and other signs also matter.

## 8. Where do we listen to each heart valve?

The best listening points on the chest are **not necessarily directly over the anatomical valve openings**. Vibrations are transmitted through blood and cardiac tissues.

| Auscultation area | Surface landmark |
|---|---|
| **Aortic** | Second **right** intercostal space, near sternum |
| **Pulmonary** | Second **left** intercostal space, near sternum |
| **Tricuspid** | Lower **left** sternal border |
| **Mitral** | **Apex**, commonly fifth left intercostal space near midclavicular line |

**Orientation check:** These are locations on the **patient's** chest, not the observer's. When facing a patient, the patient's left is on your right. The tricuspid area is at the **patient's lower left sternal border** (commonly around the fourth or fifth intercostal space). Do not confuse the valve's anatomical position with its best listening point.

A stethoscope's **diaphragm** transmits many higher-frequency sounds well (e.g. S1, S2 and many murmurs). Its **bell**, applied lightly, can help detect low-frequency S3, S4 and some diastolic murmurs. This is a supplementary clinical technique.

## 9. Why can bruits or murmurs arise elsewhere?

A **<span data-term="bruit">bruit</span>** is an abnormal vascular sound heard over a vessel when flow generates audible vibrations.

The lecture also names conditions that can produce abnormal cardiovascular sounds:

| Condition | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)** | Aorta-to-pulmonary artery flow may continue during both systole and diastole, producing a *continuous* murmur. |
| **Ventricular septal defect (VSD)** | Abnormal flow between ventricles commonly produces a systolic murmur. |
| **Arteriovenous fistula** | High-velocity artery-to-vein flow can create a bruit. |
| **Anaemia** | Reduced viscosity and increased flow can cause a functional murmur without valve stenosis. |
| **Vascular aneurysm** | Altered vessel geometry may disturb flow; not every aneurysm produces a bruit. |
| **Hypervascular goitre** | Increased thyroid blood flow can cause a bruit over the gland. |

The common mechanism is **flow-induced vibration**, but its timing depends on the relevant pressure gradient and anatomy.

## Understanding Check

**1. A sound is heard at the beginning of ventricular systole. Which valves are mainly responsible, and what pressure change closes them?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>S1, mainly from mitral and tricuspid valve closure.</strong> Early ventricular contraction raises ventricular pressure above atrial pressure, reversing the gradient across the AV valves. Their closure abruptly alters blood motion and generates vibrations heard as S1.</p></details>

**2. Why does physiological splitting of S2 increase with inspiration?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Inspiration increases venous return to the right heart. Greater right ventricular filling can prolong ejection and <strong>delay pulmonary valve closure (P2)</strong> relative to aortic closure (A2). The A2–P2 interval widens. Expiration usually narrows the separation; the valves are not required to close at exactly the same instant.</p></details>

**3. An extra sound occurs shortly after S2 during rapid ventricular filling. Identify it and explain when it may be physiological.**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>S3.</strong> Rapid early-diastolic filling and deceleration produce vibrations in ventricular structures. An S3 may be normal in children or young adults, but in an older symptomatic patient it can suggest ventricular overload or dysfunction. Timing and clinical context are essential.</p></details>

**4. A low-frequency sound just before S1 disappears when a patient develops atrial fibrillation. Explain the likely sound and why it disappears.**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>S4.</strong> It occurs in late diastole when effective atrial contraction forces blood into a relatively stiff ventricle, producing vibrations. In atrial fibrillation, coordinated atrial contraction is lost; without the atrial kick, the usual mechanism producing an atrial S4 is absent.</p></details>

**5. Why can mitral stenosis cause a diastolic murmur but mitral regurgitation cause a systolic murmur?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>Stenosis obstructs normal forward filling:</strong> the mitral valve should be open in diastole for left atrium-to-ventricle flow, so narrowing generates a diastolic murmur. <strong>Regurgitation permits backward flow:</strong> the valve should be closed during ventricular systole, but leakage sends blood into the left atrium and produces a systolic murmur.</p></details>

**6. A valve leaks blood from the aorta back into the left ventricle. Name the lesion and predict the murmur's timing.**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>Aortic regurgitation; diastolic murmur.</strong> During ventricular relaxation, aortic pressure is greater than left ventricular pressure and the aortic valve should be closed. If it is incompetent, blood leaks backward into the ventricle during diastole and generates an audible murmur.</p></details>

**7. Why might an anaemic patient have a systolic flow murmur even when all four valves are structurally normal?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Anaemia reduces blood viscosity and often increases cardiac output. Faster flow through otherwise normal valves may generate audible flow-related vibrations, often as an ejection-type systolic murmur. A murmur therefore does not automatically mean a damaged or narrowed valve.</p></details>

**8. A stethoscope is placed over the second right intercostal space. Which valve area is being examined, and why is this not necessarily the valve's anatomical position?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>The aortic auscultation area.</strong> Valve vibrations travel with blood and through surrounding tissues, so the point at which they are best transmitted to the chest wall is not necessarily directly over the anatomical aortic valve.</p></details>

**9. Listen to the normal heart recording. Which interval corresponds to ventricular systole, and why?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>The S1-to-S2 interval is ventricular systole.</strong> S1 follows AV valve closure as ventricular pressure rises; S2 follows semilunar valve closure when ventricular ejection ends. The S2-to-next-S1 interval is ventricular diastole. The recording illustrates normal S1 and S2, not a proven S3 or S4.</p></details>

**10. A continuous murmur is heard in a patient with a patent ductus arteriosus. Why does the abnormal flow persist across both parts of the cardiac cycle?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>A patent ductus joins the aorta to the pulmonary artery. Because aortic pressure is typically greater than pulmonary arterial pressure during both systole and diastole, blood may continue to cross the connection throughout the cycle. Thus the sound can be <strong>continuous</strong>, unlike a murmur confined to one phase.</p></details>


### Additional application and mechanism checks

**Supplementary 1. How would increased aortic pressure tend to affect the timing of aortic valve opening?**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Aortic valve opening occurs only when left ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure. If aortic diastolic pressure rises and the rate of ventricular pressure development stays similar, it takes longer to reach the opening threshold, so isovolumetric contraction may lengthen. Changes in contractility can modify this response.</p></details>

**Supplementary 2. Why can't a loud A2 alone tell you how severe aortic valve disease is?**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>A2 intensity depends on the pressure difference, how freely the valve moves and how vibrations reach the chest wall. Higher aortic pressure may accentuate A2, but a severely immobile valve may produce a soft sound. **Loudness alone does not measure lesion severity**; other findings and investigations are needed.</p></details>

## Chapter Essentials

1. **S1:** Mostly due to mitral and tricuspid closure at the start of ventricular systole.
2. **S2:** Mostly due to aortic and pulmonary closure at the start of ventricular diastole.
3. **S2 splitting:** Inspiration normally widens the A2–P2 interval, largely through relative delay of P2.
4. **S3:** Early-diastolic rapid-filling sound; may be physiological in younger people.
5. **S4:** Late-diastolic sound involving atrial contraction into a relatively stiff ventricle; typically absent in atrial fibrillation.
6. **Murmurs:** Audible flow-induced vibrations; assess timing, direction and affected valve.
7. **Semilunar stenosis / AV regurgitation:** Usually **systolic** murmurs.
8. **AV stenosis / semilunar regurgitation:** Usually **diastolic** murmurs.
9. **Auscultation:** Aortic—second right; pulmonary—second left; tricuspid—lower left sternal border; mitral—apex.
10. **Auscultation areas are listening points**, not the exact anatomical valve locations.
11. **Bruits and nonvalvular murmurs:** Can accompany abnormal vascular connections, turbulent flow or high-flow states.
12. **Continuous PDA murmur:** Flow can persist because the aorta-to-pulmonary artery pressure difference remains present through much of the cardiac cycle.
