# Heart Sounds and Murmurs

## 1. Why does the heart produce sounds?

With each beat, a healthy heart makes two main sounds, heard through a stethoscope as **"lub-dub"**. Each one is made by a pair of valves shutting.

| Sound | Valves that shut | When it happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **<span data-term="s1">S1</span>, "lub"** | **Mitral** and **tricuspid**, the inlet valves between the atria and the ventricles | As the ventricles start to contract |
| **<span data-term="s2">S2</span>, "dub"** | **Aortic** and **pulmonary**, the outlet valves into the aorta and the pulmonary artery | As the ventricles stop ejecting and start to relax |

**Why does a shutting valve make a sound?** The lecture puts it simply: the sounds are made by the valves closing. But what you hear is not the leaflets clapping together. When a valve snaps shut, the blood moving against it stops suddenly. That sudden stop makes the blood, the tightened valve leaflets and the nearby heart and artery walls vibrate. It's the same reason a water pipe bangs when a tap is turned off quickly. The vibrations travel through the chest wall to the stethoscope.

**Why is there no sound when a valve opens?** A normal valve opens gently as pressure builds behind it, and blood simply starts to flow smoothly. Nothing is stopped suddenly, so there is nothing to hear.

**What the sounds tell you about timing.** S1 marks the start of ventricular contraction, and S2 marks its end. So:

- **S1 → S2** is **<span data-term="systole-diastole">ventricular systole</span>**: the ventricles contract and eject.
- **S2 → next S1** is **ventricular diastole**: the ventricles relax and fill.

At a resting heart rate, diastole is the longer phase. So the silence after "dub" is longer than the gap between "lub" and "dub". That is how you tell S1 from S2 when listening. At fast heart rates the two gaps become similar, so clinicians feel the carotid pulse while listening: the pulse comes just after S1. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cardiac-cycle?section=1-one-heartbeat-what-the-atria-and-ventricles-are-doing">→ Cardiac Cycle, §1</a>)*

## 2. Why does S1 occur before S2?

Both sounds follow the valve rule from Chapter 4. A valve **opens** when the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure in front of it. It **shuts** the moment that difference reverses. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cardiac-cycle?section=2-the-one-rule-every-valve-follows">→ Cardiac Cycle, §2</a>)* Follow one beat. The left and right sides do the same thing at the same time; the right side just works at lower pressures.

**S1: the inlet valves shut as contraction begins.** The electrical signal spreads through the ventricles (the <span data-term="qrs">QRS complex</span> on the ECG), and the ventricles start to contract. Within moments, pressure in each ventricle rises above the pressure in its atrium. Blood starts to push back towards the atria, and that push snaps the **mitral** and **tricuspid** valves shut: **S1**. The outlet valves are still shut as well, so for a moment the ventricles squeeze with every valve closed. This is <span data-term="isovolumetric">isovolumetric contraction</span>. S1 therefore marks the **start of ventricular systole**. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cardiac-cycle?section=4-isovolumetric-contraction-contracting-but-no-blood-leaves">→ Cardiac Cycle, §4</a>)*

**S2: the outlet valves shut as ejection ends.** The ventricles keep ejecting until their force fades. Their pressure then falls below the pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery. Blood briefly starts to flow back towards the ventricles, and this snaps the **aortic** and **pulmonary** valves shut: **S2**. The ventricles now relax with every valve closed again. This is isovolumetric relaxation. As the lecture puts it, S2 marks the **end of ventricular systole, which is the beginning of diastole**. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cardiac-cycle?section=6-closing-the-outflow-valves-and-isovolumetric-relaxation">→ Cardiac Cycle, §6</a>)*

In one line: **S1 is when the ventricle overtakes the atrium; S2 is when the artery overtakes the ventricle.**

**How long are they, and why do they sound different?** The lecture gives about **0.15 s for S1** and **0.12 s for S2**. S1 is the longer, lower "lub". S2 is the shorter, sharper "dub": the aortic and pulmonary valves, and the stretched artery walls behind them, are tighter, so they vibrate at a higher pitch.

**Do both valves in a pair shut at exactly the same moment?** Almost, but not quite. The lecture says they close at about the same time. For S1 the gap is too small to hear, so S1 sounds single. For S2 the gap can widen enough to hear as two parts. That is S2 splitting, the subject of the next section.

## 3. Why does S2 sometimes split with inspiration?

**Why S2 has two parts.** S2 is made of **A2** (aortic closure) and **P2** (pulmonary closure). A2 normally comes first. During **expiration** they are close together and often sound single. During **inspiration**, P2 moves later and the gap widens: **<span data-term="s2-splitting">physiological splitting of S2</span>**. Two effects delay P2:

1. **More right-heart filling.** Inspiration makes chest pressure more negative, drawing more venous blood into the right heart. The right ventricle fills more and takes longer to eject.
2. **Lower pulmonary impedance.** The expanding lungs lower the <span data-term="pulmonary-impedance">impedance</span> (opposition to flow) of the pulmonary vessels. Blood keeps flowing forward into the pulmonary artery for longer after right ventricular pressure peaks, so the pulmonary valve closes later.

A2 may also move slightly earlier. The expanded lung vessels hold more blood for a few beats, so left ventricular filling dips briefly.

These two effects are what the lecture means by "venous return" and "reduces pulmonary resistance… thus longer time for blood flow".

**Why "physiological"?** Because it is normal. It happens in healthy people with every breath in, and it fades with every breath out. What matters is the **change with breathing**: the gap widens on inspiration and narrows on expiration. A split that stays wide during expiration as well is not normal. That is clinical material beyond this lecture.

**Where and how to hear it.** P2 is a soft sound, so listen at the **pulmonary area**: the second left intercostal space, beside the sternum (see <a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-sounds?section=8-where-do-we-listen-to-each-heart-valve">§8</a>). Ask the person to breathe slowly. On a breath in, the single "dub" becomes a quick "da-dub". On a breath out, it merges back into one.

## 4. What causes S3 and S4?

S1 and S2 are valves shutting. S3 and S4 are different: no valve shuts. They are **filling sounds**, made in diastole as blood rushes into the ventricle and is then suddenly slowed. The lecture says the same: both are due to ventricular filling. What separates them is **what drives the rush**, and that puts them at opposite ends of diastole.

Recall how a ventricle fills *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cardiac-cycle?section=3-filling-how-blood-enters-the-relaxed-ventricle">→ Cardiac Cycle, §3</a>)*:

- **Rapid filling.** The mitral and tricuspid valves open, and blood stored in the atria pours into the ventricles.
- **<span data-term="diastasis">Diastasis</span>.** Filling slows to a trickle.
- **<span data-term="atrial-kick">Atrial kick</span>.** The atria contract and push in the last portion of blood.

**<span data-term="s3">S3</span>: at the end of rapid filling, early in diastole.** About 0.1–0.2 s after S2, the rush of early filling reaches the limit of how far the ventricle wall will easily stretch. The inflowing blood is suddenly slowed, and the ventricle wall and blood vibrate. The result is a low, dull sound just after S2: **"lub-dub-dum"**. Many students remember the rhythm as *Ken-TUC-ky*.

- **When it is normal:** in children, young adults, athletes and pregnant women. Their ventricles relax quickly and fill very fast.
- **When it suggests a problem:** with age, early filling normally slows and S3 disappears. In an older adult, an S3 usually means the ventricle is being overfilled or is enlarged and failing. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-failure">→ Heart Failure</a>)*

**<span data-term="s4">S4</span>: during the atrial kick, late in diastole.** Just before S1, the atria contract and push their blood into the ventricles. If the ventricle is **stiff**, for example thickened, it resists this final push. The blood is stopped abruptly and the wall vibrates. The result is a low sound just before S1: **"da-lub-dub"**, or *TEN-nes-see*.

- **It depends entirely on atrial contraction.** It comes just after the <span data-term="p-wave">P wave</span> and goes with the <span data-term="a-wave">a wave</span> in the atria. So it is **absent in atrial fibrillation**, where the atria quiver instead of contracting together.
- **When it suggests a problem:** an S4 you can actually hear with a stethoscope in an adult usually means a stiff ventricle.

| | **S3** | **S4** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When | Early diastole, just after S2 | Late diastole, just before S1 |
| What drives the rush | Passive rapid filling | Atrial contraction (the atrial kick) |
| Rhythm | lub-dub-**dum** (*Ken-TUC-ky*) | **da**-lub-dub (*TEN-nes-see*) |
| Heard in healthy people? | Often, in the young | Rarely heard with a stethoscope |
| In an older adult it usually means | An overfilled or failing ventricle | A stiff ventricle |
| Needs atrial contraction? | No | Yes, so it is lost in atrial fibrillation |

**How to listen.** Both are very low-pitched. Use the **bell** of the stethoscope, pressed lightly, at the **apex**, with the person turned onto their **left side**. This brings the apex closer to the chest wall.

## 5. Why do heart sounds change in loudness?

Each sound is made by a valve snapping shut. So anything that makes the snap **harder** makes the sound **louder**. For S1 and S2, two different things control how hard the snap is.

**S1 depends on how wide open the mitral and tricuspid valves are when contraction starts.** The lecture links S1's loudness to how the ventricle fills. Think of a door: one that is already nearly shut closes quietly, but one flung shut from wide open bangs.

- **Slow heart rate → soft S1.** Diastole is long, so the ventricles finish filling well before they contract. As the inflow slows, the cusps of the mitral and tricuspid valves drift up towards each other. The lecture calls these "floating valves". When the ventricles finally contract, the cusps only have a short way to go, so they close gently.
- **Fast heart rate → loud S1.** Diastole is short, so the ventricles contract while blood is still rushing in and the cusps are still wide apart. Rising ventricular pressure slams them shut from fully open.
- **A stronger contraction also gives a louder S1**, for example during exercise. Ventricular pressure rises faster, so the cusps are driven shut harder.

<figure class="learning-figure teaching-widget" data-widget="s1-loudness"><figcaption>Why a slow heart has a soft S1</figcaption><p>Two panels of the mitral valve at the instant the ventricle starts to contract. Slow heart: the cusps are already close together and travel a short distance (soft S1). Fast heart: the cusps are still wide open and travel a long distance (loud S1). Play animates both closing at the same moment, with a quiet and a loud lub. Schematic.</p></figure>

**S2 depends on the pressure in the artery behind the valve.** The aortic and pulmonary valves are pushed shut by the pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery. The higher that pressure, the harder and quicker the valve is driven shut. This is the lecture's "brisk closure".

- **High blood pressure in the aorta** (systemic hypertension) → loud **A2**.
- **High pressure in the pulmonary artery** (pulmonary hypertension) → loud **P2**. It is heard best at the pulmonary area, the second left intercostal space.

**What a stethoscope picks up also depends on the route to the chest wall.** Sounds are quieter through a thick chest wall, fluid around the heart, or over-inflated lungs. A badly damaged, stiff valve may also barely move, so its closing sound becomes softer even though the disease is severe. So **loudness alone never tells you how serious a problem is**. It is one clue to put alongside timing and where the sound is heard.

## 6. What makes a murmur?

**Normal blood flow is silent.** Blood normally moves in smooth layers, sliding past one another. This is **<span data-term="laminar-flow">laminar flow</span>**, the lecture's "streamlined" flow. Nothing tumbles or collides, so there is nothing to hear.

A **<span data-term="murmur">murmur</span>** is the sound of blood flow that has become **<span data-term="turbulent-flow">turbulent</span>**: swirling and tumbling instead of sliding in layers. The swirls shake the heart and vessel walls for as long as the turbulence lasts. So a murmur is a longer **whooshing** sound, not a short thud like S1 or S2.

**What makes flow turbulent?** Flow tips into turbulence when it is **fast**, when the blood is **thin** (low viscosity), or when it is forced through a **sudden narrowing**. The lecture sums this up with the Reynolds number: the higher it is, the more likely turbulence becomes. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/hemodynamics?section=5-laminar-flow-turbulence-and-reynolds-number">→ Haemodynamics, §5</a>)*

**The heart can make flow turbulent in three ways.** These follow the lecture's list.

1. **A narrowed valve (<span data-term="stenosis">stenosis</span>).** When the valve should be open, it opens only partly. Blood is squeezed through the small opening as a fast jet, which becomes turbulent beyond the valve. This murmur is made by **forward** flow.
2. **A leaking valve (<span data-term="regurgitation">regurgitation</span>, the lecture's "incompetence").** When the valve should be shut, it does not close fully. Blood is driven **backwards** through the gap as a fast jet. This is the lecture's "backflow".
3. **Fast flow through normal valves (a "hyperdynamic" circulation).** The valves are healthy, but blood moves fast enough, or is thin enough, to become turbulent anyway. The lecture's example is **anaemia**, where thin blood and a raised cardiac output combine. Exercise, fever and pregnancy can do the same. These **<span data-term="flow-murmur">flow murmurs</span>** do not mean the valves are damaged.

<figure class="learning-figure teaching-widget" data-widget="murmur-figure"><figcaption>Three ways to make a murmur</figcaption><p>Four panels. Normal: a fully open valve with smooth, parallel arrows (laminar: silent). Stenosis: a narrowed opening with a fast jet that breaks into swirls beyond it (forward jet). Regurgitation: a valve that should be shut, with a thin jet leaking backwards into swirls (backward jet). High flow: a normal open valve with many long, fast arrows becoming swirls (normal valve, fast flow). In every murmur there is turbulence somewhere; the questions are where it is and which way the blood is going.</p></figure>

**The key question for any murmur:** which way is the abnormal blood flowing, and is that happening in systole or diastole? That decides when you hear it, which is the subject of <a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-sounds?section=7-predict-a-murmur-s-timing-without-memorising-a-list">§7</a>.

**Murmur or bruit?** Both come from the same mechanism. A murmur is heard over the heart; a bruit is heard over a blood vessel (<a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-sounds?section=9-why-can-bruits-or-murmurs-arise-elsewhere">§9</a>).

## 7. Predict a murmur's timing without memorising a list

The lecture's tip is to **group the valves that close together**. The aortic and pulmonary valves behave as one pair, and the mitral and tricuspid valves as the other. Then you only need to know what each pair should be doing in each phase:

| During | Mitral and tricuspid (inlet valves) | Aortic and pulmonary (outlet valves) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ventricular systole** (S1 → S2) | Should be **shut** | Should be **open**, for ejection |
| **Ventricular diastole** (S2 → S1) | Should be **open**, for filling | Should be **shut** |

For any faulty valve, ask one question: **when is the faulty valve supposed to be open (for stenosis) or shut (for a leak)?** That phase is when the abnormal flow happens, so that is when you hear the murmur.

**Systolic murmurs (between S1 and S2)**

- **Aortic or pulmonary stenosis.** These valves should be open while the ventricles eject. A narrowed opening squeezes forward flow into a fast jet, so the murmur is **systolic**.
- **Mitral or tricuspid regurgitation.** These valves should be shut while the ventricles contract. If they leak, the contracting ventricle drives blood back into the atrium. The leak starts as soon as contraction starts and lasts until relaxation, so the murmur can fill **all of systole**.

**Diastolic murmurs (between S2 and the next S1)**

- **Mitral or tricuspid stenosis.** These valves should be open while the ventricles fill. A narrowed opening obstructs filling, so the murmur is **diastolic**.
- **Aortic or pulmonary regurgitation.** These valves should be shut while the ventricles relax. The high pressure in the artery drives blood back into the ventricle, starting **right after S2**, so the murmur is **diastolic**.

**At a glance**

| Valve pair | Stenosis (forward jet) | Regurgitation (backward jet) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aortic / pulmonary** | **Systolic** | **Diastolic** |
| **Mitral / tricuspid** | **Diastolic** | **Systolic** |

The pattern is a cross: each lesion falls in the opposite phase to its partner. You never need to memorise it. If you forget, ask the question again.

## 8. Where do we listen to each heart valve?

The lecture says each valve is best heard over its **surface marking**: the spot on the chest wall where that valve's sound comes through most clearly. These four spots are the **<span data-term="auscultation-areas">auscultation areas</span>**:

| Area | Where on the chest |
| --- | --- |
| **Aortic** | Second **right** intercostal space, beside the sternum |
| **Pulmonary** | Second **left** intercostal space, beside the sternum |
| **Tricuspid** | Lower **left** border of the sternum (fourth or fifth intercostal space) |
| **Mitral** | The **apex**: fifth left intercostal space, in line with the middle of the clavicle |

<!-- OPENSTAX-FIGURE -->

**Why aren't these spots directly over the valves?** The four valves sit close together behind the sternum, packed into a small area. Each valve's sound travels **in the direction its blood flows**, and comes out where that chamber or vessel lies closest to the chest wall:

- The aortic valve sits behind the left side of the sternum. Its sound is carried up the aorta, which comes nearest the surface at the **right** second intercostal space.
- The mitral valve also sits behind the sternum. Its sound is carried down through the left ventricle to the **apex**, where the ventricle touches the chest wall.

**A practical tip for telling S1 from S2:**

- **S1 is loudest at the apex**, where the mitral sound comes out.
- **S2 is loudest at the base**, the aortic and pulmonary areas at the top of the heart.

Moving the stethoscope between the two and hearing which sound gets louder tells you which is which.

<div class="teaching-widget sound-clips" data-widget="sound-clips" data-clips="normal-apex,normal-base"><p>Two recordings of the same normal heart sounds: at the apex, where S1 is louder, and at the base, where S2 is louder.</p></div>

**Bell or diaphragm?** The **diaphragm**, pressed firmly, picks up higher-pitched sounds: S1, S2 and most murmurs. The **bell**, resting lightly, picks up low-pitched sounds such as S3, S4 and the rumble of mitral stenosis.

## 9. Why can bruits or murmurs arise elsewhere?

Turbulence is not limited to the heart valves. Wherever blood is forced through a narrowing, takes a short cut, or flows unusually fast, it can become turbulent and make a sound (<a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-sounds?section=6-what-makes-a-murmur">§6</a>). Heard over a vessel, this sound is called a **<span data-term="bruit">bruit</span>**. The lecture lists these examples:

| Condition | Why the flow becomes turbulent | When it is heard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)**: the fetal channel between the aorta and pulmonary artery stays open after birth | Aortic pressure is higher than pulmonary pressure in **both** systole and diastole, so blood crosses all the time | **Continuous**, through S2 |
| **Ventricular septal defect (VSD)**: a hole between the ventricles | When the ventricles contract, left ventricular pressure far exceeds right, so blood jets through the hole | **Systolic** |
| **Arteriovenous (AV) fistula**: a direct channel from an artery to a vein | High-pressure arterial blood rushes into a low-pressure vein, skipping the capillaries | Continuous bruit over the site |
| **Aneurysm**: a ballooned section of artery | The sudden widening disturbs smooth flow (not every aneurysm makes a sound) | A bruit over the aneurysm |
| **Goitre** (enlarged thyroid) with high blood flow | Increased blood flow through the gland | A bruit over the thyroid |
| **Anaemia** | Thin, fast blood (see §6) | A soft systolic flow murmur |

The rule is the same everywhere: **find the pressure difference that drives the abnormal flow, and when it exists.** That tells you when the sound is heard. The PDA murmur is continuous for exactly this reason: the pressure difference between the aorta and pulmonary artery never reverses.

## 11. Chapter Essentials

1. **S1 ("lub")**: the mitral and tricuspid valves shutting as the ventricles start to contract. It marks the start of ventricular systole.
2. **S2 ("dub")**: the aortic and pulmonary valves shutting as ejection ends. It marks the start of ventricular diastole.
3. **The sound is not the leaflets clapping**: it is the vibration caused when moving blood is suddenly stopped. Normal valves open silently.
4. **S1 → S2 is systole; S2 → S1 is diastole.** At rest the pause after S2 is longer. S1 is loudest at the apex, S2 at the base.
5. **Physiological splitting of S2**: on inspiration, more right-heart filling and lower pulmonary impedance delay P2, so A2 and P2 separate. They merge again on expiration.
6. **S3**: early diastole, at the end of rapid filling. Often normal in the young; in older adults it suggests an overfilled or failing ventricle.
7. **S4**: late diastole, from the atrial kick into a stiff ventricle. It needs atrial contraction, so it is absent in atrial fibrillation.
8. **Loudness**: a slow heart gives a soft S1 (the cusps have floated nearly shut). High aortic or pulmonary pressure gives a loud A2 or P2. Loudness alone does not measure severity.
9. **Murmurs are turbulent flow**: from a narrowed valve (forward jet), a leaking valve (backward jet), or fast, thin blood through normal valves (flow murmur, e.g. anaemia).
10. **Timing rule**: ask when the faulty valve should be open (stenosis) or shut (leak). Aortic/pulmonary stenosis and mitral/tricuspid regurgitation are **systolic**. Mitral/tricuspid stenosis and aortic/pulmonary regurgitation are **diastolic**.
11. **Auscultation areas**: aortic at the second right intercostal space; pulmonary at the second left; tricuspid at the lower left sternal border; mitral at the apex. These are where each valve's sound comes out, not where the valves sit.
12. **Bruits** follow the same rule as murmurs: find the pressure difference driving the abnormal flow, and when it exists. A PDA is continuous because aortic pressure stays above pulmonary pressure all the time.
