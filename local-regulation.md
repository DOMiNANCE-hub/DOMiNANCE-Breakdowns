# Local Control of Blood Flow

## A tissue can adjust its own supply
**Autoregulation** is a tissue's ability to keep flow relatively stable despite moderate changes in perfusion pressure. It is especially important in cerebral, renal and coronary circulations. A change in arteriole calibre alters local resistance: **Q = ΔP/R**.

## Metabolic mechanism
When local tissue metabolism rises or perfusion falls, vasodilator signals tend to accumulate. Relevant signals can include increased CO₂/H⁺, K⁺, adenosine, lactate in some contexts, and reduced tissue O₂. Dilation increases flow, improves oxygen delivery and helps wash out signals. Different tissues do **not** use identical mixtures of mediators.

Increased activity causes **active hyperaemia** (e.g. exercise in skeletal muscle). Transient vessel occlusion followed by a flow overshoot causes **reactive hyperaemia**, reflecting accumulated dilator signals and other mechanisms during reduced flow.

## Myogenic mechanism
An abrupt increase in intraluminal pressure stretches resistance-vessel smooth muscle, which responds by contracting. This increases resistance and helps prevent flow from rising excessively. Reduced stretch can favour relaxation. The myogenic response and metabolic control often act together.

**Laplace's principle:** a larger-radius thin-walled cylindrical vessel requires greater circumferential tension to oppose a given transmural pressure (approximately T = P × r per unit axial length). Different wall geometries use different mathematical forms. This helps explain why vessel radius matters to wall stress, though myogenic autoregulation involves active smooth-muscle signalling, not Laplace's law alone.

## Endothelial signals
**Nitric oxide (NO)** produced by endothelium promotes smooth-muscle relaxation through cGMP. Endothelin is a powerful vasoconstrictor. Local endothelial, neural and metabolic influences collectively determine vessel calibre.

**Check:** A resistance arteriole is suddenly exposed to greater perfusion pressure. How can myogenic autoregulation oppose a large rise in flow?

## Compare metabolic and myogenic autoregulation
**Metabolic theory:** When local perfusion fails to match tissue activity, oxygen may fall while CO₂, H⁺, adenosine, K⁺ and other local signals accumulate. Arteriolar dilation increases blood delivery and helps wash out metabolites. This predicts **active hyperaemia** during heightened metabolism (for instance exercising skeletal muscle) and **reactive hyperaemia** following temporary arterial occlusion: the longer/deeper the interruption, the greater the transient unmet demand and typical flow overshoot when obstruction is released.

**Myogenic mechanism:** Higher **transmural pressure = intravascular − surrounding pressure** distends arteriolar smooth muscle, activating contraction and raising resistance; decreased pressure tends to reduce stretch-induced tone. This stabilises local flow despite moderate driving-pressure changes and is important, for example, in cerebral and renal vascular beds. Metabolic and myogenic explanations are **complementary**, not mutually exclusive.

## Do not memorise the incorrect Laplace formula
For an ideal **thin-walled cylinder**, circumferential tension per unit axial length is approximately **T = Ptm × r**. If describing average circumferential wall **stress**, **σθ ≈ Ptm × r / wall thickness**. For an ideal sphere, the corresponding membrane tension is **T = Ptm × r / 2**. These are model-dependent, and real arteries have thick, active walls; the inconsistent reciprocal/sum expressions on Regulation slides 9–10 should not be reproduced without fixing the definitions.

## Endothelium: active signalling organ
**Nitric oxide (NO)** activates smooth-muscle soluble guanylyl cyclase → **cGMP** → relaxation. **Endothelin-1** is a potent constrictor. **Histamine and bradykinin** can promote vasodilatation and permeability changes through context-dependent pathways. Cold commonly favours cutaneous vasoconstriction. These responses depend on vascular bed and receptor distribution; do not infer one mediator affects every vessel identically.

**Practice:** What happens to autoregulatory arteriolar tone if local perfusion pressure suddenly increases? Myogenic constriction tends to counter excessive flow; the metabolic response depends on local tissue needs.
