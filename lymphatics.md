# Lymphatic Circulation

## 1. Why do we need lymphatic vessels if we already have veins?

Every day, capillaries filter fluid and a little protein into the tissues, and most of it doesn't go straight back into the blood. **Capillaries can't take protein back at all.** Without another route, tissues would steadily swell. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/microcirculation?section=6-classic-starling-model-versus-revised-understanding">→ Microcirculation, §6</a>)*

The **lymphatic system** is that route. It collects the excess fluid, proteins and particles from the tissues and returns them to the veins. Once inside a lymph vessel, the fluid is called **lymph**.

<div class="lymph-flow" role="group" aria-label="Lymphatic return route"><span>Capillary filtration</span><b>→</b><span>Interstitial fluid</span><b>→</b><span>Lymph capillaries</span><b>→</b><span>Lymph vessels and ducts</span><b>→</b><span>Venous blood</span></div>

**The lymphatics are a main route, not a back-up.** In most tissues, they return most of the fluid filtered from the capillaries. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/microcirculation?section=6-classic-starling-model-versus-revised-understanding">→ Microcirculation, §6</a>)*

## 2. How does lymph enter a lymphatic capillary?

**Lymph capillaries start as blind-ended tubes** in the tissues, closed at one end like the finger of a glove. Their wall is one layer of endothelial cells whose edges **overlap like roof tiles**. Fine **anchoring filaments** tie these cells to the surrounding tissue.

**One-way flaps:**

- When tissue fluid builds up, it pushes on the overlapping edges, and the anchoring filaments pull them apart, so the **flaps open inwards** and fluid, proteins and even bacteria flow in.
- When pressure inside the lymph capillary rises, the flaps are **pushed shut**, so nothing leaks back out.

This is how **proteins** get back into the circulation. They are too big to re-enter blood capillaries, but they pass easily through the lymphatic flaps.

## 3. How can lymph move when there is no central lymphatic heart?

Lymph flows at very low pressure, with no heart to pump it. Several things keep it moving:

- **The vessels pump themselves.** Larger lymph vessels have smooth muscle that contracts rhythmically, segment by segment.
- **Valves** inside the vessels allow flow only towards the heart.
- **The skeletal muscle pump.** Moving muscles squeeze the lymph vessels, just as they squeeze veins. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/venous-return?section=4-how-do-the-skeletal-muscle-pump-and-venous-valves-work">→ Venous Return, §4</a>)*
- **Breathing.** Pressure changes in the chest and abdomen draw lymph upwards.
- **Pulsing arteries** next to lymph vessels squeeze them with each beat.

**Like veins, but with one key difference:** veins continue on from blood capillaries in a closed circuit. Lymphatics **start blind, in the tissue**, and only join the blood at the very end.

## 4. Where does lymph finally return to blood?

All lymph drains into two main ducts. Each empties into the veins at the **venous angle**, where the <span data-term="jvp">internal jugular vein</span> joins the subclavian vein:

| Duct | What it drains | Where it empties |
|---|---|---|
| **Thoracic duct** | **Everything except the right upper quarter**: both legs, the abdomen, the left side of the chest, left arm, left head and neck | **Left** venous angle |
| **Right lymphatic duct** | **The right upper quarter only**: the right side of the head and neck, the right arm, the right side of the chest | **Right** venous angle |

**Memory hook:** the thoracic duct takes **three-quarters** of the body; the right lymphatic duct takes the **right upper quarter**.

### What do lymph nodes do?

On its way, lymph passes through **lymph nodes**: bean-shaped filters packed with immune cells. They check the lymph for bacteria, viruses and cancer cells. That is why nodes swell during an infection, and why cancer often spreads first to the nearest nodes. **Nodes filter; they don't pump.**

## 5. Why is the intestinal lymphatic system important in fat absorption?

Each finger-like villus of the small intestine contains a lymph capillary called a **lacteal** (*lac* = milk, after the milky look of lymph after a fatty meal).

**How fat gets into the blood:**

<div class="lymph-flow" role="group" aria-label="Intestinal lipid absorption through lymphatics"><span>Long-chain dietary lipids</span><b>→</b><span>Enterocyte chylomicrons</span><b>→</b><span>Villus lacteal</span><b>→</b><span>Mesenteric lymph</span><b>→</b><span>Thoracic duct → Blood</span></div>

1. Gut cells absorb the digested fats and repackage them into large fat droplets wrapped in protein: **chylomicrons**.
2. Chylomicrons are **too big** to enter blood capillaries, so they enter the leaky **lacteals** instead.
3. They travel up the lymphatics and the thoracic duct, and enter the blood at the left <span data-term="venous-angle">venous angle</span>.

This milky, fat-rich lymph from the gut is called **chyle**.

**Not all fat goes this way.** Short- and medium-chain fatty acids are small enough to go straight into the blood capillaries, and on to the liver in the <span data-term="portal-vein">portal vein</span>. It is the **long-chain fats** (and fat-soluble vitamins) that need the lymph.

## 6. How does lymphatic drainage protect tissue fluid balance?

The lymphatics keep the tissues from swelling by matching what the capillaries filter. When filtration rises, lymph flow can rise several-fold to keep up, which is a safety margin. **Oedema appears only when filtration outstrips this, or when the lymphatics themselves fail.** *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/edema?section=1-what-exactly-is-oedema">→ Oedema, §1</a>)*

| Problem | What fails | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Too much filtration** | The lymphatics can't keep up | Venous congestion, heart failure |
| **Too little drainage** | Normal filtration, but it can't be removed | Filariasis, surgery that removes lymph nodes |

## 7. Why does lymphatic obstruction cause lymphoedema?

When lymphatics are blocked, protein-rich fluid builds up. The protein holds the water in the tissue, and over time causes inflammation and scarring. The result is **lymphoedema**: swelling that is soft at first and becomes **firm and non-pitting** as it lasts. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/edema?section=8-why-are-pitting-and-non-pitting-oedema-different">→ Oedema, §8</a>)*

The lecture's examples:

- **Filariasis:** parasitic worms (spread by mosquitoes) live in and damage the lymph vessels, and can cause the gross swelling of **elephantiasis**.
- **Radical mastectomy:** removing the armpit lymph nodes in breast cancer surgery can leave the arm swollen.

Cancer blocking lymph nodes, and lymphatics that never formed properly (congenital), can do the same.

## 8. What happens if lymphatic function is lost?

Three jobs fail:

- **Fluid balance:** fluid and protein stay in the tissues, causing <span data-term="lymphoedema">lymphoedema</span>.
- **Immune defence:** lymph no longer reaches the nodes, so infections are detected and fought less well, and swollen limbs get infected easily.
- **Fat absorption:** if the gut lymphatics fail, long-chain fats and fat-soluble vitamins can't be absorbed properly.

## 9. Chapter Essentials

1. **Lymphatics return** excess tissue fluid and **all escaped protein** to the blood. They are a main route, not a back-up.
2. **Lymph capillaries** are blind-ended, with overlapping flaps that open inwards and shut against backflow.
3. **Lymph moves** by vessel-wall contractions, valves, the muscle pump, breathing and pulsing arteries. There is no heart, and nodes don't pump.
4. **Thoracic duct** drains three-quarters of the body into the **left** venous angle. **Right lymphatic duct** drains the right upper quarter into the **right** venous angle.
5. **Lymph nodes** filter lymph for the immune system.
6. **Lacteals** carry long-chain fats as **chylomicrons**. Milky gut lymph is **chyle**. Short- and medium-chain fats go straight to the blood.
7. **Lymph flow can rise several-fold** to prevent oedema. Swelling appears when filtration outstrips it or drainage fails.
8. **Lymphoedema** (filariasis, node removal, cancer): protein-rich, becoming firm and non-pitting.
9. **Lost lymphatics** → swelling (fluid and protein are left in the tissues), weaker immune defence (lymph no longer reaches the nodes' immune cells) and poor fat absorption (lacteals can't carry chylomicrons).
