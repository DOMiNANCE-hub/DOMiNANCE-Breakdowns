# Lymphatic Circulation

## A return route for interstitial fluid and proteins
Lymphatic capillaries begin blindly in tissues and collect water, proteins and other materials that cannot simply remain in the interstitium. Collecting lymphatics contain valves and contractile segments; skeletal-muscle activity and external pressure help lymph move toward the venous circulation.

Most of the body drains via the **thoracic duct** into the left venous angle near the internal jugular–subclavian junction. The **right lymphatic duct** drains a smaller right-upper quadrant territory to the right venous angle. Lymph nodes also support immune surveillance.

## Why lymphatic flow matters to Starling balance
Microvessels continually filter some fluid into tissue spaces. Lymphatics return excess fluid and leaked proteins to the circulation. If filtration rises sufficiently, lymph flow initially increases; when capacity is overwhelmed, oedema results.

Blockage after node surgery, malignancy or filarial infection can cause lymphoedema. Lymphatic obstruction can yield protein-rich interstitial fluid, leading over time to tissue inflammation and fibrosis.

## Map lymphatic return to venous blood
Initial lymphatic capillaries are blind-ended channels with overlapping endothelial flaps that admit fluid, proteins and some particles from interstitium. Collecting vessels have **valves** and rhythmic smooth-muscle activity; skeletal-muscle movement, breathing and nearby arterial pulsation help propel lymph toward the venous angles.

The **thoracic duct** drains most of the body, usually entering the junction of left internal jugular and left subclavian veins. The **right lymphatic duct** drains the right upper quadrant into the corresponding right venous angle; anatomy varies. Lymph passes through **lymph nodes**, where immune surveillance occurs. Specialized intestinal lymphatics called **lacteals** transport absorbed long-chain dietary lipids as **chylomicrons**; they do not directly absorb all lipids by the same route.

## Why lymphatic obstruction causes swelling
When capillaries filter fluid and leak proteins into the interstitium, lymphatics return that material to blood. An obstruction (e.g. **filariasis** or surgery affecting lymph drainage) reduces clearance of interstitial fluid and protein, contributing to chronic swelling and potentially fibrosis.
