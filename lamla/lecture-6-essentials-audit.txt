LAMLA editorial notes — lecture-6

The lecture groups six regional beds. Slide 5 incorrectly implies normal CSF occupies the subdural space; the approved cerebral chapter correctly places CSF in the subarachnoid space. Slide 24 loosely names amino acids/lipids/proteins as alternative brain fuels; the approved chapter identifies ketone bodies during prolonged starvation. Slides 28–29 contain compressed/ambiguous coronary statements; the approved chapter's net β₁-mediated metabolic vasodilatation and reversible angina versus infarction are followed. Slides 47–48 are lymphatic reference images; the dedicated approved Lymphatics chapter covers that topic.

- Chapter: 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier | Current line: "**Fuel:** glucose (about 90%), taken up without insulin. Prolonged starvation: ketone bodies." | Suggested replacement: "**Brain fuel:** glucose supplies about 90% of cerebral energy and enters without insulin; during prolonged starvation, liver-derived ketone bodies supply some energy."
- Chapter: 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier | Current line: "**Supply:** internal carotids + vertebrals → basilar, joined in the **Circle of Willis**. Beyond the ring, **little anastomosis**." | Suggested replacement: "**Brain supply:** carotids and vertebral–basilar arteries connect through the Circle of Willis, but few distal anastomoses mean sudden branch occlusion still causes ischaemia."
- Chapter: 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier | Current line: "**Monro–Kellie:** brain + blood + CSF in a rigid skull. **CPP = MAP − ICP.**" | Suggested replacement: "**Monro–Kellie:** added intracranial brain/blood/CSF volume raises ICP once displacement reserve is exhausted; CPP = MAP − ICP, so rising ICP lowers cerebral perfusion."
- Chapter: 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier | Current line: "**Hydrocephalus:** non-communicating (pathway blocked) or communicating (absorption fails)." | Suggested replacement: "**Hydrocephalus:** obstruction within ventricular drainage causes non-communicating CSF buildup; failure of arachnoid-villus absorption causes communicating buildup despite open pathways."
- Chapter: 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier | Current line: "**Stroke:** ischaemic (blocked) or haemorrhagic (ruptured, often an aneurysm)." | Suggested replacement: "**Stroke:** occlusion from plaque/thrombus/embolus cuts tissue perfusion (ischaemic); arterial rupture causes bleeding and possible ICP rise (haemorrhagic)."

- Chapter: 23 — Coronary Circulation | Current line: "**The <span data-term=\"subendocardium\">subendocardium</span>** is the most vulnerable layer." | Suggested replacement: "**Subendocardium:** this inner ventricular layer is compressed most strongly in systole, so reduced diastolic perfusion makes it ischaemic first."
- Chapter: 23 — Coronary Circulation | Current line: "**Active hyperaemia:** adenosine and other metabolites dilate the arterioles. Sympathetic β₁ effects make the net result dilation." | Suggested replacement: "**Active coronary hyperaemia:** increased myocardial work releases adenosine and other vasodilators; β₁ stimulation increases work, so metabolic dilation dominates direct α constriction."
- Chapter: 23 — Coronary Circulation | Current line: "**Narrowed artery** → reserve used up at rest → **angina on exertion**. **Infarction** = muscle death from prolonged ischaemia." | Suggested replacement: "**Coronary narrowing:** resting distal dilation preserves baseline flow but exhausts reserve → exertion causes reversible ischaemic angina; prolonged severe ischaemia causes irreversible infarction."

- Chapter: 24 — Pulmonary Circulation | Current line: "**Exercise:** recruitment and distension let flow rise with little rise in pressure." | Suggested replacement: "**Exercise:** previously closed lung capillaries recruit and open ones distend → resistance falls, so cardiac output rises with little pulmonary-pressure increase."
- Chapter: 24 — Pulmonary Circulation | Current line: "**Upright:** most flow at the base. **West zones** compare alveolar, arterial and venous pressures." | Suggested replacement: "**West zones:** upright basal perfusion is greater; zone 1 PA>Pa>Pv gives no flow, zone 2 Pa>PA>Pv limits flow, zone 3 Pa>Pv>PA allows continuous flow."
- Chapter: 24 — Pulmonary Circulation | Current line: "**Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** diverts blood to well-ventilated alveoli. When hypoxia is everywhere, it causes **pulmonary hypertension**." | Suggested replacement: "**Low alveolar O₂** constricts local pulmonary arteries to redirect flow; when hypoxia affects all alveoli, widespread constriction raises resistance and pulmonary pressure."

- Chapter: 25 — Splanchnic and Hepatic Circulation | Current line: "**Gut arteries:** coeliac trunk, superior mesenteric and inferior mesenteric." | Suggested replacement: "**Gut arterial inflow:** coeliac, superior mesenteric and inferior mesenteric branches of the aorta deliver blood to abdominal digestive organs."
- Chapter: 25 — Splanchnic and Hepatic Circulation | Current line: "**After meals**, gut flow rises. **In haemorrhage**, splanchnic vessels constrict, mobilising blood but risking gut ischaemia." | Suggested replacement: "**Splanchnic flow:** post-meal metabolism dilates gut arterioles; haemorrhage triggers sympathetic constriction to mobilise stored blood, but prolonged restriction risks gut ischaemia."
- Chapter: 25 — Splanchnic and Hepatic Circulation | Current line: "**Ascites:** portal hypertension + lymph overflow + salt retention + low albumin (cirrhosis), or right heart failure." | Suggested replacement: "**Ascites:** portal/hepatic venous pressure increases sinusoidal filtration; lymph overflow, renal salt retention and reduced albumin worsen fluid accumulation in the peritoneum."

- Chapter: 26 — Cutaneous Circulation and the Triple Response | Current line: "**Haemorrhage → cold, pale skin** (sympathetic shutdown); early septic shock may give warm skin." | Suggested replacement: "**Shock and skin:** haemorrhage raises sympathetic cutaneous vasoconstriction, producing cold/pale skin; early sepsis may cause warm skin through vasodilatation."
- Chapter: 26 — Cutaneous Circulation and the Triple Response | Current line: "**Triple response:** **red line** (local dilation) → **flare** (axon reflex) → **wheal** (histamine, leaky capillaries, oedema)." | Suggested replacement: "**Lewis triple response:** local capillary dilation forms red line; antidromic sensory-axon signals dilate nearby arterioles (flare); histamine increases permeability, producing wheal."

- Chapter: 27 — Fetal Circulation and the Transition at Birth | Current line: "**Remnants:** fossa ovalis, ligamentum arteriosum, ligamentum venosum, ligamentum teres, medial umbilical ligaments." | Suggested replacement: "**Remnants:** foramen ovale→fossa ovalis; ductus arteriosus→ligamentum arteriosum; ductus venosus→ligamentum venosum; umbilical vein→teres; distal umbilical arteries→medial ligaments."
- Chapter: 27 — Fetal Circulation and the Transition at Birth | Current line: "**At birth:** the first breaths lower pulmonary resistance; cord clamping raises systemic resistance; **left pressure > right**." | Suggested replacement: "**Birth transition:** lung aeration lowers pulmonary resistance and raises left atrial return, while cord clamping raises systemic resistance → left atrial pressure exceeds right and shuts the foramen ovale."

## Source discrepancies and checks

- **Cerebral:** Slide 5 suggests CSF in the subdural space. The approved chapter correctly limits normal CSF to the subarachnoid space. Slide 24 loosely lists fats/proteins as starvation fuel; the approved chapter specifies ketone bodies.
- **Coronary:** Slide 28 compresses α/β effects and appears to misstate the net β₁-metabolic effect. Use the approved chapter: β₁ increases work and local metabolites, causing net coronary vasodilatation. The chapter also adds the supply/demand mechanism of angina.
- **Pulmonary:** Slide 31 lists 10 and 25 mmHg pressures, but West zone definitions and exercise recruitment/distension are developed in the approved chapter; include these mechanisms.
- **Splanchnic:** Slide 37 suggests a universally protein-rich ascitic fluid. The approved chapter correctly explains that ascitic protein content varies by cause.
- **Skin:** The approved chapter expands the axon reflex to include substance P and CGRP; the slide names the antidromic reflex but does not state the specific mediators.
- **Fetal:** Slide 45 describes HbF as disappearing at four months. The approved chapter describes *gradual* replacement over months, avoiding an instantaneous cutoff.
- **Reference images:** Slides 47–48 are lymphatics, covered separately in the approved Chapter 15. The LAMLA includes the Chapter 15 lymphatics sheet in this lecture and in Circulation 1, per the requested coverage.

Chapter 15 — Lymphatic Circulation (additional audit, reused from Circulation 1)

- Chapter: 15 — Lymphatic Circulation | Current line: "**Lymph moves** by vessel-wall contractions, valves, the muscle pump, breathing and pulsing arteries. There is no heart, and nodes don't pump." | Suggested replacement: "**Lymph propulsion:** intrinsic lymphatic contractions and external muscle, respiratory and arterial compression propel lymph proximally; one-way valves prevent reflux, nodes filter rather than pump."
- Chapter: 15 — Lymphatic Circulation | Current line: "**Lymph flow can rise several-fold** to prevent oedema. Swelling appears when filtration outstrips it or drainage fails." | Suggested replacement: "**Lymphatic reserve:** increased filtration raises lymph formation and collecting-vessel pumping; persistent swelling develops once lymphatic transport capacity is exceeded or obstructed."
- Chapter: 15 — Lymphatic Circulation | Current line: "**Lost lymphatics** → swelling, weaker immune defence, poor fat absorption." | Suggested replacement: "**Lymphatic loss:** trapped tissue fluid/protein causes swelling; lost node transport impairs immune surveillance; failed intestinal lacteal drainage reduces long-chain fat absorption."
