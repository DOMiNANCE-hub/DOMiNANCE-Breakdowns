## Chapter 6 — Electrocardiography and Basic Rhythms

### Must-know
- ★ **ECG** records voltage differences from cardiac depolarisation and repolarisation at the skin; it does not directly measure contraction or blood flow.
- **Electrical activation** normally precedes muscle contraction → an ECG complex can appear even when the heart is not pumping effectively.
- **Electrode** means the physical skin contact; a lead is the electrical view calculated from electrode voltages.
- **Twelve-lead ECG** uses ten electrodes to create twelve views; the right-leg electrode reduces electrical interference rather than providing a lead.
- ★ **Depolarisation direction** determines polarity: towards a lead's positive end gives an upward deflection; away gives a downward deflection.
- **Perpendicular depolarisation** produces a small or biphasic deflection because little of the electrical vector points along the lead.
- **Lead II** usually has an upright QRS, while aVR usually has a downward QRS, because they view ventricular depolarisation from different directions.
- **T-wave direction** often matches the QRS because ventricular repolarisation travels in reverse order and has electrical polarity opposite to depolarisation.
- **Standard limb leads** I, II and III are bipolar because each measures voltage between a negative and a positive limb electrode.
- **Lead I** measures right arm negative to left arm positive; its positive viewing direction points towards the left arm.
- **Lead II** measures right arm negative to left leg positive; its positive viewing direction points towards the left leg.
- **Lead III** measures left arm negative to left leg positive; its positive viewing direction points towards the left leg.
- **Einthoven's law** states Lead II = Lead I + Lead III for voltages measured at the same instant.
- **Augmented limb leads** aVR, aVL and aVF view from right arm, left arm and left foot respectively, against an averaged limb reference.
- **Chest leads** V1–V6 view the heart in the horizontal plane, whereas limb leads view it in the frontal plane.
- **V1 and V2** sit at the fourth intercostal space beside the sternum, on the right and left respectively.
- **V4** sits at the fifth intercostal space in the midclavicular line; V3 lies halfway between V2 and V4.
- **V5 and V6** sit level with V4 at the anterior and midaxillary lines respectively; wrong placement can distort the ECG.
- ★ **P wave** represents atrial depolarisation spreading from the SA node; atrial contraction follows the electrical event.
- ★ **PR interval** runs from the start of P to the start of QRS, including atrial activation and delayed conduction towards the ventricles.
- **PR segment** starts at the end of P and stops at the start of QRS; it excludes the P wave itself.
- ★ **QRS complex** represents ventricular depolarisation; its larger amplitude reflects the greater mass of ventricular muscle.
- **Q, R and S** name successive downward, upward and downward deflections; an individual lead may not display all three.
- **Atrial repolarisation** is usually hidden within the much larger QRS complex rather than forming a distinct visible wave.
- **ST segment** runs from the end of QRS to the start of T, while most ventricular muscle is depolarised in its plateau phase.
- **Flat ST segment** does not mean ventricular rest; ischaemia or hypoxia can produce ST elevation or depression.
- **T wave** represents ventricular repolarisation, which normally lasts longer than ventricular depolarisation.
- **U wave** sometimes follows T; the lecture calls it late ventricular repolarisation, although its exact origin is uncertain.
- **QT interval** starts with QRS and ends with T → it spans ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation and shortens as heart rate rises.
- **ECG calibration** at 25 mm/s gives 0.04 s per horizontal millimetre; at 10 mm/mV, each vertical millimetre is 0.1 mV.
- **One large ECG square** is 5 mm → 0.20 s horizontally and 0.5 mV vertically at standard calibration.
- **Heart rate** for a regular rhythm at 25 mm/s equals 1500 divided by small squares, or 300 divided by large squares, between R waves.
- **RR interval** is the time between R waves → heart rate in beats/min equals 60 divided by RR measured in seconds.
- **Irregular rhythms** have unequal RR gaps → counting QRS complexes over six seconds and multiplying by ten gives an average rate.
- **Normal sinus rhythm** passes four checks: rate about 60–100 beats/min at rest, equal RR gaps, a P wave before every QRS, and normal PR and QRS durations.
- **First-degree AV block** prolongs the PR interval beyond 0.20 s while every P wave still conducts to a QRS.
- **Second-degree AV block** causes some P waves to lack a following QRS because some atrial impulses fail to conduct.
- **Third-degree AV block** shows independent P waves and QRS complexes because atrial impulses no longer conduct to the ventricles.
- **ECG and mechanics** differ in timing: P precedes atrial systole, QRS precedes ventricular systole, and T accompanies the transition towards relaxation.

### Core chains
1. **Recording an ECG**: Cardiac cells depolarise or repolarise → voltage differences spread through tissues → skin electrodes detect differences → leads display electrical traces.
2. **Lead polarity**: Depolarisation vector points towards a lead's positive end → positive voltage is recorded → ECG deflects upwards; a vector pointing away produces a downward deflection.
3. **Electrical-to-mechanical sequence**: SA-node activation spreads through atria → P wave → atrial contraction → AV conduction delay → QRS → ventricular contraction → T wave → ventricular relaxation.
4. **Finding heart rate**: Count 22 small squares between R waves at 25 mm/s → RR = 22 × 0.04 = 0.88 s → 60 ÷ 0.88 ≈ 68 beats/min.

### Exam traps
- Trap: **ECG activity** proves the heart is pumping → Truth: It measures electrical activity, so effective blood ejection requires separate assessment.
- Trap: **An upward ECG wave** means the heart moves upwards → Truth: It shows the direction of electrical activity relative to the positive lead.
- Trap: **Ten electrodes** give only ten leads → Truth: One electrode arrangement can generate twelve voltage views.
- Trap: **PR interval** begins after the P wave → Truth: It starts at the beginning of P; the PR segment begins after P ends.
- Trap: **The QRS** is the ventricles contracting → Truth: It is the electrical switch-on of the ventricles; contraction follows it a moment later.
- Trap: **Atrial repolarisation** must form a separate visible ECG wave → Truth: Its small electrical signal is usually buried within QRS.
- Trap: **A flat ST segment** means the ventricles are relaxed → Truth: Ventricular cells are depolarised and mechanically active during the plateau.
- Trap: **QT interval** ends when T begins → Truth: It ends when T finishes because it includes ventricular repolarisation.
- Trap: **One horizontal small square** measures 0.1 mV → Truth: Horizontal distance measures time; voltage is vertical height.
- Trap: **1500 ÷ large squares** gives heart rate → Truth: Use 1500 for small squares and 300 for large squares at 25 mm/s.
- Trap: **One RR gap** reliably estimates an irregular rhythm → Truth: Unequal gaps require averaging over several beats, such as six seconds.
- Trap: **First-degree AV block** means missed ventricular beats → Truth: Every P still produces QRS, but the PR interval is prolonged.
