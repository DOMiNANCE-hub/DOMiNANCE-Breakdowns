## Chapter 19 — Gravity, Posture and Acceleration

### Must-know
- ★ **Hydrostatic pressure** increases below heart level and decreases above it because the vertical column of blood has weight.
- **Standing head pressure** is about 60 mmHg (mean) because the head sits about 45 cm above the heart, so gravity removes about 35 mmHg.
- **Standing foot pressure** is about 180 mmHg (mean) because the feet sit about 115 cm below the heart, so the blood column adds about 90 mmHg.
- **Leg vein pooling** occurs on standing because compliant veins expand under increased hydrostatic pressure, trapping blood away from the heart.
- ★ **Initial standing response** reduces venous return → end-diastolic volume falls → stroke volume and cardiac output fall through Frank–Starling physiology.
- **Dependent oedema** develops during prolonged standing because raised leg capillary hydrostatic pressure promotes fluid filtration into tissues.
- **Cerebral hypoperfusion** causes light-headedness on standing because head-level arterial pressure and cardiac output initially decrease.
- ★ **Baroreceptor unloading** during standing reduces carotid sinus and aortic arch firing → sympathetic activity rises and vagal activity falls.
- **Positive chronotropy** increases heart rate during standing because reduced vagal tone and greater sympathetic drive accelerate the SA node.
- **Positive inotropy** strengthens ventricular contraction during standing because sympathetic stimulation raises cardiac contractility.
- **Arteriolar constriction** raises total peripheral resistance during standing, helping restore arterial blood pressure.
- **Venoconstriction** reduces peripheral blood storage → more blood returns to the heart and supports stroke volume.
- ★ **Skeletal-muscle pump** squeezes deep leg veins during walking; one-way valves direct blood upward and prevent pooling.
- **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone** supports pressure during sustained upright posture through vasoconstriction and renal sodium/water retention.
- **Cerebral perfusion gradient** is partly preserved on standing because cerebral venous pressure decreases along with arterial pressure.
- **Falling intracranial pressure** protects cerebral flow on standing because it reduces external compression of cerebral vessels.
- **Cerebral metabolic dilation** responds to increased local CO₂ and reduced O₂ by widening brain arterioles and limiting flow loss.
- **Prolonged motionless standing** weakens the muscle-pump defence → venous pooling worsens and cerebral flow may become insufficient.
- **Fainting after prolonged standing** can restore cerebral perfusion because lying horizontally brings the head close to heart level.
- **Vasovagal syncope** causes hypotension through sympathetic withdrawal and bradycardia through increased vagal activity.
- **Micturition syncope** occurs during urination, often after rising at night, because reflex hypotension reduces cerebral blood flow.
- **Carotid sinus syncope** follows pressure on a sensitive carotid sinus → reflex bradycardia and vasodilatation lower cerebral flow.
- ★ **Orthostatic hypotension** occurs when arterial pressure falls excessively after standing because compensatory sympathetic responses or circulating volume are inadequate.
- **Autonomic neuropathy** causes orthostatic hypotension because damaged sympathetic nerves fail to constrict vessels appropriately, as in diabetes.
- **Sympatholytic drugs** cause postural hypotension by preventing normal sympathetic vasoconstriction when blood pools in the legs.
- **Autonomic failure** may prevent heart rate and plasma noradrenaline from rising on standing despite reduced arterial pressure.
- **Low blood volume** worsens orthostatic hypotension because there is less circulating blood available to maintain venous return.
- **Positive acceleration (+g)** shifts blood towards the feet → cerebral perfusion and retinal perfusion fall, causing grey-out or blackout.
- **Vision loss under high +g** may precede unconsciousness because retinal perfusion fails when pressure at eye level becomes inadequate.
- **Negative acceleration (−g)** shifts blood towards the head → congestion, headache, red-out and possible bleeding around the eyes.
- **Intracranial-pressure rise under −g** helps protect cerebral vessels because venous congestion raises pressure outside them as arterial pressure rises.
- **Anti-g suits** compress the abdomen and legs → peripheral pooling decreases and venous return rises during positive acceleration.
- **Horizontal positioning** improves acceleration tolerance because the vertical blood column between the heart and brain becomes shorter.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrostatic pressure change with height | About 0.77 mmHg per cm | Explains lower pressure above the heart and higher pressure below it. |
| Mean pressure at head level while standing | About 60 mmHg | Lecture illustration without compensation; pressure is lower than at the heart. |
| Mean pressure at heart level | About 93 mmHg | Corresponds to approximately 120/80 mmHg. |
| Mean pressure at feet while standing | About 180 mmHg | Demonstrates the added hydrostatic column below the heart. |
| Blood pooled on first standing | About 500 mL | Less blood is immediately available for venous return. |
| Usual rise in heart rate on standing | About 10–20 beats/min | Demonstrates baroreflex compensation. |
| Orthostatic systolic-pressure fall | At least 20 mmHg | One diagnostic criterion within 3 minutes of standing. |
| Orthostatic diastolic-pressure fall | At least 10 mmHg | Alternative diagnostic criterion within 3 minutes of standing. |
| Positive acceleration associated with severe cerebral underperfusion | Above about +5 g | Vision can fail, followed by blackout and unconsciousness. |

### Core chains
1. **Standing and baroreflex rescue**: Stand upright → leg veins expand and pool blood → venous return and cardiac output fall → baroreceptors fire less → sympathetic drive raises rate, contractility and vascular tone → arterial pressure recovers.
2. **Protecting the brain**: Head-level arterial pressure falls → cerebral venous and intracranial pressures also fall → local CO₂ rises and O₂ falls → cerebral arterioles dilate → cerebral flow is partly maintained.
3. **Prolonged standing and syncope**: Remain motionless → no leg-muscle pumping → venous pooling persists → cerebral perfusion falls → fainting places the head near heart level → cerebral perfusion improves.
4. **Acceleration and countermeasures**: Strong +g → blood shifts towards legs → retinal and cerebral flow fall → anti-g suit compresses abdomen and legs → pooling decreases → venous return and head-level perfusion improve.

### Don't confuse
| | **Ordinary standing** | **Vasovagal syncope** |
|---|---|---|
| Initial autonomic response | Sympathetic activity rises and vagal activity falls. | Sympathetic activity withdraws and vagal activity increases. |
| Typical heart-rate response | Heart rate rises to defend pressure. | Heart rate slows as blood pressure falls. |
| Main vascular response | Arterioles and veins constrict. | Widespread vasodilatation reduces pressure. |

| | **Orthostatic hypotension from autonomic failure** | **Normal baroreflex on standing** |
|---|---|---|
| Sympathetic response | Insufficient vessel constriction; noradrenaline may fail to rise. | Vessel constriction and noradrenaline release increase. |
| Heart rate | May fail to increase appropriately. | Usually increases. |

| | **Positive g (+g)** | **Negative g (−g)** |
|---|---|---|
| Blood redistribution | Towards the feet and abdomen. | Towards the head and neck. |
| Main danger | Low retinal/cerebral flow → grey-out, blackout. | Head congestion → red-out, headache. |
| Protective response | Anti-g suit and horizontal positioning reduce pooling. | Increased intracranial pressure partly opposes vessel distension. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Standing pressure** is equal everywhere because the heart generates one pressure → Truth: Gravity adds or subtracts hydrostatic pressure according to height.
- Trap: **Standing tachycardia** means blood pressure initially rose → Truth: A pressure drop unloads baroreceptors, causing compensatory tachycardia.
- Trap: **Brain protection** occurs because cerebral arterial pressure never falls → Truth: Venous pressure, intracranial pressure and arteriolar resistance fall too.
- Trap: **Prolonged standing** is harmless if the person does not walk → Truth: Without the muscle pump, venous pooling can lead to fainting.
- Trap: **Vasovagal fainting** is simply a stronger sympathetic baroreflex → Truth: Sympathetic withdrawal and increased vagal firing produce vasodilatation and bradycardia.
- Trap: **Orthostatic hypotension** is diagnosed only by a low standing pressure → Truth: It is defined by a pressure fall after standing, within the specified time.
- Trap: **Negative g** causes the same blackout mechanism as positive g → Truth: Negative g shifts blood towards the head and produces congestion and red-out.
- Trap: **Anti-g suits** work by increasing heart muscle force directly → Truth: Abdominal and leg compression limits venous pooling and supports return.

## Chapter 20 — Circulatory Shock

### Must-know
- ★ **Shock** means tissue perfusion cannot sustain normal metabolism, so prolonged inadequacy causes cellular injury and organ dysfunction.
- ★ **Normal early blood pressure** does not exclude shock because sympathetic arteriolar constriction may temporarily maintain pressure despite poor tissue flow.
- **Hypovolaemic shock** follows loss of blood or fluid → reduced circulating volume impairs venous return and cardiac output.
- **Cardiogenic shock** follows pump failure → cardiac output decreases despite blood being present in the circulation.
- **Distributive shock** follows widespread vasodilatation → blood pools and effective perfusion falls despite initially normal volume.
- **Obstructive shock** follows blocked cardiac filling or forward blood flow → inadequate circulation reaches tissues.
- ★ **Haemorrhage** reduces blood volume → venous return falls → ventricular filling, stroke volume and cardiac output decrease.
- **Hypotension in haemorrhage** develops because reduced cardiac output can no longer sustain arterial pressure despite compensation.
- **Reflex tachycardia** follows lower baroreceptor stretch → sympathetic drive increases while vagal activity decreases.
- **Weak pulse in shock** results from reduced stroke volume, even when sympathetic stimulation makes the heart beat faster.
- **Cool, pale skin** occurs because sympathetic vasoconstriction restricts cutaneous blood flow to favour vital organs.
- **Low urine output** reflects reduced renal perfusion and filtration, alongside sodium and water retention; blood urea may rise.
- **Rapid respiration** occurs because hypoxia and lactic acidosis stimulate chemoreceptors and respiratory drive.
- **Thirst in shock** increases as angiotensin II and ADH rise in response to reduced circulating volume.
- **Restlessness early in shock** accompanies sympathetic arousal; later apathy may indicate cerebral ischaemia from prolonged hypoperfusion.
- ★ **Lactic acidosis** develops because inadequate oxygen delivery promotes anaerobic glycolysis; acidosis weakens the heart and vascular catecholamine responses.
- **Baroreflex compensation** increases sympathetic cardiac stimulation, arteriolar resistance and venous return when arterial pressure falls.
- **Blood redistribution** protects heart and brain relatively because constriction is stronger in skin, kidneys and splanchnic vessels.
- **Splanchnic venoconstriction** mobilises stored blood → venous return improves temporarily when circulating blood volume falls.
- **Pulmonary venous mobilisation** can shift stored blood towards systemic output, briefly supporting central filling during shock.
- **Adrenal medullary release** of adrenaline and noradrenaline reinforces sympathetic cardiac and vascular compensation.
- **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation** promotes vasoconstriction and renal sodium/water retention, helping defend pressure and volume.
- **ADH (vasopressin)** increases renal water retention and can constrict vessels, helping maintain circulation during volume loss.
- **ACTH and cortisol** rise during shock as part of the endocrine stress response described in the lecture.
- **Interstitial-to-plasma refill** occurs when capillary hydrostatic pressure falls → net fluid movement into blood helps restore volume.
- **Immediate haemoglobin concentration** may remain normal after acute whole-blood loss because red cells and plasma are lost together.
- **Delayed haemoglobin fall** follows tissue-fluid or infused-fluid replacement → red cells become diluted without immediate replacement.
- **Plasma protein dilution** follows fluid restoration after bleeding because fluid returns faster than lost proteins are replaced.
- **Erythropoietin** stimulates red-cell production over weeks, allowing haemoglobin to recover after haemorrhage.
- ★ **Progressive shock** becomes self-amplifying when poor perfusion injures the heart, brain vasomotor centre, kidneys and capillary circulation.
- **Myocardial ischaemia in shock** reduces contractility → cardiac output falls further → coronary perfusion worsens.
- **Vasomotor-centre ischaemia** eventually depresses sympathetic output → bradycardia and vasodilatation accelerate hypotension.
- **Acidosis in shock** reduces cardiac function and responsiveness to catecholamines → compensatory vasoconstriction becomes ineffective.
- **Capillary stasis** arises when precapillary sphincters relax but venules remain constricted → pressure rises and fluid leaks into tissues.
- **Acute tubular damage** may follow prolonged renal hypoperfusion, worsening organ failure during severe shock.
- **Irreversible shock** persists despite correction of blood volume because severe tissue damage prevents circulatory recovery.
- **Concealed haemorrhage** may occur with femoral fracture, ruptured spleen or ectopic pregnancy despite absent external bleeding.
- **Burn shock** causes plasma loss through damaged skin → haemoconcentration can increase haematocrit rather than decrease it.
- **Traumatic muscle injury** releases myoglobin, which can damage renal tubules and worsen shock complications.
- **Cardiogenic shock** may cause raised venous pressure and jugular venous pressure because blood backs up behind the failing heart.
- **Septic shock** involves bacterial toxins, widespread vasodilatation and capillary leak; skin may feel warm early.
- **Anaphylactic shock** follows histamine release during an antigen–antibody response → vasodilatation and increased capillary permeability.
- **Neurogenic shock** follows loss of sympathetic vascular tone → low peripheral resistance, sometimes with bradycardia.
- **Cardiac tamponade** impairs cardiac filling through external compression → obstructive shock reduces forward output.
- **Tension pneumothorax** can compress great veins and impair return to the heart → obstructive shock develops.
- **Pulmonary embolism** blocks pulmonary arterial flow → right-sided outflow obstruction reduces circulation through the lungs.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Approximate blood-volume recovery if bleeding stops | 12–72 hours | Fluid shift and renal retention restore volume before red cells are fully replaced. |
| Approximate haemoglobin recovery after blood loss | 4–5 weeks | Renal erythropoietin drives red-cell replacement over weeks. |
| Blood concealed by a fractured femur | More than 1 litre may be hidden | External inspection can underestimate serious blood loss. |

### Core chains
1. **Haemorrhagic shock**: Whole-blood loss → venous return falls → end-diastolic volume and stroke volume fall → cardiac output and organ perfusion decrease → sympathetic reflexes attempt compensation.
2. **Metabolic deterioration**: Tissue perfusion falls → oxygen delivery becomes inadequate → anaerobic glycolysis produces lactate → acidosis weakens myocardium and vascular catecholamine responses → perfusion falls further.
3. **Volume recovery versus haemoglobin recovery**: Bleeding stops → capillary fluid refill and salt/water retention restore plasma volume → haemoglobin concentration falls by dilution → erythropoietin restores red cells over weeks.
4. **Irreversible progression**: Prolonged hypoperfusion → cardiac and vasomotor-centre ischaemia plus capillary stasis → further hypotension and fluid loss → cellular injury persists despite later volume replacement.

### Don't confuse
| | **Hypovolaemic shock** | **Cardiogenic shock** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary defect | Blood/fluid volume is depleted. | Pump function is inadequate. |
| Venous filling pressure | Usually low after blood loss. | May be high because blood backs up. |
| Examples | Haemorrhage, burns, severe diarrhoea. | Myocardial infarction, heart failure, arrhythmia. |

| | **Distributive shock** | **Obstructive shock** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary defect | Widespread vessel dilation and pooling. | Physical blockage to filling or flow. |
| Examples | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock. | Tamponade, tension pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism. |

| | **Acute whole-blood haemorrhage** | **Burn-related plasma loss** |
|---|---|---|
| What is lost | Red cells and plasma together. | Mainly plasma through injured skin. |
| Early haemoglobin/haematocrit | Concentration may be initially unchanged. | Concentration may rise due to haemoconcentration. |
| Later course | Fluid replacement dilutes remaining red cells. | Fluid loss persists until corrected. |

| | **Compensated shock** | **Irreversible shock** |
|---|---|---|
| Circulatory responses | Sympathetic and hormonal responses still partly defend perfusion. | Heart, central vasomotor and microvascular damage prevent recovery. |
| Blood pressure | May appear near normal early. | Usually severely reduced despite attempted correction. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Shock** requires a very low measured blood pressure → Truth: Tissue perfusion may already be inadequate while vasoconstriction temporarily preserves pressure.
- Trap: **Tachycardia** proves adequate cardiac output → Truth: A rapid heart can still eject too little blood per beat.
- Trap: **A normal initial haemoglobin** excludes major bleeding → Truth: Whole blood loss preserves concentration until fluid redistribution causes dilution.
- Trap: **Burn shock** must initially lower haematocrit → Truth: Plasma escapes while red cells remain, causing haemoconcentration.
- Trap: **Renal oliguria** is only caused by dehydration → Truth: Reduced renal perfusion, lower filtration and hormonal sodium/water retention contribute.
- Trap: **All shock patients** have cold skin → Truth: Early septic shock may produce warm skin because cutaneous vessels are dilated.
- Trap: **Compensation** restores oxygen delivery completely → Truth: Prioritising brain and heart can coexist with severe kidney, skin and gut hypoperfusion.
- Trap: **Capillary filtration** must keep falling throughout shock → Truth: Late precapillary relaxation with venular constriction causes stasis and increased leakage.
- Trap: **Irreversible shock** means blood volume cannot be replaced → Truth: Circulation fails to recover even after volume is restored because tissues are damaged.
- Trap: **Obstructive shock** is simply vasodilatation → Truth: Tamponade, tension pneumothorax or embolism physically impedes blood flow.

## Chapter 21 — Heart Failure Physiology

### Must-know
- ★ **Heart failure** occurs when the heart cannot meet tissue needs, or does so only at abnormally high filling pressures.
- **Near-normal resting cardiac output** does not exclude failure because excessive ventricular filling pressure can sustain output while causing congestion.
- ★ **Forward failure** reduces blood delivered to organs → poor perfusion causes fatigue, exercise intolerance and renal sodium/water retention.
- ★ **Backward failure** raises pressure upstream of a failing chamber → venous congestion and fluid accumulation develop.
- **Systolic failure** reflects weak ventricular contraction → reduced ejection fraction and increased residual ventricular blood.
- **Diastolic failure** reflects impaired relaxation or stiff ventricles → high filling pressures despite often-preserved ejection fraction.
- **Preserved ejection fraction** does not exclude failure because a stiff ventricle may eject a normal fraction of a small filling volume.
- **Both systolic and diastolic failure** can cause congestion because ventricular filling pressure rises in either condition.
- **Tachycardia in a stiff ventricle** worsens filling because diastolic time shortens while the chamber already fills slowly.
- ★ **Left-sided backward failure** raises left atrial and pulmonary venous pressure → pulmonary capillary filtration increases and pulmonary oedema develops.
- **Pulmonary oedema** impairs gas exchange → breathlessness and rapid breathing may accompany left ventricular failure.
- **Orthopnoea** occurs because lying flat returns leg blood to the chest → a weak left ventricle cannot handle the extra filling.
- ★ **Right-sided backward failure** raises systemic venous pressure → jugular distension, liver congestion, ascites and dependent oedema develop.
- **Jugular venous pressure** rises in right-sided failure because impaired right ventricular emptying increases right atrial pressure.
- **Hepatomegaly and ascites** follow systemic venous congestion because hepatic pressure rises and abdominal fluid accumulates.
- **Ankle oedema** develops in right-sided failure because raised peripheral venous pressure increases capillary filtration.
- **Left-heart failure** commonly leads to right-heart failure because sustained pulmonary pressure increases right ventricular workload.
- **Sympathetic compensation** raises heart rate, contractility and resistance → pressure may improve briefly but afterload and workload increase.
- **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation** constricts vessels and retains sodium/water → initial perfusion support worsens long-term congestion.
- **ADH release** promotes renal water retention → fluid overload can increase despite already swollen tissues.
- **Effective arterial underfilling** stimulates renal fluid retention because a weak pump delivers too little pressure even when total body water is high.
- **Frank–Starling limitation** means extra filling of a failing ventricle raises pressure more than output because its function curve is flattened.
- **Cardiac remodelling** can thicken or dilate a chronically stressed ventricle → pumping efficiency deteriorates over time.

### Core chains
1. **Left-sided backward failure**: Left ventricle empties poorly → left atrial and lung venous pressures rise → pulmonary capillary filtration increases → pulmonary oedema develops → breathlessness worsens.
2. **Right-sided backward failure**: Right ventricle empties poorly → right atrial/systemic venous pressure rises → jugular veins distend and capillary filtration increases → liver congestion, ascites and ankle oedema develop.
3. **Maladaptive compensation**: Pump output falls → baroreceptors and kidneys perceive arterial underfilling → sympathetic, RAAS and ADH activity rise → afterload and fluid retention increase → ventricular failure and oedema worsen.
4. **Frank–Starling failure**: Ventricular filling volume increases → failing ventricular muscle provides little extra ejection → filling pressure rises disproportionately → upstream venous congestion increases.

### Don't confuse
| | **Forward failure** | **Backward failure** |
|---|---|---|
| Main problem | Too little blood reaches downstream tissues. | Too much blood/pressure builds up upstream. |
| Clinical consequence | Fatigue and poor organ perfusion. | Pulmonary or systemic venous congestion. |

| | **Left-sided failure** | **Right-sided failure** |
|---|---|---|
| Where blood backs up | Pulmonary veins and lungs. | Systemic veins, liver and legs. |
| Characteristic signs | Breathlessness, orthopnoea, pulmonary oedema. | Raised JVP, hepatomegaly, ascites, ankle oedema. |

| | **Systolic dysfunction** | **Diastolic dysfunction** |
|---|---|---|
| Principal mechanical defect | Weak contraction and poor ejection. | Impaired relaxation or stiff filling. |
| Ejection fraction | Usually reduced. | Often preserved. |
| Filling pressure | Rises because blood is left behind. | Rises because a stiff ventricle needs higher pressure to fill. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Heart failure** always means low cardiac output at rest → Truth: Output may appear adequate only because filling pressures are abnormally high.
- Trap: **Preserved ejection fraction** means the ventricle is healthy → Truth: A stiff ventricle can eject a normal proportion but fill at excessive pressure.
- Trap: **Left failure** primarily raises systemic venous pressure → Truth: Its characteristic backward effect is increased pulmonary venous pressure and oedema.
- Trap: **Right failure** primarily causes pulmonary oedema → Truth: Right-sided backward failure produces systemic venous congestion, raised JVP and dependent oedema.
- Trap: **Lying flat** relieves all heart-failure breathlessness → Truth: Greater venous return can worsen pulmonary congestion when the left ventricle fails.
- Trap: **Renal salt retention** indicates total-body dehydration → Truth: Effective arterial delivery may be low despite substantial whole-body fluid overload.
- Trap: **More preload** always substantially raises cardiac output → Truth: A failing ventricle may gain little output while filling pressure and congestion rise.
