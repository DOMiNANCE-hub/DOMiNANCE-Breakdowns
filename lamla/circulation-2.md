## Chapter 16 — Local Control of Blood Flow

### Must-know
- ★ **Autoregulation** keeps tissue blood flow relatively constant when perfusion pressure changes because local arterioles adjust their resistance.
- **Arteriolar radius** strongly controls blood flow because vascular resistance varies inversely with the fourth power of radius.
- **Autoregulation limits** matter because maximally dilated arterioles cannot compensate further when perfusion pressure becomes very low.
- ★ **Myogenic control** responds to increased vessel-wall stretch by contracting vascular smooth muscle → increased resistance limits the pressure-driven rise in flow.
- **Reduced transmural pressure** decreases arteriolar stretch → smooth muscle relaxes → resistance falls and flow is supported.
- **Transmural pressure** is pressure inside a vessel minus surrounding tissue pressure; it determines the distending force on the wall.
- **Laplace's law** for a cylinder states T = P × r → larger radius requires greater wall tension at the same transmural pressure.
- **A capillary's tiny radius** means it needs only a small wall tension, so a wall one cell thick can withstand blood pressure; a widened vessel (aneurysm) needs more tension and is more likely to burst.
- ★ **Metabolic control** increases blood flow when tissue demand exceeds supply because accumulated local substances dilate resistance vessels.
- **Low tissue oxygen** makes the local arterioles dilate → more oxygenated blood reaches the under-supplied tissue.
- **High CO₂, H⁺ and lactate** accompany inadequate supply or increased metabolism → local arterioles dilate and tissue flow rises.
- **K⁺, adenosine and ADP** accumulate with cellular activity or ATP breakdown → they help dilate local vessels, especially in active myocardium.
- **Metabolite washout** follows increased flow → local dilator signals fall and vascular resistance moves back towards resting levels.
- **Serotonin** released from platelets at a vessel injury favours local vasoconstriction → blood flow through the injured region falls.
- **Cold and angiotensin II** promote vasoconstriction → increased vascular resistance opposes blood flow.
- ★ **Active hyperaemia** occurs during increased tissue activity because accumulating metabolites dilate arterioles and raise blood flow to match demand.
- ★ **Reactive hyperaemia** occurs after temporary flow obstruction because metabolites accumulated during ischaemia and blood surges through dilated vessels on release.
- **Longer flow obstruction** produces a greater or longer reactive hyperaemic response because more oxygen debt and vasodilator signals accumulate.
- **Endothelial nitric oxide** is released with shear stress → it raises smooth-muscle cGMP and causes vasodilation.
- **Bradykinin, substance P and acetylcholine** can stimulate endothelial nitric oxide release → underlying vascular smooth muscle relaxes.
- **Prostacyclin** dilates vessels and inhibits platelet aggregation, whereas endothelin-1 constricts vascular smooth muscle.
- **Histamine (H₂) and ANP** promote vasodilation; ANP can act directly on vascular smooth muscle.
- **Endothelial damage** reduces nitric oxide availability → vessels lose some ability to dilate and become more prone to constriction.
- **Working skeletal muscle** can receive more blood during exercise despite sympathetic activation because strong local metabolic dilation opposes constriction.
- **Brain, kidney and heart flow** show strong autoregulation because stable perfusion is particularly important to their function.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Resistance–radius relationship | R ∝ 1/r⁴ | A small reduction in arteriolar radius sharply increases resistance. |
| Resistance after radius is halved | 16 times the original | Illustrates why small changes in vessel calibre strongly alter flow. |
| Cylindrical-vessel wall tension | T = P × r | At constant transmural pressure, larger vessels need greater wall tension. |

### Core chains
1. **Myogenic autoregulation**: Perfusion pressure rises → vessel wall stretches → smooth muscle contracts → arteriolar resistance rises → flow returns towards its previous level.
2. **Metabolic autoregulation**: Oxygen delivery falls → vasodilator metabolites accumulate → arterioles widen → flow increases → metabolites are washed away.
3. **Active hyperaemia**: Tissue activity increases → oxygen use and metabolite production rise → arterioles dilate → blood flow increases during activity.
4. **Reactive hyperaemia**: Blood flow is temporarily blocked → oxygen debt and metabolites accumulate → obstruction is removed → flow surges → accumulated metabolites clear.

### Don't confuse
| | **Myogenic control** | **Metabolic control** |
|---|---|---|
| Main signal | Change in transmural pressure and wall stretch | Mismatch between tissue oxygen supply and demand |
| Response to rising pressure or inadequate supply | Stretch triggers smooth-muscle contraction | Metabolite accumulation triggers arteriolar dilation |

| | **Autoregulation** | **Hyperaemia** |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Keep blood flow relatively steady despite a pressure change | Increase blood flow when tissue needs or has been deprived of it |
| Typical trigger | Change in perfusion pressure | Increased activity or release of an obstruction |

| | **Active hyperaemia** | **Reactive hyperaemia** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | While tissue activity is increased | After a temporary interruption of blood flow |
| Cause | Activity-related vasodilator metabolites | Ischaemia-related metabolite accumulation and oxygen debt |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Autoregulation** keeps flow constant at any blood pressure → Truth: It works within limits; fully dilated vessels cannot protect flow indefinitely.
- Trap: **Myogenic constriction** occurs because metabolites accumulate → Truth: Myogenic contraction responds to wall stretch; metabolite accumulation favours dilation.
- Trap: **Reactive hyperaemia** means increased flow during exercise → Truth: That is active hyperaemia; reactive hyperaemia follows temporary obstruction.
- Trap: **Laplace's law** means larger vessels need less wall tension → Truth: At the same transmural pressure, tension rises with radius.
- Trap: **Nitric oxide** constricts arterioles → Truth: Endothelial nitric oxide increases smooth-muscle cGMP and promotes dilation.
- Trap: **Sympathetic activation** stops working-muscle vasodilation → Truth: Local metabolic signals can override constriction in active muscle.


## Chapter 17 — Neural and Hormonal Control

### Must-know
- ★ **Mean arterial pressure** depends mainly on cardiac output and total peripheral resistance → central control can change either to defend systemic pressure.
- **Cardiac output** equals heart rate × stroke volume → changing rate, contractility or venous return can change cardiac output.
- **Central regulation** coordinates the whole circulation, while local control adjusts blood supply to individual tissues.
- ★ **Medullary pressor area** in the upper lateral medulla excites spinal sympathetic neurons → vascular constriction and cardiac stimulation raise pressure.
- **Medullary depressor area** in the lower medial medulla inhibits the pressor area → sympathetic outflow and pressure tend to decrease.
- **Nucleus tractus solitarius** receives cardiovascular sensory input → medullary circuits adjust sympathetic and vagal output.
- ★ **Sympathetic noradrenaline** activates vascular α₁ receptors → arterioles and muscular veins constrict.
- **Resistance-vessel constriction** narrows arterioles → total peripheral resistance rises and blood flow is redistributed.
- **Capacitance-vessel constriction** reduces venous blood storage → venous return, ventricular filling and potential stroke volume increase.
- **Capillaries** lack vascular smooth muscle → they are not directly constricted by sympathetic vasomotor nerves.
- **Tonic sympathetic discharge** keeps arterioles partly constricted at rest → increasing or reducing this firing changes vessel calibre.
- **Reduced vasoconstrictor tone** allows vessels to relax → vasodilation does not usually require activation of separate dilator fibres.
- **Sympathetic cholinergic dilator fibres** can release acetylcholine to skeletal-muscle vessels, although their importance in humans is uncertain.
- ★ **Cardiac sympathetic stimulation** activates β₁ receptors → the heart beats faster, conducts impulses faster and contracts more forcefully.
- **Vagal stimulation** activates cardiac M₂ receptors → sinoatrial firing and atrioventricular conduction slow, with little direct ventricular inotropic effect.
- **Resting vagal tone** suppresses the sinoatrial node's intrinsic rate → the resting pulse is lower than the unrestrained pacemaker rate.
- **Vagotomy or atropine** removes or blocks the vagal brake on the heart → resting heart rate rises.
- **Vagal control of pressure** acts mainly through the heart because most systemic blood vessels have little parasympathetic innervation.
- **Adrenaline at β₁ receptors** increases heart rate and contractility → cardiac output tends to rise.
- **Adrenaline at β₂ receptors** dilates arterioles in skeletal muscle and liver → blood delivery to these beds can increase.
- **Adrenaline at α₁ receptors** constricts vessels, particularly in skin and gut → blood is redirected away from those beds.
- **Angiotensin II** constricts arterioles and promotes aldosterone, ADH and thirst → pressure and circulating volume are supported.
- **Aldosterone** increases renal sodium retention → water follows and circulating volume is supported over hours to days.
- **ADH (vasopressin)** increases renal water retention and can constrict arterioles at high levels → circulating volume and pressure are supported.
- ★ **Atrial natriuretic peptide** is released by stretched atria → vasodilation and renal sodium/water loss help reduce volume overload.
- **Bradykinin** promotes endothelial nitric oxide release → arterioles dilate; **VIP** also dilates vessels.
- **Rapid neural responses** adjust pressure within seconds; circulating hormones reinforce the response over minutes, and kidneys regulate volume over longer periods.
- **Early haemorrhage compensation** raises sympathetic firing and reduces vagal tone → faster beats, stronger contraction and vessel constriction defend pressure.
- **Blood-volume recovery** requires retained or replaced salt and water → nerve-driven vasoconstriction alone cannot replace lost fluid.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Unrestrained sinoatrial node rate | About 100 beats/min | Resting vagal tone normally slows the heart below this rate. |
| Resting heart rate | About 60–80 beats/min | The gap below 100 is the vagal brake (vagal tone). |
| Cardiac output relation | CO = HR × SV | Shows the two direct determinants of cardiac output. |
| Mean arterial pressure relation | MAP ≈ CO × TPR | Links cardiac pumping and arteriolar resistance to arterial pressure. |

### Core chains
1. **Pressor response**: Medullary pressor area becomes more active → spinal sympathetic discharge rises → arterioles and veins constrict → resistance and venous return rise → pressure increases.
2. **Cardiac autonomic control**: Sympathetic β₁ activation rises and vagal M₂ activation falls → heart rate and contraction strength rise → cardiac output tends to increase.
3. **Adrenaline redistribution**: Circulating adrenaline stimulates muscle β₂ receptors and skin/gut α₁ receptors → muscle vessels dilate while skin/gut vessels constrict → flow is redistributed.
4. **Haemorrhage compensation**: Blood volume falls → rapid sympathetic responses defend pressure → angiotensin II, ADH and aldosterone support retention → blood volume recovers as fluid becomes available.

### Don't confuse
| | **Resistance vessels** | **Capacitance vessels** |
|---|---|---|
| Main vessels | Arterioles | Veins |
| Effect of sympathetic constriction | Increases resistance and redirects flow | Reduces blood storage and increases venous return |

| | **Sympathetic cardiac control** | **Vagal cardiac control** |
|---|---|---|
| Main receptor | β₁ | M₂ |
| Effect on rate | Increases sinoatrial firing | Decreases sinoatrial firing |
| Effect on ventricular force | Increases contractility | Little direct effect |

| | **Adrenaline at β₂** | **Noradrenaline at α₁** |
|---|---|---|
| Main vascular effect | Dilates responsive muscle vessels | Constricts arterioles and muscular veins |
| Consequence | Can increase local muscle flow | Raises resistance or mobilises venous blood |

| | **Neural regulation** | **Renal–hormonal regulation** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical speed | Seconds | Minutes to days |
| Main strength | Rapid defence of pressure | Sustained control of sodium, water and volume |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Vasodilation** requires firing of sympathetic dilator nerves → Truth: Most systemic dilation follows reduced tonic constrictor discharge.
- Trap: **Venoconstriction** directly raises total peripheral resistance as its main effect → Truth: It mainly shifts stored blood towards the heart.
- Trap: **Vagotomy** slows the heart → Truth: Removing resting vagal inhibition increases sinoatrial firing and heart rate.
- Trap: **Adrenaline** has one effect on every vessel → Truth: β₂-mediated dilation and α₁-mediated constriction differ by vascular bed.
- Trap: **Parasympathetic nerves** control most arterioles directly → Truth: Sympathetic tone dominates most systemic resistance vessels.
- Trap: **Rapid sympathetic compensation** replaces lost blood → Truth: It supports pressure; sodium/water retention and fluid replacement restore volume.


## Chapter 18 — Cardiovascular Reflexes

### Must-know
- **Cardiovascular reflexes** link a stimulus, receptor, afferent nerve, medullary centre and autonomic output → they rapidly adjust the heart and vessels.
- ★ **Arterial baroreceptors** detect stretch of arterial walls rather than measuring pressure directly → greater stretch increases afferent firing.
- **Carotid sinus baroreceptors** lie near the common carotid bifurcation → their afferents travel through the carotid sinus nerve and glossopharyngeal nerve IX.
- **Aortic arch baroreceptors** monitor stretch in the aortic arch → their afferents travel mainly through vagus nerve X.
- **Nucleus tractus solitarius** receives baroreceptor signals in the medulla → it alters pressor, depressor and vagal activity.
- ★ **Rising arterial pressure** increases baroreceptor firing → vagal activity rises and sympathetic activity falls, reducing cardiac output and vascular resistance.
- **Baroreflex cardiac slowing** follows increased vagal output → heart rate falls; reduced sympathetic drive also lowers ventricular contractility.
- **Baroreflex vasodilation** follows reduced sympathetic constrictor tone → arterioles relax and veins store more blood.
- ★ **Falling arterial pressure** reduces baroreceptor firing → sympathetic output rises and vagal output falls, causing tachycardia and vasoconstriction.
- **Venoconstriction during hypotension** shifts blood towards the heart → venous return and stroke volume are supported.
- **Baroreflex negative feedback** counters rapid pressure disturbances → it is especially useful during sudden standing or blood loss.
- **Chronic baroreceptor resetting** shifts the firing response toward sustained pressure → the reflex cannot alone control long-term hypertension.
- **A functioning baroreflex** does not replace haemorrhaged blood → faster pulse and constriction can coexist with inadequate organ perfusion.
- ★ **Low-pressure volume receptors** in atria and pulmonary vessels detect filling-related stretch rather than high arterial pressure.
- **Type A atrial receptors** fire during atrial systole and signal contraction; type B receptors fire during later filling and signal distension.
- **Increased atrial filling** stimulates type B receptors → signals favour responses that reduce excess circulatory volume.
- **Atrial stretch** can increase heart rate through the Bainbridge reflex → extra venous inflow is pumped onwards more quickly.
- **Bainbridge tachycardia** is weak or variable in humans because arterial baroreceptor influences may outweigh the atrial response.
- **Atrial-volume responses** can reduce ADH and ACTH release; reduced ADH allows greater renal water loss during volume overload.
- **Atrial natriuretic peptide** increases with atrial stretch → renal sodium and water excretion increase, helping relieve volume overload.
- **Atrial-stretch vasodilation** lowers peripheral resistance and may lower pressure even when heart rate increases.
- **Carotid and aortic bodies** are peripheral chemoreceptors → they detect low oxygen, high carbon dioxide and increased H⁺.
- **Chemoreceptor activation** primarily increases breathing and can increase sympathetic vasoconstriction when perfusion is dangerously low.
- **Direct medullary hypoxia or hypercapnia** stimulates the vasomotor centre → sympathetic outflow and systemic arterial pressure rise.
- ★ **Cushing reflex** begins when raised intracranial pressure reduces medullary perfusion → sympathetic hypertension triggers secondary baroreflex bradycardia.
- **Cushing triad** consists of hypertension, bradycardia and irregular breathing → it signals dangerous brainstem compromise.
- **Tumour, blood clot or hydrocephalus** can raise intracranial pressure → reduced cerebral perfusion may trigger the Cushing response.
- **Anger, excitement and brief sharp pain** can increase medullary sympathetic drive through higher centres → arterial pressure rises.
- **Fear, grief or prolonged severe pain** can produce vasodilation and bradycardia → pressure falls and fainting may occur.
- **Lung inflation** sends inhibitory vagal afferent signals to the vasomotor centre → vasodilation can lower blood pressure.
- **Opposite heart-rate patterns** arise because haemorrhage decreases baroreceptor firing, whereas Cushing hypertension increases baroreceptor firing.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Carotid sinus sensory nerve | IX | Glossopharyngeal nerve carries carotid sinus pressure signals to the medulla. |
| Aortic arch sensory nerve | X | Vagus nerve carries aortic arch pressure signals to the medulla. |

### Core chains
1. **High-pressure baroreflex**: Blood pressure rises → carotid and aortic walls stretch → baroreceptor firing rises → vagal output increases and sympathetic output falls → heart rate and resistance decrease.
2. **Low-pressure baroreflex**: Blood pressure falls → arterial stretch and afferent firing fall → pressor output rises and vagal drive falls → heart and vessels increase pressure towards normal.
3. **Atrial volume reflex**: Venous filling rises → atrial type B receptors discharge → ADH falls and ANP rises → sodium/water excretion increases; Bainbridge tachycardia may occur.
4. **Cushing reflex**: Intracranial pressure rises → medullary blood flow falls → local hypoxia and CO₂ rise → sympathetic hypertension develops → arterial baroreceptors trigger bradycardia.

### Don't confuse
| | **Arterial baroreceptors** | **Atrial volume receptors** |
|---|---|---|
| Main location | Carotid sinus and aortic arch | Atria and pulmonary vessels |
| Main stimulus | Arterial-wall stretch | Filling-related stretch |
| Key response | Buffers sudden arterial pressure change | Helps adjust excess circulating volume |

| | **Carotid sinus** | **Carotid body** |
|---|---|---|
| Receptor | Baroreceptor | Chemoreceptor |
| Detects | Arterial-wall stretch | Low O₂, high CO₂ and increased H⁺ |

| | **Baroreflex after haemorrhage** | **Cushing reflex** |
|---|---|---|
| Initial problem | Arterial pressure falls | Intracranial pressure compromises medullary flow |
| Heart-rate response | Tachycardia from reduced baroreceptor firing | Bradycardia after sympathetically driven hypertension |

| | **Type A atrial receptor** | **Type B atrial receptor** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | During atrial contraction | During later atrial filling |
| Main information | Atrial contraction | Atrial filling and distension |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Baroreceptors** directly sense blood chemistry → Truth: They sense arterial stretch; carotid and aortic bodies sense blood gases and H⁺.
- Trap: **Higher blood pressure** increases sympathetic output through the normal baroreflex → Truth: Increased stretch reduces sympathetic output and increases vagal activity.
- Trap: **A rapid pulse after haemorrhage** means perfusion is restored → Truth: Tachycardia is compensation; circulating volume may remain severely reduced.
- Trap: **Atrial stretch** necessarily slows the heart → Truth: Bainbridge signalling can increase rate, although the effect is variable in humans.
- Trap: **Cushing bradycardia** means sympathetic drive is low → Truth: Medullary ischaemia first raises sympathetic pressure; the arterial baroreflex then slows the heart.
- Trap: **Cushing hypertension** guarantees adequate brain blood flow → Truth: It compensates for dangerous cerebral underperfusion caused by high intracranial pressure.
- Trap: **Pain** always raises blood pressure → Truth: Brief sharp pain raises it, but prolonged severe pain can lower it and cause fainting.
