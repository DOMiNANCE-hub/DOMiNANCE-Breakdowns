## Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier

### Must-know
- ★ **Continuous cerebral blood flow** is essential because the brain stores almost no oxygen or glucose; a short interruption rapidly causes unconsciousness.
- **High cerebral oxygen demand** makes the brain vulnerable to hypoxia because it uses a large share of cardiac output despite low body mass.
- **Hypoxic sensitivity** is greatest in cortex, basal ganglia and thalamus; brainstem functions are relatively more resistant.
- **Glucose** provides most cerebral energy because the brain depends on circulating fuel and takes up glucose without insulin.
- **Hypoglycaemia** may produce convulsions or coma because neurons cannot sustain energy-dependent electrical activity without sufficient substrate.
- **Prolonged starvation** allows some cerebral energy to come from ketone bodies made in the liver, reducing dependence on glucose.
- **Internal carotid arteries** mainly supply anterior and middle cerebral territories, including much of the cerebral hemispheres.
- **Vertebral arteries** unite into the basilar artery → posterior brain regions and brainstem receive a separate arterial inflow.
- **Circle of Willis** joins carotid and vertebrobasilar circulations → collateral supply may compensate when an inflow artery narrows slowly.
- **Limited distal anastomoses** leave cerebral territories vulnerable because arteries beyond the Circle of Willis have few effective alternative pathways.
- **Anterior cerebral artery** supplies much of the medial frontal/parietal surface → obstruction particularly affects the opposite leg.
- **Middle cerebral artery** supplies the lateral hemisphere → blockage often impairs opposite face/arm and dominant-hemisphere speech.
- **Posterior cerebral artery** supplies the occipital cortex → obstruction can cause visual disturbance.
- ★ **Cerebral autoregulation** constricts arterioles when perfusion pressure rises and dilates them when pressure falls → flow stays relatively steady within limits.
- **Increased cerebral CO₂** raises local H⁺ → cerebral arterioles dilate and blood flow increases.
- **Hyperventilation** lowers arterial CO₂ → cerebral arterioles constrict and blood flow may fall enough to cause dizziness.
- **Severe cerebral hypoxia** dilates cerebral arterioles because inadequate oxygen delivery demands increased blood flow.
- **Neurovascular coupling** increases flow to active brain regions because local metabolic activity generates vasodilator signals.
- **Cerebral innervation** includes adrenergic, cholinergic and sensory nerves, but local chemical regulation dominates flow control.
- **Pain-sensitive cerebral vessels and meninges** can generate headache because sensory nerves innervate them, unlike most brain tissue.
- ★ **Monro–Kellie doctrine** states brain, intracranial blood and CSF share a rigid skull → added volume ultimately raises intracranial pressure.
- **Compensatory intracranial shifts** initially move CSF and venous blood out → small volume increases cause limited pressure rises.
- **Exhausted intracranial reserve** makes pressure rise steeply → cerebral vessels are compressed and flow becomes endangered.
- **Cerebral perfusion pressure** equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure → rising ICP can reduce brain perfusion despite unchanged arm BP.
- **Cushing reflex** begins with severe brainstem ischaemia → sympathetic vasoconstriction raises BP, and baroreceptors subsequently slow the heart.
- **Cushing triad** combines hypertension, bradycardia and irregular breathing → a late warning of critically raised intracranial pressure.
- **Dura mater** forms the tough outer meningeal covering; arachnoid mater lies beneath it and pia follows the brain surface.
- **Subarachnoid space** contains CSF and cerebral vessels between arachnoid and pia; normal subdural space is only potential.
- **Choroid plexus** produces most CSF in the cerebral ventricles → continuous flow replaces CSF several times daily.
- ★ **CSF flow** follows lateral ventricles → Monro → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → Magendie/Luschka → subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi.
- **Arachnoid villi** absorb CSF into dural venous sinuses → waste and water return to the bloodstream.
- **CSF cushioning** protects brain tissue from impacts because fluid and arachnoid trabeculae reduce movement-related injury.
- **CSF buoyancy** markedly reduces the brain's effective weight → pressure on its base is much smaller than in air.
- **CSF waste removal** carries metabolites and some drugs into venous blood through continuous fluid turnover.
- **CSF hormone transport** permits substances released into the fluid to reach distant brain regions.
- **Non-communicating hydrocephalus** follows obstruction inside the ventricular pathway → CSF accumulates upstream and ventricles enlarge.
- **Communicating hydrocephalus** follows impaired arachnoid-villus absorption despite open communication between ventricles and subarachnoid space.
- **Lumbar puncture** samples CSF from the lower spinal subarachnoid space below the spinal cord's end.
- **Lumbar puncture hazard** arises when raised ICP from a mass creates a dangerous pressure gradient → brain herniation may follow fluid removal.
- ★ **Blood–brain barrier** is formed mainly by tight junctions between brain capillary endothelial cells → most blood chemicals cannot pass freely.
- **Astrocyte endfeet and pericytes** help maintain the cerebral endothelial barrier and stable extracellular conditions for neurons.
- **Lipid-soluble substances and gases** cross the BBB comparatively easily because they diffuse through endothelial cell membranes.
- **Glucose entry to brain** requires GLUT1 transporters because glucose cannot freely cross endothelial tight junctions.
- **Plasma proteins and bound drugs** cross the BBB poorly → circulating substances may not reach neuronal tissue.
- **BBB disruption** in infection, inflammation, injury or tumours increases entry of normally excluded blood substances.
- **Choroid-plexus blood–CSF barrier** is chiefly epithelial rather than endothelial → it differs anatomically from the BBB.
- **Ischaemic stroke** results from thrombus, embolus or atherosclerotic blockage → downstream brain tissue loses blood and may die.
- **Haemorrhagic stroke** results from arterial rupture, sometimes an aneurysm → bleeding injures tissue and can raise ICP.

### Core chains
1. **Autoregulation and CO₂**: Cerebral perfusion pressure changes → arterioles constrict or dilate to stabilise flow; if local CO₂ increases → H⁺ rises → cerebral arterioles dilate → flow increases.
2. **Raised intracranial pressure**: Haemorrhage or swelling adds volume inside rigid skull → CSF/venous reserve is exhausted → ICP rises → CPP falls → medullary ischaemia triggers hypertension → baroreflex bradycardia.
3. **CSF pathway**: Choroid plexus secretes fluid into lateral ventricles → Monro → third ventricle → Sylvius → fourth ventricle → Magendie/Luschka → subarachnoid space → arachnoid-villus absorption.
4. **Cerebral stroke**: Cerebral artery occlusion or rupture → oxygen delivery falls or local bleeding increases → neuronal dysfunction and injury → focal neurological deficit corresponding to the affected artery.

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Brain oxygen stores** can sustain several minutes of flow interruption → Truth: The brain stores very little oxygen and unconsciousness follows rapidly.
- Trap: **Circle of Willis** guarantees protection from every cerebral artery blockage → Truth: The ring may be incomplete and distal arteries have few anastomoses.
- Trap: **Hyperventilation** raises cerebral flow by adding oxygen → Truth: Falling CO₂ constricts cerebral arterioles and can cause dizziness.
- Trap: **A normal arm pressure** ensures cerebral perfusion → Truth: High intracranial pressure reduces CPP even when MAP is unchanged.
- Trap: **Cushing bradycardia** means the brain initially lowered sympathetic output → Truth: Severe ICP triggers sympathetic hypertension, followed by baroreflex slowing.
- Trap: **CSF** normally fills the subdural space → Truth: CSF occupies the subarachnoid space; normal subdural space is potential.
- Trap: **Communicating hydrocephalus** means a blocked cerebral aqueduct → Truth: Open ventricular pathways with reduced absorption define communicating disease.
- Trap: **Glucose** crosses the BBB by unrestricted diffusion → Truth: It uses a carrier, chiefly GLUT1.
- Trap: **Every stroke** is a thrombotic blockage → Truth: Haemorrhagic stroke follows vessel rupture and may increase ICP.

## Coronary Circulation

### Must-know
- ★ **Separate coronary arteries** nourish thick myocardium because oxygen cannot diffuse sufficiently from blood inside the heart chambers.
- **Aortic sinuses** give rise to right and left coronary arteries immediately above the aortic valve cusps.
- ★ **High resting oxygen extraction** means myocardium must increase coronary flow when work rises because little additional extraction reserve remains.
- **Left anterior descending artery** supplies anterior left ventricle and much of the interventricular septum → its obstruction threatens these regions.
- **Left circumflex artery** chiefly supplies the lateral left ventricular wall.
- **Right coronary artery** mainly supplies the right ventricle and commonly the inferior ventricular wall.
- **Coronary dominance** is defined by the origin of the posterior descending artery, not which vessel supplies most of the heart.
- **Coronary venous drainage** mostly enters the coronary sinus and then right atrium; some veins drain directly into chambers.
- ★ **Left ventricular systole** compresses intramyocardial coronary vessels → left ventricular blood flow becomes greatest during diastole.
- **Right ventricular coronary flow** occurs in both systole and diastole because right ventricular contraction compresses vessels less strongly.
- **Left coronary driving pressure** is approximately aortic diastolic minus left ventricular diastolic pressure → low aortic pressure reduces coronary perfusion.
- **High left ventricular filling pressure** reduces coronary driving pressure because it narrows the diastolic pressure gradient across the ventricular wall.
- **Subendocardium** is most prone to ischaemia because high cavity pressure compresses its vessels strongly during contraction.
- **Aortic stenosis** raises left ventricular pressure and oxygen demand → subendocardial vessels are further compressed.
- **Myocardial oxygen demand** rises with heart rate, contractility and wall tension → coronary delivery must increase proportionately.
- ★ **Coronary active hyperaemia** follows myocardial activity → adenosine and other metabolites dilate coronary arterioles and increase flow.
- **Low O₂, K⁺ and endothelial nitric oxide** contribute to metabolic coronary vasodilatation as myocardial oxygen demand rises.
- **Direct sympathetic α stimulation** constricts coronary vessels, but this does not usually determine the net exercise response.
- **Sympathetic β₁ stimulation** increases cardiac work → greater metabolite production produces net coronary vasodilatation.
- **Tachycardia** increases myocardial oxygen demand and shortens diastole → both changes favour coronary ischaemia.
- **Diastolic shortening** reduces the time for left ventricular coronary filling, especially when coronary arteries are narrowed.
- ★ **Coronary flow reserve** is the ability to increase flow above resting levels by further arteriolar dilatation.
- **Fixed coronary narrowing** causes distal arterioles to dilate even at rest → less reserve remains available during exercise.
- **Exertional angina** occurs when myocardial demand exceeds limited coronary supply → temporary ischaemia causes chest pain without irreversible cell death.
- **Myocardial infarction** follows prolonged severe ischaemia → cardiac muscle cells die rather than simply becoming temporarily underperfused.
- **Plaque rupture and thrombosis** may suddenly obstruct coronary flow → myocardial infarction can occur despite previously compensated narrowing.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Myocardial oxygen extraction at rest | About 70% | Increased demand normally requires more flow rather than much more extraction. |
| Typical coronary flow reserve | About 4–5 times resting flow | Healthy coronary arterioles can markedly increase supply. |

### Core chains
1. **Diastolic coronary perfusion**: Ventricular systole compresses left myocardial vessels → intramuscular flow falls → ventricular relaxation releases compression → aortic diastolic pressure drives coronary blood into the left ventricle.
2. **Active coronary hyperaemia**: Heart rate or contractility increases → oxygen demand and ATP turnover increase → adenosine and local vasodilators accumulate → coronary arterioles widen → myocardial blood flow rises.
3. **Angina from narrowed artery**: Coronary lumen narrows → downstream arterioles dilate at rest to preserve baseline flow → reserve becomes limited → exercise raises demand → ischaemic angina appears.
4. **Tachycardia and infarction risk**: Faster heart → oxygen demand rises while diastole shortens → supply/demand mismatch worsens → prolonged severe occlusion can cause irreversible myocardial death.

### Don't confuse
| | **Left ventricular coronary flow** | **Right ventricular coronary flow** |
|---|---|---|
| Main perfusion time | Predominantly diastole. | Both systole and diastole. |
| Reason | High LV contraction pressure compresses vessels. | Lower RV contraction pressure causes less compression. |

| | **Angina pectoris** | **Myocardial infarction** |
|---|---|---|
| Injury | Temporary, reversible myocardial ischaemia. | Irreversible death of myocardial cells. |
| Common trigger | Exertional demand exceeds restricted reserve. | Prolonged severe obstruction or thrombosis. |

| | **Coronary α effect** | **Coronary β₁-mediated effect** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Direct vasoconstriction of coronary vessels. | Greater cardiac work increases metabolic vasodilator release. |
| Exercise net effect | Usually outweighed by metabolic signals. | Net coronary flow normally increases. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Coronary arteries** are filled only during aortic valve opening → Truth: Their ostia remain patent, and LV perfusion is strongest during diastole.
- Trap: **Coronary dominance** means the dominant artery supplies the entire heart → Truth: It identifies which coronary artery gives the posterior descending branch.
- Trap: **More myocardial work** is met mainly by greater oxygen extraction → Truth: The heart already extracts much oxygen; coronary flow must increase.
- Trap: **Faster heart rate** always improves myocardial oxygen supply → Truth: Tachycardia shortens diastolic perfusion and increases oxygen demand.
- Trap: **Direct α vasoconstriction** means exercise must reduce coronary flow → Truth: Metabolic dilation from increased work usually dominates.
- Trap: **Angina** necessarily means permanent muscle death → Truth: Angina is transient ischaemia; sustained severe ischaemia causes infarction.
- Trap: **Subendocardium** is safest because it lies near chamber blood → Truth: Wall compression makes it especially vulnerable to reduced coronary supply.

## Pulmonary Circulation

### Must-know
- ★ **Pulmonary circulation** carries right ventricular blood through lung capillaries for gas exchange → pulmonary veins return it to the left atrium.
- **Bronchial circulation** arises from the aorta and nourishes conducting airways and other supporting lung tissues.
- **Bronchial venous mixing** slightly reduces arterial oxygenation because some deoxygenated bronchial drainage enters pulmonary veins.
- ★ **Equal ventricular outputs** flow through pulmonary and systemic circuits in series, but the pulmonary circuit needs a much lower pressure gradient.
- **Low pulmonary vascular resistance** results from short, wide, compliant vessels and many parallel capillary pathways.
- **Pulmonary recruitment** opens previously unperfused capillaries as output increases → total circuit resistance falls.
- **Pulmonary distension** widens already perfused vessels during exercise → increased flow causes only a small pressure rise.
- **Low pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure** limits outward filtration and helps protect the thin alveolar gas-exchange surface.
- **Plasma oncotic pressure** opposes fluid leaving pulmonary capillaries → lymphatics can remove the small normal filtrate.
- **Pulmonary oedema in left failure** develops when high left atrial pressure raises lung capillary pressure → filtration overwhelms lymphatic drainage.
- **Alveolar flooding** impairs oxygen transfer because fluid increases the diffusion barrier between air and blood.
- ★ **Gravity in upright lungs** increases vascular pressure and perfusion towards the bases → apical blood flow remains lower.
- **West zone 1** has alveolar pressure above arterial and venous pressure → compressed capillaries may carry no blood.
- **West zone 2** has arterial pressure above alveolar pressure but venous pressure below it → flow depends on arterial-to-alveolar pressure.
- **West zone 3** has arterial and venous pressures above alveolar pressure → capillaries stay open and flow continuously.
- **West zones** are changing pressure relationships, not permanently fixed lung regions; posture and blood pressure can move their boundaries.
- **Supine posture** reduces top-to-bottom pressure differences → pulmonary flow spreads more evenly than during upright posture.
- **Pulmonary blood reservoir** expands when lying flat because blood redistributes from lower-body systemic veins into lung vessels.
- **Orthopnoea** worsens in left failure because lying flat increases lung blood volume and pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure.
- ★ **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** narrows vessels serving poorly ventilated alveoli → blood is redirected towards better ventilated units.
- **Hypoxic pulmonary constriction** differs from systemic arteriolar control because low oxygen usually dilates systemic resistance vessels.
- **Raised local pulmonary CO₂/H⁺** promotes vasoconstriction → perfusion shifts away from poorly ventilated alveoli.
- **Generalised alveolar hypoxia** constricts vessels throughout both lungs → total resistance and pulmonary arterial pressure rise.
- **Persistent pulmonary vasoconstriction** may cause pulmonary hypertension and increased right ventricular workload.
- **Pulmonary nitric oxide** relaxes vascular smooth muscle → local pulmonary vessels dilate.
- **Pulmonary sympathetic stimulation** can constrict lung vessels, although local oxygen control strongly affects regional distribution.

| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Pulmonary arterial pressure | About 25/8 mmHg; mean 15 mmHg | Far lower than systemic pressure despite similar flow. |
| Systemic arterial pressure for comparison | About 120/80 mmHg; mean 93 mmHg | Illustrates the much larger systemic driving pressure. |
| Pulmonary vascular resistance relative to systemic | About one-tenth | Supports right ventricular output with low pressure. |
| Resting cardiac output through either circuit | About 5 L/min | Right and left ventricles pump equal volumes in series. |
| Pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure | About 10 mmHg | Low outward fluid pressure normally limits oedema. |
| Plasma oncotic pressure | About 25 mmHg | Helps retain fluid inside pulmonary capillaries. |
| Pulmonary capillary pressure linked with oedema | Above about 25 mmHg | High pressure can overwhelm lymphatic clearance. |
| Blood shifted into lungs by lying down | About 400 mL | Explains increased congestion in left heart failure. |

### Core chains
1. **Exercise adaptation**: Cardiac output increases → pulmonary capillaries recruit and distend → vascular resistance falls → much more blood passes with only a modest rise in pressure.
2. **Pulmonary oedema**: Left ventricle fails → left atrial and pulmonary venous pressure rise → capillary hydrostatic pressure increases → fluid filtration exceeds lymphatic removal → alveoli flood.
3. **Local hypoxia**: One alveolar region is poorly ventilated → alveolar oxygen falls → supplying pulmonary arterioles constrict → blood moves to better ventilated regions → ventilation/perfusion matching improves.
4. **Global hypoxia**: Many alveoli become hypoxic → widespread pulmonary vasoconstriction → resistance and pulmonary pressure rise → right ventricular workload increases.

### Don't confuse
| | **Pulmonary circulation** | **Bronchial circulation** |
|---|---|---|
| Origin and role | Right ventricle; gas exchange. | Aortic branches; nourish airways. |
| Pressure | Low pressure. | Systemic arterial pressure. |
| Drainage | Pulmonary veins to left atrium. | Partly drains into pulmonary veins. |

| | **Systemic response to hypoxia** | **Pulmonary response to hypoxia** |
|---|---|---|
| Vascular change | Arterioles usually dilate. | Small pulmonary arteries constrict. |
| Purpose | Deliver more oxygen to oxygen-starved tissues. | Divert flow away from poorly ventilated alveoli. |

| | **Recruitment** | **Distension** |
|---|---|---|
| Vascular change | Previously closed/underperfused capillaries open. | Already open capillaries widen. |
| Effect | More parallel pathways reduce resistance. | Larger vessel calibre reduces resistance. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Pulmonary pressure** should equal systemic pressure because flows are equal → Truth: Lower pulmonary resistance permits equal flow at much lower pressure.
- Trap: **Exercise** must greatly raise pulmonary pressure → Truth: Recruitment and distension accommodate more flow with modest pressure change.
- Trap: **Most pulmonary blood flow** occurs at the apex while upright → Truth: Gravity normally favours perfusion at the lung bases.
- Trap: **Zone 1** is present in every healthy lung → Truth: Alveolar pressure exceeding arterial pressure generally requires low pressure or positive-pressure ventilation.
- Trap: **Lying flat** eases left-failure breathlessness → Truth: More blood enters lung vessels and worsens pulmonary congestion.
- Trap: **Low alveolar oxygen** dilates pulmonary arteries as in other organs → Truth: Pulmonary vasoconstriction diverts blood towards better ventilated alveoli.
- Trap: **Generalised hypoxia** improves pulmonary distribution without cost → Truth: Widespread constriction raises pressure and strains the right ventricle.

## Splanchnic and Hepatic Circulation

### Must-know
- **Coeliac trunk** supplies upper abdominal digestive territories as one of three unpaired aortic branches serving the gut.
- **Superior and inferior mesenteric arteries** supply most remaining intestinal territories from the abdominal aorta.
- ★ **Hepatic portal route** sends gut venous blood through the liver before systemic return so absorbed nutrients, drugs and toxins are processed.
- **Two capillary beds** lie in series along the portal pathway: intestinal capillaries followed by hepatic sinusoids.
- **Portal vein formation** involves superior mesenteric and splenic veins → venous blood from gut/spleen enters the liver.
- ★ **Dual hepatic inflow** supplies blood through portal vein and hepatic artery, mixing in liver sinusoids before venous drainage.
- **Portal venous blood** delivers nutrient-rich but partly deoxygenated blood from intestine to the liver.
- **Hepatic arterial blood** supplies oxygen-rich blood to liver tissue despite contributing less of its total flow.
- **Portal triad** contains branches of portal vein, hepatic artery and bile duct → incoming blood and outgoing bile meet anatomically.
- **Liver sinusoidal flow** moves from portal-triad branches towards central veins → hepatic veins drain into inferior vena cava.
- **Bile flow** travels opposite sinusoidal blood, towards bile ducts near portal triads.
- ★ **Zone 1 hepatocytes** lie near portal triads and receive the most oxygenated sinusoidal inflow.
- **Zone 3 hepatocytes** lie near central veins and receive less oxygen → hypoperfusion injures them first.
- **Space of Disse** lies between liver sinusoids and hepatocytes → filtered fluid can become hepatic lymph.
- **Postprandial hyperaemia** increases intestinal blood flow after meals because greater digestive activity produces local metabolic vasodilatation.
- **Large splanchnic venous reservoir** stores blood in compliant veins → sympathetic constriction can mobilise it during haemorrhage.
- **Splanchnic sympathetic vasoconstriction** redirects blood towards heart and brain during shock, but prolonged low gut flow risks ischaemia.
- **Intestinal mucosal flow** is prominent because absorption and metabolism create substantial local perfusion demand.
- **Ascites** means fluid accumulation within the peritoneal cavity from excessive filtration and inadequate fluid clearance.
- ★ **Portal hypertension** raises hepatic sinusoidal hydrostatic pressure → excessive fluid filtration and lymph production promote ascites.
- **Hepatic lymph overflow** allows fluid to enter the abdomen when leaky sinusoids produce more lymph than drainage can handle.
- **Low plasma albumin** reduces vascular oncotic pressure → fluid is less effectively retained inside capillaries.
- **Renal sodium/water retention** adds circulating fluid → persistent portal hypertension can worsen ascitic accumulation.
- **Right heart failure** raises hepatic venous pressure backwards → liver congestion can also cause ascites.
- **Ascitic fluid protein** varies by cause; porous liver sinusoids do not mean every form of ascites has high protein.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Main gut arteries from aorta | 3 | Coeliac trunk plus superior and inferior mesenteric arteries. |
| Portal vein share of hepatic blood flow | About 75% | Major venous delivery of gut-derived nutrients. |
| Hepatic artery share of hepatic blood flow | About 25% | Smaller-volume supply contributes around half the oxygen. |
| Hepatic arterial oxygen contribution | About 50% | Oxygen delivery cannot be inferred from blood-flow share alone. |
| Duration of increased intestinal flow after a meal | About 3 hours (lecture slide) | Represents sustained digestive hyperaemia after eating. |

### Core chains
1. **Hepatic portal flow**: Coeliac/mesenteric inflow perfuses gut → venous blood enters portal vein → hepatic sinusoids process absorbed substances → central veins → hepatic veins → inferior vena cava.
2. **Hepatic oxygen gradient**: Portal and hepatic arterial blood mix near portal triads → blood loses oxygen travelling towards central veins → zone 3 receives less oxygen → zone 3 is injured first during hypoperfusion.
3. **Haemorrhage mobilisation**: Blood volume falls → sympathetic drive constricts splanchnic vessels and veins → stored blood returns centrally → heart/brain perfusion is prioritised while gut ischaemia risk rises.
4. **Ascites**: Portal or hepatic venous pressure increases → sinusoidal filtration and hepatic lymph rise → lymphatic drainage is overwhelmed → fluid enters the peritoneum; low albumin and renal retention worsen it.

### Don't confuse
| | **Portal vein** | **Hepatic artery** |
|---|---|---|
| Blood type | Nutrient-rich, partly deoxygenated venous blood. | Oxygen-rich arterial blood. |
| Approximate liver flow share | 75%. | 25%. |
| Role | Sends absorbed gut products through liver processing. | Contributes substantially to liver oxygen delivery. |

| | **Zone 1 near portal triad** | **Zone 3 near central vein** |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen supply | Higher, because blood has just entered sinusoids. | Lower, after upstream hepatocytes have extracted oxygen. |
| Ischaemic injury | Relatively better protected. | Especially vulnerable during shock. |

| | **Portal hypertension in cirrhosis** | **Hepatic congestion in right failure** |
|---|---|---|
| Main pressure origin | Resistance to portal flow within scarred liver. | High systemic venous pressure backs into hepatic veins. |
| Shared result | Greater sinusoidal filtration and ascites. | Greater sinusoidal filtration and ascites. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Portal venous blood** returns directly from gut to the heart → Truth: It passes through liver sinusoids first.
- Trap: **Liver oxygen delivery** follows its blood-flow shares exactly → Truth: The hepatic artery supplies a disproportionate share of oxygen.
- Trap: **Zone 3** is safest because it lies near central vein outflow → Truth: It receives the least oxygenated sinusoidal blood.
- Trap: **Bile** flows in the same direction as sinusoidal blood → Truth: Blood moves towards central veins while bile moves towards portal triads.
- Trap: **Haemorrhage-related constriction** cannot harm gut because it supports pressure → Truth: Prolonged severe reduction in gut flow can cause ischaemia.
- Trap: **Ascites** is always caused by low albumin alone → Truth: High sinusoidal pressure, lymph overflow and renal retention also contribute.
- Trap: **Ascitic protein** is invariably high due to fenestrated hepatic sinusoids → Truth: Protein level depends on the underlying cause.

## Cutaneous Circulation and the Triple Response

### Must-know
- ★ **Cutaneous blood flow** is strongly regulated for heat exchange, so flow can exceed the skin's nutritional requirements.
- **Heat exposure** widens skin vessels → more warm blood approaches the surface and heat loss increases.
- **Cold exposure** increases sympathetic skin vasoconstriction → superficial flow falls and body heat is conserved.
- **Arteriovenous anastomoses** provide shortcuts between small skin arteries and veins → warm blood can rapidly reach superficial vessels.
- **Arteriovenous shunts** are prominent in fingers, toes, palms, ears and nose, where sympathetic control adjusts heat transfer.
- ★ **Haemorrhagic shock** increases sympathetic skin vasoconstriction → pale, cold skin appears as flow is diverted to heart and brain.
- **Early septic shock** can cause warm flushed skin because circulating mediators dilate cutaneous vessels.
- **Skin temperature alone** cannot determine shock type because peripheral flow varies with vascular tone and systemic state.
- ★ **White skin reaction** after a light stroke is brief pallor from local constriction; it is separate from Lewis's triple response.
- **Red line** after a firm skin stroke comes from local capillary dilatation along the point of contact.
- **Flare** is spreading redness around a stroke because sensory-nerve branches trigger nearby arteriolar dilation.
- **Wheal** is a raised swelling because histamine increases local capillary and postcapillary venular permeability → fluid enters tissue.
- ★ **Lewis triple response** proceeds through red line → spreading flare → raised wheal after firm mechanical stimulation.
- **Sensory axon branching** spreads signals from the stimulated branch into neighbouring branches without needing a spinal-cord synapse.
- **Antidromic conduction** describes an impulse travelling back along collateral sensory nerve branches towards peripheral endings.
- **Substance P and CGRP** released from peripheral sensory endings dilate local arterioles → redness spreads beyond the stroked line.
- **Mast-cell histamine** promotes local vasodilatation and capillary leak → the wheal develops near the injured area.
- ★ **Axon reflex** generates the flare entirely through branches of one sensory fibre, rather than a conventional central reflex arc.
- **Local anaesthesia** abolishes the flare by blocking sensory conduction, while purely local red-line and wheal components can remain.
- **Sensory nerve degeneration** removes the flare because an intact peripheral axon is needed for antidromic signalling.
- **Nerve interruption above the branch point** may preserve flare because the reflex does not require transmission to the spinal cord.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Approximate white-line duration (lecture) | About 15 seconds | Short-lived constriction from a light skin stroke. |
| Components of Lewis's triple response | 3 | Red line, flare and wheal are distinct local mechanisms. |

### Core chains
1. **Thermoregulatory vasodilatation**: Skin warms → superficial arterioles/arteriovenous shunts open → warm blood reaches skin surface → body heat is lost.
2. **Haemorrhage-related pallor**: Arterial volume falls → sympathetic discharge increases → cutaneous vessels constrict → skin flow decreases → skin becomes cold and pale.
3. **Axon-reflex flare**: Firm stroke stimulates a sensory branch → impulses enter collateral branches antidromically → substance P/CGRP are released → surrounding arterioles dilate → flare spreads.
4. **Wheal formation**: Firm stroke triggers local inflammatory mediator release → histamine increases small-vessel permeability → plasma fluid enters the dermis → raised swelling develops.

### Don't confuse
| | **White line** | **Red line** |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | Light mechanical stroke. | Firmer stroke. |
| Vascular response | Local constriction and transient pallor. | Local capillary dilation and redness. |
| Relation to triple response | Not part of it. | First component. |

| | **Flare** | **Wheal** |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Spreading red area. | Local raised swelling. |
| Main mechanism | Sensory axon reflex dilates arterioles. | Histamine increases capillary permeability. |
| Dependence on sensory nerve | Requires intact peripheral axon branches. | Primarily a local permeability response. |

| | **Axon reflex** | **Central reflex arc** |
|---|---|---|
| Neural pathway | Peripheral branches of one sensory neuron. | Afferent signals enter the CNS and return by efferents. |
| Spinal cord needed | No. | Yes for a spinal reflex. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Skin flow** is controlled chiefly by nutritional demand → Truth: Its large changes primarily regulate heat exchange.
- Trap: **Cold shock skin** proves every tissue has low flow equally → Truth: Sympathetic redistribution preserves vital organs relatively.
- Trap: **Early septic shock** must produce cold pale skin → Truth: Peripheral vasodilatation can make skin warm.
- Trap: **The white line** is one of Lewis's three reactions → Truth: The triple response is red line, flare and wheal.
- Trap: **Flare** is caused by histamine leakage alone → Truth: It spreads through an axon reflex that dilates nearby arterioles.
- Trap: **Axon reflex** needs an impulse to reach the spinal cord → Truth: Collateral branches within the peripheral nerve fibre carry the response.
- Trap: **Wheal** means an area of arteriolar constriction → Truth: Increased vascular permeability allows oedema to form.

## Fetal Circulation and the Transition at Birth

### Must-know
- ★ **Placenta** performs fetal gas exchange because the unborn lungs are fluid-filled and do not yet ventilate air.
- **High fetal pulmonary resistance** keeps most right ventricular output from flowing through the unventilated lungs.
- **Low placental vascular resistance** provides a large low-resistance circuit before birth → systemic resistance is comparatively low.
- ★ **Single umbilical vein** brings oxygenated placental blood towards the fetal liver and inferior vena cava.
- **Ductus venosus** channels much umbilical venous blood towards the inferior vena cava → most of that stream bypasses liver sinusoids.
- **IVC blood streaming** directs comparatively oxygen-rich blood towards the foramen ovale in the fetal right atrium.
- **Foramen ovale** passes blood from right atrium to left atrium → oxygen-rich inflow can reach left ventricle and upper aorta.
- **Best-oxygenated fetal blood** preferentially supplies the heart and brain through left-heart outflow from the foramen ovale.
- **Superior vena cava blood** preferentially enters right ventricle → pulmonary trunk receives relatively oxygen-poor blood.
- **Ductus arteriosus** diverts pulmonary trunk blood into descending aorta → most blood bypasses high-resistance fetal lungs.
- **Two umbilical arteries** return fetal blood from internal iliac branches to the placenta for reoxygenation.
- ★ **Right-to-left fetal shunting** occurs because high lung resistance and low placental resistance favour right-sided pressures before birth.
- **Fetal haemoglobin (HbF)** binds oxygen more tightly than adult HbA because its interaction with 2,3-BPG is weaker.
- **Reduced HbF binding to 2,3-BPG** shifts oxygen affinity upward → oxygen can be transferred from maternal haemoglobin at placental exchange surfaces.
- **Placental oxygen saturation** is below adult arterial levels, making HbF's higher oxygen affinity especially valuable.
- **HbF replacement** occurs gradually after birth as adult haemoglobin synthesis increases over the following months.
- ★ **First newborn breaths** expand oxygenated lungs → pulmonary vessels dilate, pulmonary resistance falls and pulmonary venous return increases.
- **Cord clamping** removes the low-resistance placenta → systemic vascular resistance rises and umbilical venous flow stops.
- **Left atrial pressure rise** after breathing exceeds right atrial pressure → the foramen ovale flap closes functionally.
- **Higher postnatal oxygen and lower prostaglandins** cause ductus arteriosus constriction → fetal pulmonary bypass closes.
- **Stopped umbilical flow** promotes ductus venosus closure because its placental blood source has been removed.
- **Functional closure** changes flow within minutes to days, whereas anatomical fibrous closure develops over weeks to months.
- **Fossa ovalis** is the adult remnant of the foramen ovale after atrial septal flap fusion.
- **Ligamentum arteriosum** is the fibrous remnant of the ductus arteriosus joining pulmonary trunk and aortic regions.
- **Ligamentum venosum** marks the former ductus venosus within the liver.
- **Ligamentum teres hepatis** is the remnant of the fetal umbilical vein.
- **Medial umbilical ligaments** are remnants of the distal umbilical arteries after placental flow ceases.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Fetal umbilical vein | 1 | Returns comparatively oxygen-rich blood from placenta. |
| Fetal umbilical arteries | 2 | Carry blood back to the placenta. |
| Major fetal shunts | 3 | Ductus venosus, foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus bypass liver or lungs. |
| Approximate oxygen saturation of umbilical venous blood | About 80% | Fetal blood is less saturated than adult systemic arterial blood. |
| Lecturer's approximate HbF decline reference | Around 4 months after birth | HbF is progressively replaced by HbA; avoid treating the change as instantaneous. |

### Core chains
1. **Fetal oxygenated route**: Placenta → umbilical vein → ductus venosus → inferior vena cava → right atrium → foramen ovale → left atrium/ventricle → ascending aorta → heart and brain.
2. **Fetal pulmonary bypass**: Superior vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary trunk → ductus arteriosus → descending aorta → umbilical arteries → placenta.
3. **Foramen ovale closure**: First breath increases lung oxygen → pulmonary resistance falls → more blood reaches left atrium; cord clamping raises systemic resistance → left atrial pressure exceeds right → flap shuts.
4. **Ductus arteriosus closure**: Placenta is disconnected and lungs oxygenate blood → prostaglandins fall and arterial oxygen rises → ductus arteriosus constricts → ligamentum arteriosum eventually forms.

### Don't confuse
| | **Ductus venosus** | **Ductus arteriosus** |
|---|---|---|
| Connection | Umbilical vein towards inferior vena cava. | Pulmonary trunk towards descending aorta. |
| Main bypass | Liver. | Lungs. |
| Adult remnant | Ligamentum venosum. | Ligamentum arteriosum. |

| | **Foramen ovale before birth** | **Foramen ovale after birth** |
|---|---|---|
| Atrial pressure | Right atrial side relatively higher. | Left atrial pressure becomes higher. |
| Blood movement | Right-to-left atrial shunting. | Flap closes and functional shunt ends. |

| | **HbF** | **HbA** |
|---|---|---|
| 2,3-BPG binding | Weaker binding. | Stronger binding. |
| Oxygen affinity | Higher, favouring placental oxygen uptake. | Lower, favouring peripheral oxygen unloading. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Fetal lungs** are the principal oxygen exchanger → Truth: Placenta supplies fetal oxygen while pulmonary resistance is high.
- Trap: **Umbilical arteries** carry the oxygen-rich blood towards fetus → Truth: The single umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood towards fetus.
- Trap: **Ductus venosus** bypasses lungs → Truth: It bypasses much of the liver; ductus arteriosus and foramen ovale bypass pulmonary flow.
- Trap: **Foramen ovale** shunts left-to-right before birth → Truth: Higher right-sided pressure supports right-to-left flow.
- Trap: **HbF** binds more 2,3-BPG and therefore has higher oxygen affinity → Truth: HbF binds 2,3-BPG less strongly, increasing affinity.
- Trap: **Cord clamping** lowers systemic resistance → Truth: Removing the low-resistance placenta raises systemic resistance.
- Trap: **First breaths** increase pulmonary vascular resistance → Truth: Oxygenation and lung expansion lower resistance markedly.
- Trap: **All fetal shunts** seal anatomically the instant breathing begins → Truth: Functional changes precede fibrous anatomical closure.

## Lymphatic Circulation

### Must-know
- ★ **Lymphatic vessels** return filtered interstitial fluid and escaped plasma proteins to the bloodstream, maintaining normal tissue fluid balance.
- **Blind-ended lymphatic capillaries** open within tissue spaces → excess interstitial fluid can enter without requiring a separate arterial supply.
- **Overlapping endothelial flaps** act as one-way entry valves → raised tissue pressure opens them, while higher lymph pressure closes them.
- **Lymphatic valves** prevent backward flow as lymph moves through low-pressure collecting vessels.
- ★ **Intrinsic lymph-vessel contraction** generates forward flow without a central lymphatic pump equivalent to the heart.
- **Skeletal-muscle movements** squeeze lymph vessels → valves direct lymph proximally, increasing drainage during activity.
- **Breathing movements** vary thoracic and abdominal pressures → promote movement of lymph towards the venous circulation.
- **Adjacent arterial pulsation** can mechanically compress lymph vessels and help propel their contents.
- **Lymph nodes** filter lymph and expose carried material to immune cells → they support immune surveillance rather than pumping lymph.
- ★ **Thoracic duct** drains most of the body into the left venous angle where internal jugular and subclavian veins meet.
- **Right lymphatic duct** drains the right upper body into the right venous angle.
- **Thoracic duct territory** includes both lower limbs, abdomen and left upper body, not merely the chest.
- **Right lymphatic drainage** includes right head/neck, right upper limb and right thoracic region.
- **Intestinal lacteals** absorb chylomicrons carrying long-chain dietary fats → fat reaches blood through lymphatic circulation.
- **Chyle** is milky intestinal lymph because absorbed chylomicrons are rich in triglyceride.
- **Short- and medium-chain fatty acids** enter portal blood more directly rather than depending on intestinal chylomicron lymph transport.
- ★ **Lymphatic reserve** increases drainage when capillary filtration rises → swelling is delayed until filtration exceeds maximum return capacity.
- **Escaped plasma protein** normally returns through lymphatics → lymph blockage increases interstitial oncotic pull and fluid retention.
- **Filariasis** obstructs or injures lymph vessels → persistent protein-rich lymphoedema develops.
- **Lymph-node excision** in cancer surgery may damage drainage pathways → swelling can occur in the limb they served.
- **Tumour-related lymphatic blockage** prevents normal drainage → distal tissue swelling develops.
- **Chronic lymphoedema** becomes firm and non-pitting as protein accumulation drives inflammation and fibrosis.
- **Immune consequences of lymphatic loss** arise because antigen transport and lymph-node filtering become less effective.
- **Fat-malabsorption consequences** arise when intestinal lymph drainage fails because long-chain fat transport is impaired.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Thoracic-duct drainage territory | Roughly three-quarters of body | Returns lymph to left venous angle. |
| Right lymphatic-duct territory | Right upper quarter of body | Returns lymph to right venous angle. |

### Core chains
1. **Lymph entry and return**: Plasma filters into tissues → interstitial pressure opens overlapping lymphatic endothelial flaps → fluid/proteins enter lymph capillaries → valved collecting vessels move lymph → venous angles return it to blood.
2. **Lymph propulsion**: Intrinsic collecting-vessel contractions plus muscle movement/respiration/arterial pulsation compress lymph → one-way valves prevent reflux → fluid advances without a central lymphatic heart.
3. **Fat absorption**: Long-chain dietary fat is packaged into chylomicrons → enters intestinal lacteals → travels as chyle through lymphatic channels/thoracic duct → enters venous blood.
4. **Lymphatic obstruction**: Parasites, cancer or surgery damage lymph drainage → fluid and proteins accumulate → chronic inflammation/fibrosis develop → firm non-pitting lymphoedema appears.

### Don't confuse
| | **Thoracic duct** | **Right lymphatic duct** |
|---|---|---|
| Drains | Both lower limbs, abdomen and left upper body. | Right upper limb, right chest, right head/neck. |
| Venous outlet | Left jugulo-subclavian junction. | Right jugulo-subclavian junction. |

| | **Long-chain dietary fats** | **Short-/medium-chain fats** |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption route | Chylomicrons through intestinal lacteals and lymph. | Primarily portal venous blood. |
| Lymphatic dependence | High. | Much lower. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Lymph nodes** provide the main pumping force for lymph → Truth: Vessel contractions and external mechanical pumps move lymph; nodes filter.
- Trap: **Thoracic duct** drains only the left chest → Truth: It drains most of the body including both lower limbs and the abdomen.
- Trap: **Lymphatics** only carry extra water → Truth: They return escaped plasma protein and transport dietary long-chain fat.
- Trap: **Lymphatic capillary flaps** require an active muscular valve → Truth: Tissue-to-lymph pressure differences move overlapping endothelial flaps.
- Trap: **Lymphoedema** is always soft and pitting indefinitely → Truth: Protein accumulation causes chronic inflammation and fibrosis, producing firm non-pitting swelling.
