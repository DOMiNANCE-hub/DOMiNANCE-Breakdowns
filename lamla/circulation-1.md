## Chapter 1 — Introduction to the Heart and Circulation

### Must-know
- ★ **Circulatory transport** is necessary because diffusion alone cannot deliver oxygen and nutrients fast enough over large tissue distances.
- **Microvascular proximity** brings blood within micrometres of cells → short diffusion distances allow rapid exchange.
- ★ **Pulmonary circulation** carries right ventricular blood to lungs for oxygenation → pulmonary veins deliver it to left atrium.
- ★ **Systemic circulation** carries left ventricular blood to organs → systemic veins return it to the right atrium.
- **Circulations in series** require equal long-term right and left ventricular output because persistent mismatch would accumulate blood in one circuit.
- **Systemic organ branches** lie in parallel → each tissue receives arterial blood and can control its resistance separately.
- **Coronary arteries** provide a separate arterial route for myocardium because chamber blood cannot nourish thick muscle walls directly.
- **Right atrium** receives blood from systemic veins → right ventricle is filled for pulmonary pumping.
- **Left atrium** receives oxygenated pulmonary venous blood → left ventricle is filled for systemic pumping.
- **Atria** mainly receive and top up blood, whereas ventricles generate the pressure that drives blood through vessels.
- **Tricuspid and mitral valves** permit atrium-to-ventricle flow when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure.
- **Pulmonary and aortic valves** permit ventricular outflow when ventricular pressure exceeds the downstream arterial pressure.
- ★ **Passive valve movement** follows pressure gradients; valve leaflets do not actively contract to open or close.
- **Arteries and veins** are defined by direction: arteries carry blood away from heart; veins return it, regardless of oxygen content.
- **Left ventricular wall thickness** is greater because systemic resistance needs higher pressure, not because the left ventricle pumps more volume.
- **Low pulmonary circuit pressure** protects thin alveolar capillaries from excessive filtration and pulmonary oedema.
- **Systole and diastole** describe a chamber’s contraction/pressure generation and relaxation/filling, respectively.
- **Atrial systole** occurs near the end of ventricular diastole → the atrial kick completes ventricular filling.
- **Cardiac electrical activation** normally travels SA node → AV node → His–Purkinje system before organised ventricular contraction.
- **Electrical-before-mechanical sequence** allows depolarisation to trigger contraction, followed by pressure changes, valve action and blood movement.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Resting output from each ventricle | About 5 L/min | Right and left outputs match in circuits connected in series. |
| Systemic left ventricular systolic pressure | About 120 mmHg | Reflects high systemic vascular resistance. |
| Right ventricular/pulmonary systolic pressure | About 25 mmHg | Reflects low-resistance pulmonary flow. |

### Core chains
1. **Whole-body circulation**: Systemic veins → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → systemic arteries.
2. **Cardiac pumping sequence**: SA node fires → atria depolarise and contract → AV delay permits ventricular filling → His–Purkinje activates ventricles → pressure rises → outflow valves open.
3. **Valve operation**: Upstream pressure exceeds downstream pressure → valve opens → blood flows; gradient reverses → valve shuts → backflow is prevented.

### Don't confuse
| | **Systemic circulation** | **Pulmonary circulation** |
|---|---|---|
| Pump origin | Left ventricle. | Right ventricle. |
| Main purpose | Deliver oxygen/nutrients to tissues. | Oxygenate blood in lungs. |
| Pressure/resistance | Relatively high. | Relatively low. |

| | **Artery** | **Vein** |
|---|---|---|
| Defining direction | Away from heart. | Towards heart. |
| Oxygen content | Often high, except pulmonary/umbilical exceptions. | Often low, except pulmonary/umbilical exceptions. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Left ventricle** pumps more blood each minute because its wall is thicker → Truth: Both ventricles have equal long-term output but different pressure workloads.
- Trap: **Pulmonary artery** must contain oxygenated blood because it is an artery → Truth: Arteries are named by direction; pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood.
- Trap: **Heart valves** need their own muscle contraction to open → Truth: Pressure differences move their leaflets passively.
- Trap: **Atrial systole** takes place while ventricles are already ejecting → Truth: Atrial contraction tops up ventricles near the end of their diastole.

## Chapter 2 — Cardiac Muscle Physiology

### Must-know
- ★ **Cardiac muscle cells** are striated, branched and electrically connected → they generate rhythmic coordinated contractions rather than isolated twitches.
- **Intercalated discs** join adjacent myocytes mechanically and electrically so contractile force and depolarisation spread throughout myocardium.
- **Gap junctions** pass ionic current between cells → electrical signals spread directly between connected cardiac fibres.
- **Desmosomes** protect intercellular attachments from tearing during repeated contraction.
- **Fascia adherens** anchors actin to cell junctions → contractile force passes to neighbouring myocytes.
- **Functional syncytium** means atrial or ventricular cells behave as an electrically coordinated unit despite remaining separate cells.
- **Fibrous atrioventricular skeleton** electrically insulates atria from ventricles → normal impulses cross chiefly via the AV conducting pathway.
- ★ **Ventricular action-potential phase 0** rapidly depolarises working muscle because fast Na⁺ channels open and sodium enters.
- **Ventricular phase 1** is brief early repolarisation following fast sodium-channel inactivation.
- ★ **Ventricular phase 2 plateau** persists because inward L-type Ca²⁺ current roughly balances outward K⁺ current.
- **Ventricular phase 3** repolarises myocytes because K⁺ efflux dominates as calcium influx declines.
- **Ventricular phase 4** maintains a stable resting membrane potential until the next activating impulse.
- **ST segment** corresponds largely to the ventricular action-potential plateau, when myocardium is depolarised but still mechanically active.
- ★ **Calcium-induced calcium release** begins when Ca²⁺ enters through L-type channels and opens ryanodine receptor RyR2 on sarcoplasmic reticulum.
- **Cytosolic calcium rise** permits Ca²⁺ binding to troponin C → tropomyosin moves → actin–myosin cross-bridges form.
- **Cross-bridge cycling** generates force when myosin repeatedly interacts with actin using ATP.
- **SERCA2a** uses ATP to pump cytosolic Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum → muscle relaxation becomes possible.
- **Na⁺–Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX)** removes intracellular Ca²⁺ across the sarcolemma → helps terminate contraction.
- **Phospholamban** normally inhibits SERCA; its phosphorylation releases inhibition and accelerates calcium reuptake.
- **ATP dependence of relaxation** means calcium removal fails when energy supply is inadequate, prolonging contractile dysfunction.
- **Long cardiac refractory period** overlaps most of contraction → rapid repetitive stimuli cannot cause sustained ventricular tetanus.
- **Prevention of tetanus** preserves time for ventricular relaxation, filling and repeated effective ejection.
- **Sympathetic β₁ activation** increases cAMP and PKA → Ca²⁺ entry/release rise, increasing force and accelerating relaxation.
- **Positive inotropy** means stronger contraction at the same initial fibre stretch or ventricular filling.
- **Positive lusitropy** means faster myocardial relaxation through more rapid intracellular calcium removal.
- **Positive chronotropy** means increased heart rate through faster pacemaker firing.
- **Vagal stimulation** primarily slows SA nodal firing and atrial contraction, with much less direct effect on ventricular force.
- **Phosphodiesterase inhibition by xanthines** reduces cAMP breakdown → stronger sympathetic-like intracellular signalling can develop.
- **Impaired SERCA function** delays calcium clearance → relaxation becomes slower and diastolic filling pressure may rise.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Principal phases of a ventricular myocyte action potential | 0–4 | Fast depolarisation, early repolarisation, plateau, repolarisation and resting phase. |
| Functional cardiac muscle syncytia | 2 | Atrial and ventricular units are electrically insulated from each other. |

### Core chains
1. **Excitation–contraction coupling**: Ventricular action potential → L-type calcium entry → RyR2-mediated SR calcium release → troponin C binding → cross-bridge cycling → ventricular contraction.
2. **Relaxation**: Calcium channels close and potassium repolarises cell → SERCA returns calcium to SR and NCX expels calcium → troponin releases calcium → cross-bridges cease → muscle relaxes.
3. **Sympathetic stimulation**: Noradrenaline activates β₁ → cAMP/PKA signalling rises → calcium entry and SR cycling increase → contraction becomes stronger and relaxation faster.
4. **Tetanus prevention**: Sustained plateau prolongs refractory period → cardiac muscle cannot be re-excited throughout most of systole → relaxation and diastolic filling remain possible.

### Don't confuse
| | **Working ventricular myocyte** | **SA nodal pacemaker cell** |
|---|---|---|
| Upstroke | Fast Na⁺ entry. | Predominantly Ca²⁺ entry. |
| Phase 2 | Prolonged plateau. | No comparable ventricular plateau. |
| Resting phase | Relatively stable phase 4. | Spontaneously depolarising phase 4. |

| | **Inotropy** | **Lusitropy** |
|---|---|---|
| Property | Strength of contraction at equal preload. | Speed of relaxation. |
| Main Ca²⁺ relationship | More available activator calcium increases force. | Faster calcium removal speeds relaxation. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Gap junctions** mainly prevent cells tearing → Truth: They transmit current; desmosomes and fascia adherens provide mechanical attachment.
- Trap: **Phase 2 plateau** means cardiac muscle is electrically at rest → Truth: Ventricular cells remain depolarised while Ca²⁺ entry supports contraction.
- Trap: **Calcium influx alone** supplies all activator calcium → Truth: Entering Ca²⁺ triggers much larger SR release through RyR2.
- Trap: **SERCA** sends calcium out of the cell → Truth: SERCA pumps calcium into SR; NCX helps extrude it across the membrane.
- Trap: **Cardiac muscle** can sustain tetanus like skeletal muscle → Truth: Its long refractory period prevents fusion of ventricular contractions.
- Trap: **Positive inotropy** necessarily means increased end-diastolic volume → Truth: It describes increased force at the same preload.

## Chapter 3 — Cardiac Conduction and Automaticity

### Must-know
- ★ **SA node** normally starts each heartbeat because its spontaneous diastolic depolarisation reaches threshold faster than other pacemaker tissues.
- **SA node position** is near the junction of superior vena cava and right atrium.
- ★ **Normal conduction pathway** is SA node → atrial myocardium → AV node → His bundle → right/left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricles.
- **Automaticity** means specialised pacemaker cells depolarise spontaneously; autonomic nerves modify their rate rather than create each impulse.
- **Pacemaker phase 4** spontaneously rises towards threshold, partly through funny current activated at negative membrane potentials.
- **Funny current (If)** allows inward depolarising current during the SA nodal pacemaker phase → the next spontaneous impulse is initiated.
- **SA nodal phase 0** is mainly L-type Ca²⁺ entry rather than the fast sodium upstroke of working ventricular myocytes.
- **SA nodal phase 3** repolarises as potassium exits; a ventricular-style phase 2 plateau is absent.
- **Fastest-pacemaker dominance** suppresses slower automatic foci because SA node impulses reach and reset them before they fire.
- **AV junction escape rhythm** can take over after SA node failure but typically produces a slower ventricular rate.
- **Ventricular escape rhythm** is slower again because distal conducting tissues depolarise spontaneously at a lower frequency.
- ★ **AV nodal delay** slows atrium-to-ventricle conduction so atria can finish filling ventricles before ventricular contraction begins.
- **AV nodal location** is in the lower right atrial septal region near the tricuspid valve.
- **PR interval** spans start of atrial depolarisation to start of ventricular depolarisation and includes atrial conduction plus AV delay.
- ★ **His–Purkinje rapid conduction** synchronises ventricular activation → ventricular pressure rises efficiently rather than in scattered contractions.
- **Purkinje conducting speed** is much greater than AV nodal speed because specialised fibres transmit impulses rapidly through ventricles.
- **Intrinsic SA nodal rate** is faster than the resting pulse because baseline vagal inhibition slows pacemaker firing.
- **Vagal M₂ stimulation** increases pacemaker K⁺ conductance and reduces If/cAMP → phase 4 becomes less steep and rate falls.
- **Sympathetic β₁ stimulation** increases cAMP and pacemaker depolarising currents → phase 4 steepens and heart rate rises.
- **Atropine** blocks vagal muscarinic signalling → resting heart rate rises because the normal vagal brake is removed.
- **First-degree AV block** delays conduction → PR interval is prolonged but every atrial impulse reaches ventricles.
- **Second-degree AV block** intermittently fails conduction → some P waves are not followed by ventricular QRS complexes.
- **Third-degree AV block** stops atrial-to-ventricle conduction → atrial and ventricular rhythms become independent.
- **Escape pacemakers** protect against complete asystole after proximal failure but often generate slow, inadequate ventricular rates.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Intrinsic SA nodal rate | About 100 beats/min | Vagal tone reduces the resting heart rate below this. |
| Resting heart rate with vagal tone | About 60–80 beats/min | Vagal inhibition normally limits pacemaker discharge. |
| AV junction escape rate | About 40–60 beats/min | Backup pacemaker is slower than SA node. |
| Ventricular escape rate | About 20–40 beats/min | Distal backup pacemaker may not maintain adequate perfusion. |
| AV node conduction velocity | About 0.05 m/s | Slow propagation allows atrial emptying before ventricular systole. |
| Purkinje conduction velocity | About 2–4 m/s | Very rapid activation synchronises ventricular contraction. |
| Normal PR interval | 0.12–0.20 seconds | Measures atrial-to-ventricular electrical activation time. |
| Normal QRS duration | About 0.06–0.10 seconds (lecture ECG values) | Represents rapid ventricular depolarisation. |

### Core chains
1. **Normal beat sequence**: SA node fires → atria depolarise and contract → AV node delays signal → His/bundle branches/Purkinje distribute impulse → ventricles depolarise together and contract.
2. **Pacemaker automaticity**: SA nodal phase 3 ends → If contributes to phase 4 rise → threshold opens Ca²⁺ channels → nodal phase 0 → K⁺ exit restores negative potential.
3. **Autonomic chronotropy**: Sympathetic β₁ increases cAMP → phase 4 rises faster → earlier impulse; vagal M₂ increases K⁺ and lowers cAMP → slower phase 4 → later impulse.
4. **Complete heart block**: AV conduction stops → SA node still drives atria → distal escape focus drives ventricles independently → the ventricles beat slowly and independently of the atria.

### Don't confuse
| | **SA node** | **AV node** |
|---|---|---|
| Usual role | Dominant pacemaker. | Delays atrial-to-ventricular conduction. |
| Dominant property | Fastest automaticity. | Slow transmission. |

| | **First-degree block** | **Third-degree block** |
|---|---|---|
| Impulse transmission | Every atrial impulse arrives late. | No atrial impulses conduct through AV system. |
| ECG clue | Prolonged PR with every P followed by QRS. | Independent P and QRS rhythms. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Autonomic nerves** are required to initiate SA nodal firing → Truth: Automaticity is intrinsic; autonomic nerves alter discharge rate.
- Trap: **SA nodal upstroke** uses fast sodium channels exactly like ventricles → Truth: Nodal phase 0 is driven mainly by calcium entry.
- Trap: **AV delay** means the heart wastes time before ventricular filling → Truth: It permits atrial contraction to complete filling.
- Trap: **Purkinje fibres** are slow to allow sequential ventricular contraction → Truth: They conduct rapidly to coordinate both ventricles.
- Trap: **First-degree heart block** drops some atrial impulses → Truth: Every impulse conducts but the PR interval is prolonged.
- Trap: **Complete AV block** means the atria stop firing → Truth: Atria may keep following SA rhythm while ventricular escape rhythm is independent.

## Chapter 4 — The Cardiac Cycle

### Must-know
- ★ **Cardiac cycle** is the ordered sequence of electrical activation, muscle contraction/relaxation, pressure changes, valve movement and volume changes.
- **Ventricular diastole** includes filling and relaxation, while atrial systole occurs near its end.
- **Atrial diastole** overlaps ventricular systole and much of ventricular diastole because atria relax and refill for most of each beat.
- **Joint cardiac diastole** occurs when both atria and ventricles are relaxed together during part of passive ventricular filling.
- ★ **Passive heart valves** open when upstream pressure exceeds downstream pressure and close when the pressure gradient reverses.
- **Papillary muscles** prevent AV valve leaflets everting during ventricular systole; they do not actively close the valves.
- **Rapid ventricular filling** begins when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure and AV valves open.
- **Diastasis** is a slower filling interval as the atrium-to-ventricle pressure gradient becomes small.
- **Atrial kick** adds the final portion of ventricular filling as the P wave triggers atrial contraction.
- **Atrial contraction** contributes more to filling when ventricular compliance is reduced or diastole is shortened by tachycardia.
- ★ **Isovolumetric contraction** starts when mitral/tricuspid valves close, creating S1; all valves remain shut while ventricular pressure rises at fixed EDV.
- **AV valve bulging** during early ventricular contraction causes the atrial c wave as pressure pushes the closed leaflet backwards.
- **Semilunar valve opening** occurs only after ventricular pressure exceeds aortic or pulmonary artery pressure.
- **Rapid ejection** initially expels blood quickly because ventricular pressure and outflow gradients are high.
- **Reduced ejection** follows rapid ejection as ventricular force declines and outflow slows despite continuing systole.
- **Ventricular repolarisation** appears as the T wave during later ejection before the semilunar valves close.
- **Protodiastole** is the short transition after ejection when ventricular pressure falls towards the arterial pressure.
- ★ **Semilunar valve closure** follows slight arterial backflow when ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure → S2 marks the end of ejection.
- **Aortic dicrotic notch** reflects transient pressure disturbance associated with aortic valve closure and arterial elastic recoil.
- **Isovolumetric relaxation** occurs with all four valves shut → ventricular pressure falls while volume remains at ESV.
- **Ventricular filling restarts** when pressure becomes lower than atrial pressure → mitral/tricuspid valves open passively.
- **End-diastolic volume (EDV)** is the ventricular volume immediately before contraction, after atrial filling ends.
- **End-systolic volume (ESV)** is blood remaining after ventricular ejection ends.
- **Stroke volume** equals EDV minus ESV → reflects the volume pumped out by one ventricle per beat.
- **Ejection fraction** equals stroke volume divided by EDV → gauges the fraction of filled blood ejected each beat.
- **Atrial a and c waves** reflect atrial contraction and closed AV valve bulging during early ventricular systole, respectively.
- **Atrial x descent** reflects atrial relaxation and downward AV ring movement during ventricular systole.
- **Atrial v wave** forms as the atrium fills behind a closed AV valve during ventricular contraction.
- **Atrial y descent** begins when the AV valve opens and atrial blood empties into the ventricle.
- **P wave** reflects atrial depolarisation preceding atrial mechanical contraction.
- **QRS complex** reflects ventricular depolarisation preceding isovolumetric contraction and S1.
- **T wave** reflects ventricular repolarisation during late ejection, before ventricular pressure and arterial flow fall enough for S2.
- **Wiggers diagram** aligns ECG, chamber pressures, arterial pressure, ventricular volume and sounds to one shared time axis.
- **Pressure-curve intersection** identifies each valve opening or closing because valves respond to which chamber has higher pressure.
- **Right ventricular ejection** begins at lower pressure than left ventricular ejection because pulmonary arterial pressure is much lower.
- **Physiological S2 splitting** widens on inspiration as right-sided emptying lasts longer and P2 is delayed relative to A2.
- **Faster heart rates** shorten diastole disproportionately → passive filling and left coronary perfusion time can decrease.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Typical resting cardiac cycle | About 0.8 seconds | Corresponds to roughly 75 beats/min. |
| Time both atria and ventricles are relaxed | About 0.4 seconds in a 0.8-second cycle | Passive filling overlaps atrial diastole. |
| Passive ventricular filling proportion in lecture | About 70% | Most filling usually needs no atrial contraction. |
| Atrial filling contribution in lecture | About 30% | Atrial kick completes filling; proportion varies with conditions. |
| Approximate aortic pressure needed for LV ejection | 80 mmHg | Left semilunar valve opens when LV pressure exceeds aortic diastolic pressure. |
| Approximate pulmonary pressure needed for RV ejection | 10 mmHg | Right semilunar valve opens at lower pressure. |
| Typical EDV in lecture | About 130 mL | Ventricular blood volume at end of filling. |
| Normal interval between P and QRS onset | 0.12–0.20 seconds | Electrical delay allows atrial emptying before ventricular systole. |
| Stroke-volume relation | SV = EDV − ESV | Defines the volume ejected in one beat. |
| Ejection fraction relation | EF = SV ÷ EDV × 100% | Expresses the ejected fraction of end-diastolic volume. |

### Core chains
1. **Filling and atrial systole**: Ventricles relax → AV valves open → rapid filling then diastasis → SA node fires and P wave appears → atria contract → EDV is reached.
2. **Isovolumetric contraction and ejection**: QRS activates ventricles → rising ventricular pressure closes AV valves (S1) → all valves shut and volume fixed → pressure exceeds arterial pressure → rapid then reduced ejection lowers volume.
3. **Semilunar closure and relaxation**: Ventricular repolarisation progresses → ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure → semilunar valves close (S2) → all valves shut as pressure falls at ESV → AV valves reopen.
4. **Wiggers interpretation**: Identify ECG event → find ensuing ventricular pressure change → compare upstream/downstream pressure curves → identify valve state → determine ventricular-volume change and heart sound.

### Don't confuse
| | **Isovolumetric contraction** | **Isovolumetric relaxation** |
|---|---|---|
| Ventricular pressure | Rises sharply. | Falls sharply. |
| Ventricular volume | Fixed at EDV. | Fixed at ESV. |
| Valve state | All four valves closed. | All four valves closed. |
| Associated sound | Follows S1 from AV valve closure. | Follows S2 from semilunar closure. |

| | **Atrial a wave** | **Atrial v wave** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Atrial contraction. | Atrial filling while AV valve remains closed. |
| Timing | Late ventricular diastole. | Ventricular systole. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Isovolumetric phase** means ventricular pressure stays fixed → Truth: Volume stays constant while pressure rises or falls with all valves closed.
- Trap: **Semilunar valves open** because atria contract → Truth: They open only when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure.
- Trap: **Papillary muscles** open the AV valves → Truth: They prevent prolapse of valves already forced shut by ventricular pressure.
- Trap: **The entire ejection phase** has the same high flow → Truth: Ejection begins rapidly and then becomes reduced.
- Trap: **T wave** occurs only after aortic valve closure → Truth: Ventricular repolarisation develops during reduced ejection before S2.
- Trap: **Atrial v wave** equals atrial systole → Truth: It reflects atrial venous filling behind the shut AV valve.
- Trap: **S2 widening on inspiration** is due to A2 delay → Truth: Increased right ventricular filling chiefly delays P2.

## Chapter 5 — Heart Sounds and Murmurs

### Must-know
- ★ **S1** is produced by mitral/tricuspid valve closure and resulting vibration when ventricular systole begins.
- ★ **S2** is produced by aortic/pulmonary valve closure and resulting vibration at the onset of ventricular diastole.
- **Heart sounds** chiefly reflect vibration from abruptly changing blood flow rather than valve leaflets striking like solid objects.
- **Valve opening** is normally silent because opening does not suddenly stop a moving blood column.
- **S1–S2 interval** spans ventricular systole, while S2–next S1 spans ventricular diastole.
- **Apex auscultation** generally makes S1 louder, whereas the cardiac base generally makes S2 more prominent.
- ★ **Physiological S2 splitting** occurs on inspiration because greater right-sided filling delays pulmonary valve closure (P2) relative to A2.
- **Expiration** usually narrows normal S2 splitting because the right ventricular ejection delay becomes smaller.
- **S3** occurs just after S2 during rapid ventricular filling → may be normal in youth but suggests volume overload in older adults.
- **S4** occurs immediately before S1 when atrial contraction forces blood into a stiff, non-compliant ventricle.
- **Atrial fibrillation** abolishes the true S4 mechanism because organised atrial contraction is absent.
- **Slow heart rate** may soften S1 because AV leaflets drift nearer closure before the ventricle contracts.
- **High aortic pressure** may produce a loud aortic closure component (A2) as the valve closes under higher pressure.
- **High pulmonary pressure** can accentuate P2 because increased pulmonary artery pressure strengthens semilunar closure vibration.
- **Heart-sound loudness** does not by itself measure lesion severity because valve mobility and many haemodynamic factors affect it.
- ★ **Murmur** is audible vibration from turbulent blood flow caused by narrowed valves, leaking valves or high flow through normal structures.
- **Stenotic valve murmur** occurs while the narrowed valve should be open → blood accelerates through a restricted forward opening.
- **Regurgitant murmur** occurs while a valve should be closed → abnormal backflow creates a jet and turbulent vibrations.
- **Aortic/pulmonary stenosis** causes systolic murmurs because semilunar valves should be open during ventricular ejection.
- **Mitral/tricuspid regurgitation** causes systolic murmurs because AV valves should be closed during ventricular systole.
- **Mitral/tricuspid stenosis** causes diastolic murmurs because AV valves should allow ventricular filling during diastole.
- **Aortic/pulmonary regurgitation** causes diastolic murmurs because semilunar valves should be closed during ventricular filling.
- **High-output flow murmurs** can arise with anaemia because increased velocity and low blood viscosity favour turbulence through normal valves.
- **Aortic listening area** is the second right intercostal space near the sternum, where aortic sounds transmit best.
- **Pulmonary listening area** is the second left intercostal space near the sternum, where P2 and pulmonary murmurs transmit.
- **Tricuspid listening area** is the lower left sternal border, rather than the valve's exact anatomical position.
- **Mitral listening area** is near the left ventricular apex, where mitral sounds are best heard.
- **Vascular bruits** also arise from turbulent blood due to narrowing, aneurysm or abnormal vascular connections.
- **Patent ductus arteriosus** causes a continuous murmur because aortic pressure exceeds pulmonary artery pressure during systole and diastole.
- **Arteriovenous fistula** can produce a bruit because abnormal low-resistance communication creates fast turbulent flow.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Aortic valve listening site | 2nd right intercostal space | Useful for A2 and aortic murmurs. |
| Pulmonary valve listening site | 2nd left intercostal space | Useful for P2 and pulmonary murmurs. |
| Heart-sound timing | S1, S2, S3, S4 | Distinguishes valve closure from rapid filling and atrial kick. |

### Core chains
1. **S1 and S2 generation**: Ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure → AV valves close and S1 occurs → ejection follows → ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure → semilunar valves close and S2 occurs.
2. **Normal S2 splitting**: Inspiration lowers intrathoracic pressure → right venous return increases → right ventricular ejection persists longer → pulmonary closure P2 occurs later than A2.
3. **Valve murmur prediction**: Identify valve → decide when it should be open or shut → stenosis produces turbulence while open, regurgitation while shut → infer systolic or diastolic timing.
4. **Extra heart sounds**: Rapid early diastolic filling vibrates ventricle → S3; atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle causes late diastolic vibration → S4.

### Don't confuse
| | **S3** | **S4** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Early diastole after S2. | Late diastole just before S1. |
| Mechanism | Rapid ventricular filling/volume state. | Atrial kick into stiff ventricular chamber. |
| Normal possibility | May be physiological in young people. | Generally suggests reduced ventricular compliance. |

| | **Valve stenosis** | **Valve regurgitation** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical defect | Forward opening is narrowed. | Closed valve leaks backwards. |
| Murmur timing logic | When valve should be open. | When valve should be closed. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **S1** is caused by aortic and pulmonary valve closure → Truth: S1 follows mitral/tricuspid closure; semilunar closure produces S2.
- Trap: **Inspiration splits S2** by delaying A2 → Truth: P2 is usually delayed by greater right ventricular filling and ejection duration.
- Trap: **S3** is always pathological → Truth: It may be physiological in young people but concerning in older volume-overloaded patients.
- Trap: **S4** persists in atrial fibrillation → Truth: S4 requires organised atrial contraction.
- Trap: **All systolic murmurs** are from stenotic valves → Truth: AV valve regurgitation also produces systolic turbulence.
- Trap: **Auscultation areas** exactly overlie the anatomical valve leaflets → Truth: These are surface positions where each transmitted sound is best heard.
- Trap: **Patent ductus murmur** lasts only in systole → Truth: Aortic-to-pulmonary driving pressure persists during both phases.

## Chapter 7 — Cardiac Output and the Frank–Starling Law

### Must-know
- ★ **Cardiac output (CO)** is blood pumped by one ventricle each minute → it equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.
- **Equal ventricular outputs** are required over time because the pulmonary and systemic circuits lie in series.
- **Cardiac index** adjusts output for body surface area → comparisons between differently sized people become more meaningful.
- **Heart rate alone** cannot predict cardiac output because stroke volume may fall when high rates shorten ventricular filling.
- ★ **Stroke volume** equals end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume → blood remaining after ejection is excluded from pumped volume.
- **Preload** describes ventricular stretch just before contraction and is commonly estimated by EDV.
- **Afterload** describes the pressure/resistance the ventricle must overcome during ejection, including aortic pressure for left ventricle.
- **Contractility** describes intrinsic myocardial force at the same preload, independently of how much the ventricle initially fills.
- ★ **Frank–Starling mechanism** increases stroke volume when venous return and filling rise because stretching sarcomeres improves force development.
- **Sarcomere stretch** improves actin–myosin overlap and calcium sensitivity within normal filling ranges → stronger ejection follows.
- **Output matching** occurs because ventricles eject more when more blood returns, preventing sustained blood buildup in either circulation.
- **Movement along a Starling curve** represents changed preload while the heart's intrinsic contractility is held constant.
- **Upward shift of a Starling curve** represents increased contractility → more output at the same filling pressure or EDV.
- **Downward Starling-curve shift** reflects weaker myocardium → the same filling pressure gives less stroke volume.
- **Afterload increase** makes ejection harder → stroke volume can fall and ESV rise even when preload stays unchanged.
- **Greater blood volume** increases venous return and EDV → stroke volume can increase by Frank–Starling physiology.
- **Venoconstriction** mobilises stored venous blood → higher central filling supports EDV and output.
- **Skeletal-muscle pump** returns leg blood to the heart during movement → EDV may increase.
- **Inspiration** usually supports systemic venous return to the right heart through lower thoracic pressure.
- **Atrial contraction** boosts EDV, especially when ventricular filling is impaired or the heart beats rapidly.
- **Prolonged upright posture** pools blood in leg veins → venous return, EDV and stroke volume decline.
- **Cardiac tamponade** compresses the heart and prevents adequate filling → EDV and output decrease despite circulating blood.
- **Stiff ventricular muscle** fills poorly for a given pressure → EDV and effective output may decline.
- **Very rapid heart rates** shorten diastole → EDV falls, sometimes enough to lower output despite more beats.
- ★ **Sympathetic β₁ stimulation** raises intracellular cAMP/Ca²⁺ cycling → myocardial contractility and relaxation increase.
- **Vagal stimulation** chiefly slows heart rate and has comparatively little direct influence on ventricular contractility.
- **Hypoxia and hypercapnia** can depress myocardial contractility → stroke volume decreases at a given filling level.
- **Acidosis and certain antiarrhythmic drugs** weaken inotropy → myocardial output can fall without an initial preload reduction.
- **Heart failure** shifts the Starling relation downward → extra filling increasingly causes congestion rather than adequate output gains.
- **Exercise cardiac output** rises because sympathetic activity increases rate/contractility while muscle and breathing pumps improve venous return.
- **Haemorrhage** lowers venous return and preload → compensatory tachycardia may not prevent falling output.

### Core chains

4. **Haemorrhage**: Blood volume falls → venous return and preload decrease → Frank–Starling output declines → tachycardia/vasoconstriction partly defend pressure but cannot restore lost volume.

- Trap: **Greater preload** is identical to stronger intrinsic contractility → Truth: Preload moves along the Starling curve; contractility changes the curve itself.
- Trap: **Increased afterload** automatically raises stroke volume → Truth: Higher ejection resistance can increase ESV and reduce SV.
- Trap: **Frank–Starling law** operates mainly because faster nerves are activated → Truth: The fundamental mechanism is myocardial stretch-dependent force generation.
- Trap: **Blood loss tachycardia** fully restores cardiac output → Truth: Low venous return may keep preload and output low.

## Chapter 8 — Measuring Cardiac Output and the Work of the Heart

### Must-know
- ★ **Fick principle** calculates blood flow from oxygen consumption divided by the arterial-to-mixed-venous oxygen content difference.
- **Oxygen consumption** equals cardiac output times oxygen removed from each unit volume of blood as it crosses systemic tissues.
- **Arterial oxygen content** describes actual oxygen carried per volume of blood, not merely the fraction of haemoglobin saturated.
- **Mixed venous oxygen content** is measured in pulmonary arterial blood because it contains venous return mixed from the whole body.
- **Arterial sample** may be taken from a systemic artery because systemic arterial oxygen content is approximately uniform.
- **Fick steady state** is required because measured oxygen uptake and blood content differences must represent the same stable circulation.
- **Anaemia and oxygen saturation** must be distinguished because normal saturation can coexist with low content when haemoglobin is reduced.
- **Higher arteriovenous oxygen difference** at fixed oxygen consumption implies lower cardiac output because tissues extract more from each unit of blood.
- **Indicator dilution** (supplementary) estimates flow by injecting a known marker and measuring its downstream concentration over time.
- **Rapid indicator flow** produces a smaller/shorter concentration–time curve for the same known indicator dose.
- **Thermodilution** uses cold saline as an indicator; temperature changes downstream are related to cardiac blood flow.
- ★ **Stroke work** is approximate stroke volume multiplied by ejection pressure → greater output or greater pressure requires more mechanical energy.
- **Pressure–volume loop area** represents the external mechanical stroke work performed by a ventricle each beat.
- **Equal biventricular volumes** do not imply equal work because left ventricle ejects against much higher arterial pressure.
- **Pressure loading** raises ventricular wall stress and energy demand more than an equivalent simple volume increase.
- **Myocardial oxygen demand** rises with wall tension, contractility and heart rate because all raise energetic work.
- ★ **Coronary flow increase** is needed during exercise because myocardium already extracts most available oxygen from resting coronary blood.
- **Pressure-driven oxygen demand** explains why severe arterial hypertension or aortic stenosis increases left ventricular energy use.
- **Volume-driven workload** increases with higher SV even if arterial pressure is comparatively unchanged.

3. **Stroke work and pressure**: Ventricle fills to EDV → contracts against arterial pressure and ejects SV → pressure–volume loop encloses an area → area represents external stroke work.
4. **Myocardial demand**: Pressure, rate or contractility rises → myocardial work and oxygen consumption increase → local metabolites dilate coronaries → coronary blood flow must increase.

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Fick cardiac output** can be calculated from pulse and oxygen saturation alone → Truth: Measured oxygen consumption and oxygen CONTENT difference are necessary.
- Trap: **Mixed venous oxygen** should be sampled from one peripheral arm vein → Truth: Pulmonary arterial blood represents mixed systemic venous return.
- Trap: **High arterial saturation** guarantees high arterial oxygen content → Truth: Anaemia reduces oxygen content despite high saturation.
- Trap: **Left and right stroke work** must match because their output matches → Truth: Left ventricle performs more pressure work for the same volume.
- Trap: **Increased myocardial demand** is met mainly through extracting much more oxygen → Truth: Extraction is already high and coronary blood flow must rise.

## Chapter 9 — Blood Flow and Haemodynamics

### Must-know
- ★ **Blood flow** is driven by pressure difference across a vascular segment and opposed by its resistance.
- **High absolute pressure** does not guarantee flow because a pressure gradient, not a pressure value alone, drives movement.
- **Arterial elastic recoil** releases stored systolic energy during diastole → blood continues moving while the heart relaxes.
- **Arterioles** contain smooth muscle that adjusts radius → they provide the main controllable peripheral resistance.
- **Capillaries** have very thin walls and enormous exchange area → diffusion, filtration and nutrient delivery occur efficiently.
- **Venules and veins** are compliant low-pressure channels → they return and store large blood volumes.
- **Flow versus velocity** distinguishes volume crossing a plane per time (Q) from blood displacement per time (v).
- ★ **Continuity relation** states Q = v × A → at equal flow, greater total cross-sectional area means lower mean velocity.
- **Slow capillary velocity** occurs because the combined cross-sectional area of parallel capillaries far exceeds that of the aorta.
- **Laminar blood flow** forms ordered layers with highest velocity centrally and near-zero velocity at the vessel wall.
- **Laminar flow silence** reflects smooth layered motion without significant vibration of vessel walls.
- **Turbulent flow** mixes streams irregularly → vessel vibration can generate audible cardiac murmurs and vascular bruits.
- **Reynolds number** estimates turbulence likelihood from fluid density, speed, vessel diameter and viscosity.
- **High velocity** favours turbulence, especially through narrowed valves or a high-flow arteriovenous connection.
- **Lower viscosity** can increase Reynolds number and promote flow murmurs in anaemia.
- **Larger vessel diameter** increases turbulence tendency at similar velocity and viscosity.
- ★ **Hagen–Poiseuille relation** shows ideal tube flow rises with the fourth power of radius and pressure difference.
- **Hydraulic resistance** increases with blood viscosity and vessel length but falls dramatically as radius increases.
- **Halved arteriolar radius** increases theoretical resistance sixteenfold when other conditions stay fixed.
- **Arteriolar constriction** raises resistance and lowers local flow for a given pressure gradient.
- **Parallel vascular pathways** reduce total resistance because blood has several alternative routes.
- **Series vascular resistances** add together because blood must cross each segment successively.
- **Polycythaemia** raises haematocrit and viscosity → vascular resistance and myocardial work increase.
- **Anaemia** lowers viscosity but reduces oxygen content → compensatory faster flow can still increase cardiac workload.
- **Doppler technique** measures velocity; volume flow also requires cross-sectional area.
- **Electromagnetic flow measurement** uses voltage generated as electrically conductive blood moves through a magnetic field.
- ★ **Vascular compliance** describes change in vessel volume per change in pressure → compliant veins store much blood with little pressure increase.
- **Venoconstriction** squeezes the blood reservoir → central venous return and ventricular filling increase.
- **Capacitance vessels** are mainly veins because their large compliance permits substantial storage at low pressures.

|---|---|---|
| Radius halved (other factors constant) | Resistance ×16 | Fourth-power radius sensitivity. |
| Radius narrowed by 20% | Flow falls by more than half | Poiseuille dependence: 0.8⁴ ≈ 0.41. |
| Blood held in the veins at rest | About two-thirds | High venous compliance supplies a reservoir. |
| Arm-to-tongue circulation time | About 15 seconds | Lecturer's example of whole-body transit assessment. |
| Example pressure–flow relation | 80 mmHg ÷ 20 mmHg·min/L = 4 L/min | Flow requires a pressure difference and resistance. |

### Core chains
1. **Pressure-driven flow**: Upstream pressure exceeds downstream pressure → pressure gradient acts across vascular resistance → blood moves according to Q = ΔP/R.
2. **Poiseuille effect**: Arteriolar smooth muscle contracts → radius decreases → resistance rises roughly as 1/r⁴ → tissue flow falls sharply at fixed pressure difference.
3. **Continuity**: Aorta branches into many capillaries → total cross-sectional area rises greatly → mean blood velocity falls → exchange time and area increase.
4. **Venous capacitance**: Veins relax → large volume stored at low pressure → venous return can decline; sympathetic constriction shifts stored blood to heart → output improves.

| | **Laminar flow** | **Turbulent flow** |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | Smooth parallel layers. | Irregular mixing and eddies. |
| Sound | Usually silent. | May produce murmurs/bruits. |

| | **Resistance (R)** | **Reynolds number (Re)** |
|---|---|---|
| Represents | Opposition to flow. | Tendency towards turbulence. |
| Important determinants | Radius to the fourth power, length, viscosity. | Density, velocity, diameter, inverse viscosity. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Higher arterial pressure** always guarantees greater local tissue flow → Truth: Flow depends on pressure DROP divided by resistance.
- Trap: **Blood moves fastest in capillaries** because they are individually narrow → Truth: Their enormous total area makes mean velocity slowest.
- Trap: **Poiseuille equation** makes resistance directly proportional to radius → Truth: Resistance varies inversely with the fourth power of radius.
- Trap: **A lower haematocrit** must lower cardiac work → Truth: Anaemia lowers viscosity but reduced oxygen content can drive high-output work.
- Trap: **Resistance and Reynolds number** measure the same property → Truth: One opposes flow; the other predicts tendency to turbulence.
- Trap: **Veins** carry little blood because they have low pressure → Truth: High compliance lets them store most circulating blood at low pressure.

## Chapter 10 — Arterial Blood Pressure

### Must-know
- ★ **Systolic arterial pressure** is maximum arterial pressure reached during one cardiac cycle when ventricular ejection distends arteries.
- **Diastolic arterial pressure** is minimum arterial pressure just before the next ejection, sustained partly by elastic recoil.
- **Windkessel effect** stores energy when large elastic arteries stretch in systole and releases it through recoil during diastole.
- **Stiff aorta** stretches less for a given stroke volume → systolic and pulse pressures rise.
- ★ **Mean arterial pressure** depends strongly on cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, not on heart rate alone.
- **Normal arterial pressure** does not prove normal organ perfusion because severe vasoconstriction may mask reduced cardiac output.
- **Pulse pressure** is systolic minus diastolic pressure → it widens when stroke volume or arterial stiffness rises.
- **Higher stroke volume** tends to raise systolic pressure and pulse pressure because more blood is ejected into elastic arteries.
- **Long diastolic duration** makes mean arterial pressure closer to diastolic than systolic pressure at usual resting rates.
- ★ **Approximate MAP** equals DBP plus one-third of pulse pressure at normal resting heart rates.
- **Hydrostatic pressure** adds below heart level and subtracts above → arm position can alter measured blood pressure.
- **Cuff at heart level** avoids false elevation if the arm is too low or false reduction if too high.
- **Direct arterial cannulation** measures intravascular pressure via a sensor, whereas routine cuffs estimate pressure indirectly.
- **Cuff occlusion** stops brachial artery flow when cuff pressure exceeds systolic pressure → no Korotkoff sounds occur.
- ★ **First Korotkoff sound** occurs when cuff pressure falls just below systolic pressure and blood jets through the compressed artery.
- **Korotkoff sound disappearance** during further deflation approximates diastolic pressure when flow becomes less turbulent.
- **Korotkoff sounds** arise from turbulent vibration in a partly compressed brachial artery, not from heart valve closure.
- **Palpatory systolic estimation** helps prevent missing the true systolic pressure when an auscultatory gap occurs.
- **Auscultatory gap** is temporary silence between Korotkoff sounds → unrecognised gaps can lead to incorrect systolic assessment.
- **Appropriate cuff size** is necessary because a cuff that is too narrow overestimates and one too large underestimates pressure.
- **Measurement preparation** includes quiet seated rest with supported back and feet, limiting voluntary muscle and sympathetic artefacts.
- **Slow cuff deflation** permits accurate identification of first and final Korotkoff sounds.
- **Arteriolar resistance** consumes much of the systemic pressure drop because narrow muscular resistance vessels oppose flow strongly.
- **Arteriolar damping** makes the arterial pulse progressively smaller before blood reaches capillaries.

### Core chains
1. **Windkessel pressure support**: Left ventricle ejects → elastic aorta stretches and stores energy → ventricle relaxes → aortic recoil sustains pressure and blood flow during diastole.
2. **Pressure cuff measurement**: Inflate above systolic to stop flow → gradually deflate → first Korotkoff sound marks systolic → sounds change → disappearance marks diastolic.
3. **Mean arterial pressure**: Measure systolic and diastolic → calculate PP = SBP − DBP → estimate MAP = DBP + PP/3 → recognise longer resting diastole pulls mean towards DBP.
4. **Height error**: Arm lowered below heart → hydrostatic pressure increases at cuff → falsely high reading; arm lifted above heart → falsely low reading.

### Exam traps
- Trap: **MAP** is simply the arithmetic midpoint of systolic and diastolic values → Truth: Longer diastole makes MAP closer to DBP at rest.
- Trap: **Korotkoff sounds** are transmitted S1 and S2 valve sounds → Truth: They originate from flow through the partly compressed brachial artery.
- Trap: **First Korotkoff sound** gives diastolic pressure → Truth: It marks systolic; sound disappearance gives diastolic.
- Trap: **Cuff below heart** reads artificially low → Truth: Added hydrostatic pressure makes the value falsely high.
- Trap: **Smaller-than-required cuff** reads artificially low → Truth: Excess pressure may be needed to occlude artery, overestimating BP.
- Trap: **Near-normal BP** ensures adequate organ perfusion after blood loss → Truth: High resistance may preserve pressure despite low output.

## Chapter 11 — Arterial Pulse and Jugular Venous Pulse

### Must-know
- ★ **Arterial pulse** is a pressure wave produced by ventricular ejection and travels faster than blood particles themselves.
- **Pulse-wave speed** increases when arteries become stiffer, often with age or more distal arterial branching.
- **Pulse rate** estimates the number of palpable arterial pressure waves each minute, which may differ from ventricular electrical activity.
- **Pulse rhythm** describes the spacing between pressure waves and helps identify irregular heartbeats.
- **Pulse volume and strength** reflect the amplitude and force of the peripheral pressure wave.
- **Pulse character** describes the rise and fall of the pressure wave, which may alter in valve and vascular disease.
- **Pulse deficit** occurs when some ventricular beats eject too little blood to generate a detectable peripheral pulse.
- ★ **Jugular venous pulse (JVP)** reflects right atrial pressure changes because internal jugular veins communicate without an intervening valve.
- **Visible JVP movement** is normally non-palpable because venous pressures are too low to generate a firm arterial thrust.
- **Jugular a wave** arises from right atrial contraction → venous pressure rises briefly near end-diastole.
- **Jugular c wave** arises as the closed tricuspid valve bulges towards right atrium during early ventricular systole.
- **Jugular x descent** follows atrial relaxation and downward movement of tricuspid annulus during ventricular contraction.
- **Jugular v wave** grows as right atrium fills from veins while the tricuspid valve remains closed.
- **Jugular y descent** follows tricuspid opening → right atrium empties rapidly into right ventricle.
- **Jugular waveform timing** distinguishes atrial contraction, valve bulging, atrial filling and atrial emptying.
- **Carotid pulse** is palpable and generally has one dominant thrust per beat, unlike the soft, multiwave jugular venous movement.
- **Jugular respiratory change** normally makes venous pressure fall during inspiration because thoracic pressure decreases and return improves.
- **Jugular postural change** occurs because venous column height depends on body position and right atrial pressure.
- ★ **JVP bedside assessment** measures the vertical venous pulsation above the sternal angle with the patient partially reclined.
- **Elevated jugular venous pressure** indicates increased right atrial filling pressure, as may occur in right-sided heart failure.
- ★ **Giant systolic v (cv) wave** suggests tricuspid regurgitation because ventricular contraction drives blood back into right atrium and veins.
- **Cannon a wave** occurs when right atrium contracts against a closed tricuspid valve, especially in AV dissociation or complete heart block.
- **Giant a and giant v waves** differ in timing: atrial contraction versus systolic ventricular regurgitation.

- Trap: **JVP** represents left atrial pressure → Truth: Internal jugular veins transmit right atrial pressure changes.
- Trap: **Atrial v wave** is produced by atrial contraction → Truth: It forms during atrial venous filling with tricuspid closed.

- Trap: **Patient inclination** has no influence on measured JVP → Truth: Position alters visible venous height; assess vertical distance above sternal angle.

## Chapter 12 — Veins and Venous Return

### Must-know
- ★ **Venous return** is blood reaching right atrium per minute and equals cardiac output in a steady circulation.
- **Adequate ventricular filling** requires sufficient venous return because even a strong heart cannot pump blood it has not received.
- ★ **Venous capacitance** reflects high vessel compliance → systemic veins store much of circulating blood at low pressure.
- **Vascular compliance** equals change in volume divided by change in pressure; greater compliance permits larger filling without marked pressure rise.
- **Venoconstriction** reduces venous storage capacity → stored blood moves towards heart and ventricular preload increases.
- **Venodilatation** increases peripheral blood storage → central filling and cardiac output may fall if compensation fails.
- ★ **Venous-return driving gradient** is mean systemic filling pressure minus right atrial pressure, divided by resistance to venous return.
- **Mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf)** reflects circulatory fullness and stressed vascular volume; higher fullness usually raises venous return.
- **Higher right atrial pressure** opposes the return gradient → flow back to the right heart decreases when other factors are fixed.
- **Resistance to venous return** limits blood movement from peripheral circulation towards the right atrium.
- **Skeletal-muscle pump** compresses deep veins during leg movement → blood is pushed heartward through one-way valves.
- **Venous valves** prevent backward movement when limb muscles relax → blood accumulates less in distal legs during walking.
- **Motionless standing** disables rhythmic calf compression → blood pools in compliant leg veins and venous return falls.
- **Inspiration** lowers intrathoracic pressure and increases abdominal pressure → systemic venous blood is drawn towards the thorax.
- **Arterial pulsation** compresses some neighbouring veins → assists intermittent venous return.
- ★ **Central venous pressure (CVP)** approximately reflects right atrial pressure → raised values may indicate excess volume or impaired right-heart emptying.
- **Jugular venous pressure** provides a clinical estimate of right atrial pressure because neck veins communicate with the atrium.
- **Head and neck veins** can collapse when upright because venous pressure above heart level becomes subatmospheric.
- **Dural venous sinuses** remain patent despite low pressure because rigid meningeal attachments prevent collapse.
- **Open venous sinus above heart** creates an air-embolism risk because subatmospheric venous pressure can draw air in.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Usual central venous pressure | A few mmHg | Represents low right atrial filling pressure in normal circumstances. |
| Lecture illustrative CVP | About 4.6 mmHg | Demonstrates low central venous pressure. |
| Venous blood reservoir | About half or more of systemic blood at rest | High venous compliance influences preload and shock compensation. |
| Venous-return relation | VR = (Pmsf − PRA) ÷ RVR | Upstream mean filling pressure must exceed right atrial pressure. |
| Venous compliance | C = ΔV ÷ ΔP | Large compliance supports venous storage. |

### Core chains
1. **Venous-return pressure relationship**: Blood volume/venous tone raises Pmsf → gradient to right atrium increases → venous return rises; rising right atrial pressure reduces that gradient and return.
2. **Skeletal-muscle pump**: Calf muscles contract → deep veins are compressed → upstream one-way valves open and downstream valves close → blood moves towards heart.
3. **Respiratory pump**: Inspiration lowers thoracic pressure and raises abdominal pressure → pressure gradient towards chest increases → systemic venous return to right atrium rises.
4. **Postural pooling**: Person stands motionless → gravity distends leg veins → central venous return and EDV decrease → stroke volume falls → dizziness may occur.

### Don't confuse
| | **Venous compliance** | **Venous-return resistance** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Volume stored per change in pressure. | Opposition to blood returning to heart. |
| Consequence of increase | More blood held at low pressure. | Less return for a given driving gradient. |

|---|---|---|

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Venous return** must be lower than cardiac output in a healthy steady state → Truth: Equal long-term inflow and outflow are required.
- Trap: **Veins** hold little blood because their pressure is low → Truth: High compliance allows large volumes at low pressure.
- Trap: **Increased right atrial pressure** increases return because the heart is fuller → Truth: It opposes the venous-return driving gradient.
- Trap: **Muscle pumping** works without valves → Truth: One-way valves prevent each contraction from pushing blood backwards.
- Trap: **All cranial venous channels** collapse when standing → Truth: Dural sinuses stay open, creating air-embolism risk if breached.

## Chapter 13 — Microcirculation and Capillary Exchange

### Must-know
- ★ **Microcirculation** brings oxygen/nutrients near tissues and removes wastes through thin capillary walls and large total surface area.
- **Capillary wall** is a single layer of endothelial cells on basement membrane, without contractile smooth muscle.
- **Continuous capillaries** have relatively tight endothelium and are especially restrictive in the brain.
- **Fenestrated capillaries** have endothelial pores → exchange is rapid in gut, glands and kidney.
- **Sinusoidal capillaries** have wide discontinuities → large molecules and cells can pass more readily in liver, spleen and marrow.
- ★ **Diffusion** transports gases and small solutes down concentration gradients → it is the dominant mode of nutrient and gas exchange.
- **Filtration** moves bulk fluid across capillary walls under hydrostatic and oncotic forces rather than a simple solute concentration gradient.
- **Arterioles and precapillary sphincters** regulate microvascular inflow by changing resistance and opening or restricting individual capillary pathways.
- **Metabolic activity** generates local vasodilators → more precapillary routes open and active tissue receives greater flow.
- ★ **Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc)** pushes water outward from the vascular lumen into the interstitium.
- **Plasma oncotic pressure (πc)** from albumin retains water in blood by drawing fluid inward across a selective barrier.
- **Interstitial hydrostatic pressure (Pi)** and interstitial protein pressure (πi) modify the net direction and magnitude of filtration.
- **Arteriolar dilatation** raises downstream capillary hydrostatic pressure → outward filtration tends to increase.
- **Venous congestion** raises capillary hydrostatic pressure → fluid accumulates in surrounding tissues if drainage is insufficient.
- **Hypoalbuminaemia** lowers inward plasma oncotic pressure → outward capillary filtration becomes more likely.
- **Inflammatory mediators** such as histamine increase permeability → proteins escape and contribute to tissue oedema.
- **Traditional Starling model** describes filtration at arteriolar ends with substantial reabsorption towards venous ends of capillaries.
- ★ **Endothelial glycocalyx** forms the effective molecular barrier in the revised Starling model → sustained venous-end reabsorption is often small.
- **Revised Starling interpretation** emphasises lymphatic drainage for return of most filtered fluid rather than routine venous reabsorption.
- **Low capillary velocity** results from enormous combined capillary cross-sectional area → exchange can occur efficiently.
- **Capillary blood fraction** is small despite very large aggregate capillary surface area → exchange efficiency does not require large blood storage.
- **Protein escape** occurs through leaky capillaries → interstitial oncotic pressure rises and fluid accumulation increases.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Fraction of circulating blood in capillaries | About 5% | Huge exchange surface does not imply large blood reservoir. |
| Capillary hydrostatic pressure, arterial end | About 30 mmHg | Favourable to outward filtration. |
| Capillary hydrostatic pressure, venous end | About 10 mmHg | Traditional model predicts reduced outward filtration. |
| Plasma oncotic pressure in chapter model | About 28 mmHg | Albumin-rich plasma pulls fluid in. |
| Approximate tissue hydrostatic pressure | About −3 mmHg | Influences fluid movement from capillary to tissue. |
| Approximate tissue oncotic pressure | About 8 mmHg | Interstitial proteins tend to pull fluid outward. |
| Classic lecture filtration volume | About 24 L/day | Value belongs to the traditional Starling model. |
| Traditional model reabsorbed portion | About 85% | Classroom model, not the revised glycocalyx interpretation. |
| Traditional model lymphatic return portion | About 15% | Revised model instead emphasises lymph return of most filtered fluid. |
| Traditional Starling filtration model | Jv = Kf[(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)] | Lecture model; revised glycocalyx model changes reabsorption interpretation. |

### Core chains
1. **Diffusive exchange**: Blood delivers oxygen/glucose close to cells → concentration gradient across thin capillary wall drives diffusion → tissues receive nutrients and blood picks up wastes.
2. **Raised capillary pressure**: Venous pressure rises → capillary hydrostatic pressure increases → outward water filtration exceeds lymphatic capacity → tissue oedema develops.
3. **Inflammatory permeability**: Histamine increases endothelial leak → protein moves into interstitium → tissue oncotic pull rises → additional water accumulates.
4. **Glycocalyx and lymph**: Filtered fluid passes endothelial surface layer → little sustained venous reabsorption occurs → lymphatics return most filtered fluid and escaped proteins to blood.

### Don't confuse
| | **Diffusion** | **Filtration** |
|---|---|---|
| Main driving force | Solute concentration difference. | Hydrostatic/oncotic pressure balance. |
| Principal material moved | Gases and dissolved nutrients/wastes. | Bulk water and dissolved solutes. |

| | **Traditional Starling view** | **Revised glycocalyx view** |
|---|---|---|
| Venous-end fluid | Substantial reabsorption taught. | Little sustained reabsorption in most continuous capillaries. |
| Lymph contribution | Small residual share. | Main route returning filtered fluid and escaped protein. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Capillaries** actively constrict with their own thick smooth muscle → Truth: Capillary walls are endothelial; upstream arterioles/sphincters regulate inflow.
- Trap: **High albumin** mainly pushes fluid out of capillaries → Truth: Plasma oncotic pressure pulls fluid into vascular space.
- Trap: **Reduced capillary velocity** means capillary blood flow volume is necessarily low → Truth: Total cross-sectional area is huge, so flow can remain substantial.
- Trap: **Traditional venous reabsorption percentages** describe current revised physiology → Truth: Glycocalyx-based model gives lymphatics the main steady-state return role.
- Trap: **Inflammatory oedema** requires high capillary pressure alone → Truth: Increased permeability and escaped protein can cause oedema even without major pressure rise.

## Chapter 14 — Formation of Oedema

### Must-know
- ★ **Oedema** is excess interstitial fluid when filtration and leak exceed the capacity of lymphatics to remove fluid.
- **Excess filtration** can follow stronger outward hydrostatic forces, weaker plasma oncotic forces or increased wall permeability.
- **Insufficient lymph drainage** produces swelling even when initial capillary filtration is near normal.
- ★ **Increased venous pressure** raises capillary hydrostatic pressure → more fluid is forced into tissue.
- **Prolonged standing** increases leg venous and capillary pressures → dependent ankle oedema may form.
- **Venous obstruction** prevents effective downstream drainage → pressure rises behind the block and local oedema develops.
- **Venous valve failure** can cause chronic dependent congestion because blood reflux increases leg venous pressure.
- **Right-sided heart failure** elevates systemic venous pressure → ankles, liver and abdominal fluid compartments become congested.
- **Left-sided heart failure** raises pulmonary venous pressure → lung capillary filtration increases and pulmonary oedema develops.
- ★ **Low albumin** reduces plasma oncotic retention of water → filtration rises and widespread oedema can develop.
- **Cirrhosis** reduces hepatic albumin production and can raise portal pressure → systemic/abdominal oedema becomes more likely.
- **Nephrotic syndrome** loses plasma protein into urine → plasma oncotic pressure falls and peripheral swelling develops.
- **Protein malnutrition** reduces albumin availability → interstitial water accumulates because vascular oncotic pull weakens.
- **Inflammatory histamine** increases capillary permeability → fluid and protein leave blood into interstitium.
- **Bradykinin and substance P** increase microvascular leak → tissue proteins attract more water and worsen swelling.
- **Lymphatic obstruction** prevents return of fluid and protein → protein-rich swelling becomes chronic and can cause fibrosis.
- **Filariasis** damages or obstructs lymphatics → lymphoedema may become severe and persistent.
- **Lymph-node removal** after surgery may interrupt drainage → swelling develops downstream from the removed vessels.
- **General fluid retention** increases circulating volume and vascular pressure → capillary filtration and oedema may rise.
- **Pitting oedema** leaves an indentation after pressure because mobile interstitial fluid is displaced.
- ★ **Chronic non-pitting lymphoedema** develops when protein-rich fluid induces fibrosis and tissue thickening that cannot be indented easily.
- **Swelling distribution** helps identify mechanism: dependent legs indicate systemic/venous causes, while one limb suggests local obstruction.
- **Pulmonary oedema** creates breathlessness because alveolar/interstitial fluid impairs oxygen diffusion.
- **Ascites** is peritoneal cavity fluid, often from portal/hepatic venous hypertension plus protein/fluid regulation abnormalities.
- **Starling forces** consist of vascular/tissue hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, with permeability determining effective net filtration.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Named primary mechanisms of oedema in approved chapter | 5 routes | Increased pressure, low albumin, high permeability, lymph block or overall fluid excess. |
| Traditional Starling filtration equation | Jv = Kf[(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)] | Force balance; glycocalyx and lymph must also be considered. |

### Core chains
1. **Venous oedema**: Venous outflow obstruction or right failure → venous/capillary pressure rises → net fluid filtration increases → lymphatic capacity is exceeded → interstitial swelling develops.
2. **Hypoalbuminaemic oedema**: Liver synthesis fails or kidney loses albumin → plasma oncotic pressure falls → vascular water retention decreases → diffuse swelling develops.
3. **Inflammatory oedema**: Histamine/bradykinin/substance P increase wall permeability → fluid and proteins escape → tissue oncotic pull rises → oedema increases.
4. **Lymphoedema**: Lymph vessels are blocked or destroyed → filtered protein-rich fluid cannot return → inflammation/fibrosis builds → chronic firm, often non-pitting swelling develops.

### Don't confuse
| | **Raised-hydrostatic-pressure oedema** | **Low-oncotic-pressure oedema** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary abnormality | Excess outward capillary push. | Inadequate plasma protein pull. |
| Examples | Venous blockage, heart failure. | Cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition. |

| | **Pitting oedema** | **Non-pitting lymphoedema** |
|---|---|---|
| Texture | Fluid can be displaced by pressing. | Firm protein-rich, fibrotic tissue resists indentation. |
| Typical evolution | Often earlier/mobile fluid. | Often chronic lymphatic obstruction. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Every oedema** is due to too much total blood volume → Truth: Local venous pressure, hypoalbuminaemia, inflammation and lymph blockage also cause swelling.
- Trap: **Left-sided failure** first causes ankle oedema → Truth: Pulmonary venous congestion and lung oedema are its characteristic backward effects.
- Trap: **Low plasma albumin** increases inward oncotic pull → Truth: Less albumin weakens water retention in blood.
- Trap: **Lymph blockage** only affects fluid, not proteins → Truth: Escaped proteins accumulate and promote fibrosis.
- Trap: **Non-pitting oedema** is necessarily an acute fluid leak → Truth: Chronic protein-rich lymphoedema can become fibrotic and firm.

## Chapter 15 — Lymphatic Circulation

### Must-know
- ★ **Lymphatic vessels** return filtered interstitial fluid and escaped plasma proteins to the bloodstream, maintaining normal tissue fluid balance.
- **Blind-ended lymphatic capillaries** open within tissue spaces → excess interstitial fluid can enter without requiring a separate arterial supply.
- **Overlapping endothelial flaps** act as one-way entry valves → raised tissue pressure opens them, while higher lymph pressure closes them.
- **Lymphatic valves** prevent backward flow as lymph moves through low-pressure collecting vessels.
- ★ **Intrinsic lymph-vessel contraction** generates forward flow without a central lymphatic pump equivalent to the heart.
- **Skeletal-muscle movements** squeeze lymph vessels → valves direct lymph proximally, increasing drainage during activity.
- **Breathing movements** vary thoracic and abdominal pressures → promote movement of lymph towards the venous circulation.
- **Adjacent arterial pulsation** can mechanically compress lymph vessels and help propel their contents.
- **Lymph nodes** filter lymph and expose carried material to immune cells → they support immune surveillance rather than pumping lymph.
- ★ **Thoracic duct** drains most of the body into the left venous angle where internal jugular and subclavian veins meet.
- **Right lymphatic duct** drains the right upper body into the right venous angle.
- **Thoracic duct territory** includes both lower limbs, abdomen and left upper body, not merely the chest.
- **Right lymphatic drainage** includes right head/neck, right upper limb and right thoracic region.
- **Intestinal lacteals** absorb chylomicrons carrying long-chain dietary fats → fat reaches blood through lymphatic circulation.
- **Chyle** is milky intestinal lymph because absorbed chylomicrons are rich in triglyceride.
- **Short- and medium-chain fatty acids** enter portal blood more directly rather than depending on intestinal chylomicron lymph transport.
- ★ **Lymphatic reserve** increases drainage when capillary filtration rises → swelling is delayed until filtration exceeds maximum return capacity.
- **Escaped plasma protein** normally returns through lymphatics → lymph blockage increases interstitial oncotic pull and fluid retention.
- **Filariasis** obstructs or injures lymph vessels → persistent protein-rich lymphoedema develops.
- **Lymph-node excision** in cancer surgery may damage drainage pathways → swelling can occur in the limb they served.
- **Tumour-related lymphatic blockage** prevents normal drainage → distal tissue swelling develops.
- **Chronic lymphoedema** becomes firm and non-pitting as protein accumulation drives inflammation and fibrosis.
- **Immune consequences of lymphatic loss** arise because antigen transport and lymph-node filtering become less effective.
- **Fat-malabsorption consequences** arise when intestinal lymph drainage fails because long-chain fat transport is impaired.

### Numbers to know
| Value | Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Thoracic-duct drainage territory | Roughly three-quarters of body | Returns lymph to left venous angle. |
| Right lymphatic-duct territory | Right upper quarter of body | Returns lymph to right venous angle. |

### Core chains
1. **Lymph entry and return**: Plasma filters into tissues → interstitial pressure opens overlapping lymphatic endothelial flaps → fluid/proteins enter lymph capillaries → valved collecting vessels move lymph → venous angles return it to blood.
2. **Lymph propulsion**: Intrinsic collecting-vessel contractions plus muscle movement/respiration/arterial pulsation compress lymph → one-way valves prevent reflux → fluid advances without a central lymphatic heart.
3. **Fat absorption**: Long-chain dietary fat is packaged into chylomicrons → enters intestinal lacteals → travels as chyle through lymphatic channels/thoracic duct → enters venous blood.
4. **Lymphatic obstruction**: Parasites, cancer or surgery damage lymph drainage → fluid and proteins accumulate → chronic inflammation/fibrosis develop → firm non-pitting lymphoedema appears.

### Don't confuse
| | **Thoracic duct** | **Right lymphatic duct** |
|---|---|---|
| Drains | Both lower limbs, abdomen and left upper body. | Right upper limb, right chest, right head/neck. |
| Venous outlet | Left jugulo-subclavian junction. | Right jugulo-subclavian junction. |

| | **Long-chain dietary fats** | **Short-/medium-chain fats** |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption route | Chylomicrons through intestinal lacteals and lymph. | Primarily portal venous blood. |
| Lymphatic dependence | High. | Much lower. |

### Exam traps
- Trap: **Lymph nodes** provide the main pumping force for lymph → Truth: Vessel contractions and external mechanical pumps move lymph; nodes filter.
- Trap: **Thoracic duct** drains only the left chest → Truth: It drains most of the body including both lower limbs and the abdomen.
- Trap: **Lymphatics** only carry extra water → Truth: They return escaped plasma protein and transport dietary long-chain fat.
- Trap: **Lymphatic capillary flaps** require an active muscular valve → Truth: Tissue-to-lymph pressure differences move overlapping endothelial flaps.
- Trap: **Lymphoedema** is always soft and pitting indefinitely → Truth: Protein accumulation causes chronic inflammation and fibrosis, producing firm non-pitting swelling.
