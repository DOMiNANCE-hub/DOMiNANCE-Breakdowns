# Microcirculation and Capillary Exchange

**Primary lecture:** CIRCULATION 1(4).pdf, slides 57–60 and 84–89. **Supplementary clarification:** capillary structural types and the revised glycocalyx-based Starling principle.

## 1. Why is the microcirculation necessary?

The heart delivers blood to organs, but **cells do not receive oxygen directly from the aorta**. Oxygen and nutrients must cross the tiny exchange vessels close to each tissue cell.

The **microcirculation** includes small arterioles, metarterioles or transitional vessels where present, capillaries and venules. Its tasks are to distribute blood locally, exchange substances and regulate interstitial fluid.

**Why are capillaries suitable?** Their thin walls and the very large combined capillary surface area bring flowing blood close to cells. Blood moves relatively slowly because the total cross-sectional area of parallel capillaries is large.

The lecturer notes that capillaries contain only a **small fraction of total blood volume, around 5% in its example**, despite their importance for tissue exchange.

## 2. What is a capillary wall made of?

A typical capillary is lined by **one layer of endothelial cells** supported by specialised extracellular matrix called the **basal lamina (part of the basement membrane)**. Pericytes may lie externally and help support microvascular stability.

**Important histology distinction:** A capillary does **not** possess a typical three-coat wall with a substantial smooth-muscle tunica media, unlike an artery. A capillary also does **not** need a thick subendothelial connective-tissue layer to have a basement membrane.

### Three principal capillary patterns

| Type | Structure | Common examples | Exchange implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | Relatively uninterrupted endothelial lining | Muscle, skin, CNS | Controlled passage; CNS barrier especially restrictive |
| **Fenestrated** | Endothelial pores/fenestrae | Intestinal villi, endocrine organs, renal glomeruli | Rapid exchange of water and small solutes |
| **Discontinuous / sinusoidal** | Wider gaps and discontinuities | Liver, spleen, bone marrow | Exchange of larger materials, specialised by tissue |

**A note on the lecturer's units:** Slide 58 prints measurements for gaps/fenestrations in **“mm”**. These openings are microscopic, often measured in **nanometres**, not millimetres. Treat the printed units as erroneous, while keeping the structural distinction the slide intended.

**Why do fenestrations help?** A porous endothelium provides lower resistance to selected water-soluble materials crossing the vessel. But renal filtration still depends on additional specialised layers, including the glomerular basement membrane and podocyte filtration slits; pores alone do not determine the whole barrier.

## 3. How do substances actually cross the capillary wall?

The lecture names **diffusion, filtration, fenestrations and vesicular transport**. These are not all the same kind of process.

| Mechanism | What moves? | Main driving factor |
|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion** | O₂, CO₂ and many dissolved solutes | Concentration or partial-pressure differences |
| **Intercellular routes/fenestrae** | Water and selected small hydrophilic substances | Barrier structure plus relevant gradients |
| **Vesicular transport** | Selected macromolecular cargo | Active endothelial transport mechanisms |
| **Bulk filtration** | Water and associated dissolved materials | Hydrostatic and oncotic pressure differences |

### Why can oxygen cross efficiently?

Oxygen diffuses down its **partial-pressure gradient** from capillary blood into metabolically active tissue. Carbon dioxide tends to diffuse in the opposite direction because cells produce it.

The short diffusion distance and extensive capillary surface area enable exchange. **Diffusion is not the same as fluid being forcibly filtered across the wall.**

## 4. What decides which capillaries receive more blood?

Tissues require more delivery when they become active. Local metabolic changes can dilate resistance vessels and increase the number of well-perfused microvascular pathways.

The lecturer describes **metarterioles and precapillary sphincters**. Such structures are useful classical teaching concepts but vary across vascular beds; not every capillary has an identical anatomical sphincter.

**Applied example:** During exercise, active skeletal muscle produces metabolites that promote local dilation. More blood is delivered into exchange networks where oxygen demand is high.

The lecturer notes that capillary pressure may be estimated from the external force required to occlude a capillary. It is a **method-dependent estimate**, not a direct measure of all capillary pressures everywhere.

## 5. Why does fluid leave capillaries even when protein stays in blood?

Two families of pressure forces influence movement of water across a microvascular wall:

- **Hydrostatic pressure:** Fluid pressure; inside a capillary it usually tends to push water outward.
- **Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure:** Due mainly to proteins; plasma proteins generally oppose excessive outward water movement by attracting or retaining water.

The classic balance uses four named quantities:

| Quantity | Location | Typical direction in simple model |
|---|---|---|
| **Pc** | Capillary hydrostatic pressure | Promotes outward filtration |
| **Pi** | Interstitial hydrostatic pressure | May oppose or favour filtration, depending on sign |
| **πc** | Plasma capillary oncotic pressure | Opposes outward filtration |
| **πi** | Interstitial oncotic pressure | Favours outward filtration |

**Why does plasma albumin matter?** Albumin contributes strongly to plasma oncotic pressure. Low albumin weakens the oncotic opposition to filtration, increasing oedema risk if lymphatic drainage cannot keep up.

## 6. Classic Starling model versus revised understanding

The lecturer's slides 85–88 teach hydrostatic and oncotic gradients and give an example of **24 litres filtered per day, 85% reabsorbed into capillaries and 15% returned by lymph**. This is the **classic textbook model** and explains the intended arithmetic.

**Modern physiological clarification:** The **endothelial glycocalyx** is a protein-rich surface layer that affects effective filtration and protein gradients immediately beneath it. In many continuous capillary beds, a low level of **net filtration persists during steady state**, including towards the venous end. Much of the filtered fluid is returned through **lymphatics**, rather than assuming a universal large sustained venous-end reabsorption.

**What should a student learn?** Understand the lecturer's four pressure forces and numerical illustration, but also know that the 85%/15% split **is not a universal modern physiological rule**. Transient absorption after sudden pressure changes and some specialised tissues are exceptions.

This distinction prevents a major misconception: the lymphatic system is not merely an optional back-up after routine large venous reabsorption.

## 7. What changes microvascular filtration or permeability?

Local vessel conditions alter exchange:

- **Arteriolar dilation:** May increase blood delivery and capillary hydrostatic pressure, encouraging filtration under some conditions.
- **Venous congestion:** Increases downstream and often capillary hydrostatic pressure, promoting filtration.
- **Increased endothelial permeability:** Histamine, bradykinin and substance P are examples named by the lecturer; inflammatory conditions can increase fluid/protein escape.
- **Increased surface area:** Perfusion of more capillaries can increase total exchange capacity.

**Clinical link:** Inflammation causes microvascular endothelial changes, enabling more protein and fluid to escape into tissues. Venous obstruction may raise fluid filtration without requiring an initially leaky capillary barrier. These are different causes of swelling.

The next two chapters distinguish **excessive tissue-fluid accumulation (oedema)** from **inadequate lymphatic clearance**.


## Understanding Check

**1. Why can capillaries exchange substances efficiently even though their walls are so thin?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Their thin endothelial lining and short diffusion distance bring blood close to tissue fluid. Numerous capillaries provide a large total surface area, while relatively low mean velocity creates time for exchange. Thinness is therefore an <strong>advantage for transport</strong>, not evidence that capillaries are ineffective vessels.</p></details>

**2. Do capillaries need a thick connective-tissue subendothelial layer to have a basement membrane?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>No.</strong> The basal lamina is specialised extracellular matrix closely associated with endothelial cells. It is not the same thing as a thick subendothelial connective-tissue layer, and a typical capillary lacks the muscular middle coat of an artery.</p></details>

**3. Why are fenestrated capillaries useful in endocrine organs and the intestinal villi?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Endothelial fenestrations permit relatively rapid water and small-solute exchange, suited to tissues with high exchange requirements. Their barrier properties still depend on other structural features and the tissue involved.</p></details>

**4. Why is the lecture's “mm” unit for endothelial fenestrations unsuitable?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Fenestrations are tiny pores in microscopic endothelial cells, commonly described on the <strong>nanometre scale</strong>. A pore several millimetres wide would dwarf the cell and vessel. The lecture intends to describe small permeable openings, not literal millimetre-sized gaps.</p></details>

**5. What drives oxygen diffusion from capillaries into active tissues?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Tissue cells consume oxygen, lowering local tissue oxygen partial pressure. Oxygen then diffuses from the relatively higher partial pressure in capillary blood towards lower tissue partial pressure. This is <strong>diffusion down a partial-pressure gradient</strong>, not bulk filtration of the entire blood volume.</p></details>

**6. Distinguish diffusion from filtration in one physiological example each.**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>Diffusion</strong> transfers individual molecules such as O₂ down a concentration/partial-pressure difference. <strong>Filtration</strong> moves bulk water across the microvascular barrier because of hydrostatic and oncotic forces. Solutes may accompany filtered fluid, but the driving principles differ.</p></details>

**7. If capillary hydrostatic pressure increases with other factors unchanged, what happens to filtration?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>Outward filtration generally increases.</strong> Hydrostatic pressure within the capillary pushes fluid towards the interstitium. Extra filtered fluid must be removed by lymphatic drainage or it may accumulate as oedema.</p></details>

**8. Why does a severe fall in plasma albumin promote oedema?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Albumin contributes importantly to plasma colloid osmotic pressure. When it falls, the oncotic opposition to outward capillary filtration weakens. If net filtration exceeds lymphatic return, interstitial fluid accumulates.</p></details>

**9. Does every capillary have an identical precapillary sphincter controlling whether it opens?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>No.</strong> Precapillary sphincters appear in classic microvascular models and some anatomical beds, but the degree and arrangement of local smooth-muscle control vary between tissues. Arteriolar dilation and local endothelial/metabolic mechanisms can change perfusion without a universal sphincter at every capillary.</p></details>

**10. Why does the revised Starling model place greater importance on lymphatic drainage than the classic 85% reabsorption diagram?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>The endothelial glycocalyx modifies the effective oncotic gradient. In many tissues, slight net filtration persists even near venular ends at steady state, rather than sustained reabsorption of most filtered fluid. The <strong>lymphatic system</strong> returns this filtered fluid and proteins to blood.</p></details>

**11. How may histamine increase tissue swelling without an initial increase in systemic arterial pressure?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Histamine can increase microvascular permeability, allowing more fluid and plasma proteins to cross into tissue. These local barrier changes can increase filtration and interstitial protein effects even if systemic arterial pressure has not risen.</p></details>

**12. Why can capillary recruitment improve exchange in active muscle?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>When resistance vessels dilate and more exchange paths are adequately perfused, effective capillary surface area and blood delivery increase. This brings oxygenated blood close to more active fibres and facilitates diffusion in response to tissue demand.</p></details>

## Chapter Essentials

1. **Microcirculation:** Small arterioles, capillaries and venules coordinate local flow and tissue exchange.
2. **Capillary structure:** Thin endothelium, basal lamina and possible pericytes; no typical muscular tunica media.
3. **Types:** Continuous, fenestrated and sinusoidal/discontinuous vessels differ in permeability.
4. **Transport:** Diffusion, fenestrae/paracellular routes, vesicular movement and bulk filtration are distinct.
5. **Diffusion:** O₂ and CO₂ move mainly along partial-pressure gradients.
6. **Hydrostatic Pc:** Usually promotes outward filtration; **plasma oncotic πc** usually opposes it.
7. **Other forces:** Interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures influence net fluid movement.
8. **Classic lecture model:** 24 L/day filtered, 85% reabsorbed, 15% by lymph — a historical illustrative model, not a universal modern rule.
9. **Revised Starling concept:** Endothelial glycocalyx and local protein gradients modify filtration, often leaving steady-state net filtration.
10. **Lymphatic removal:** Returns excess interstitial fluid and proteins to circulating blood.
11. **Capillary perfusion:** Arteriolar tone, local activity and vascular geometry govern exchange-path access.
12. **Permeability mediators:** Histamine, bradykinin and substance P can increase endothelial leakage and swelling.
