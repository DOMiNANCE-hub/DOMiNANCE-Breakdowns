# Microcirculation and Capillary Exchange

## Structure matches function
Capillaries consist principally of endothelial cells, a **basal lamina** and associated pericytes, with no typical three-layered arterial wall. Their small diameter and thin exchange surface facilitate diffusion. **Continuous**, **fenestrated** and **sinusoidal/discontinuous** capillaries differ in permeability and location.

Arterioles regulate incoming resistance and perfused surface area. Capillary recruitment improves exchange area in active tissues. Precapillary sphincter-like regulation varies between vascular beds and should not be assumed identical everywhere.

## Diffusion versus bulk fluid movement
**Diffusion** is driven primarily by concentration/partial-pressure gradients and is central for O₂, CO₂ and many solutes. **Bulk filtration** reflects hydrostatic and oncotic pressure differences across vascular barriers. Proteins remaining in plasma contribute to colloid osmotic pressure and oppose outward water movement.

The classic Starling expression relates net fluid flux to capillary/interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures. Modern refinements recognize the **endothelial glycocalyx** and subglycocalyx protein environment; in many continuous capillary beds there is modest net filtration along much of the vessel, with lymphatics returning interstitial fluid rather than routine large venous-end reabsorption.

## What changes exchange?
Greater capillary pressure or permeability promotes filtration; lower plasma oncotic pressure also favours tissue-fluid accumulation. Increased capillary surface area can accelerate exchange.

## Capillary wall specialisations
**Continuous capillaries** have an uninterrupted endothelial lining and comparatively controlled permeability; they are typical in muscle and CNS (the CNS has especially tight junctions). **Fenestrated capillaries** have small pores that favour exchange in tissues such as intestinal villi, endocrine glands and renal glomeruli (glomerular filtration also depends on a specialised basement membrane and podocytes). **Sinusoidal/discontinuous capillaries** have larger, irregular gaps and occur in liver, spleen and bone marrow. All are lined by endothelium supported by a **basal lamina** to varying degrees; capillaries do not have a true muscular tunica media.

The lecture's diagram describing capillary pores in **millimetres** must not be copied literally: physiological fenestrations are commonly on the **nanometre scale**, not tens of millimetres. **Precapillary sphincters** are described in classical microcirculatory models, but not every microvascular bed has discrete anatomically identical sphincters. Arteriolar tone and local microvascular mechanisms regulate perfused capillary number.

## Routes of exchange
**Diffusion** carries oxygen, carbon dioxide and many small solutes across short concentration gradients; lipid-soluble gases cross cell membranes readily. **Paracellular pathways/fenestrations** permit varying movement of water and hydrophilic solutes. **Vesicular transport** contributes to selected larger-molecule transfer, depending on tissue. **Bulk fluid filtration** is driven by hydrostatic and oncotic pressures and the endothelial barrier; it is not the same process as individual solute diffusion.

As a tissue becomes metabolically active, local mediators decrease arteriolar resistance and recruit better-perfused exchange routes, matching delivery to demand. Capillary blood normally travels slowly because the **entire capillary bed has large cross-sectional area**, promoting exchange time.

**Practice:** Why are endocrine tissues often supplied with fenestrated capillaries? Their vascular structure supports relatively rapid exchange of circulating hormones and solutes.
