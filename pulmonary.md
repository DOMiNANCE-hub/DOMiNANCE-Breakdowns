# Pulmonary Circulation

## 1. Two circulations in one lung

The lung receives blood from **two separate systems**, and they do different jobs:

| | **Pulmonary circulation** | **Bronchial circulation** |
|---|---|---|
| Job | **Gas exchange** | **Nourishes** the airways and supporting tissue |
| Comes from | Right ventricle → pulmonary arteries | **Aorta** → bronchial arteries |
| Pressure | Low | Systemic (high) |
| Drains to | Pulmonary veins → left atrium | Partly into the **pulmonary veins** |

**Why does that last row matter?** Bronchial blood has already given its oxygen to the lung tissue. When some of it drains into the pulmonary veins, it mixes a little oxygen-poor blood into the freshly oxygenated blood heading for the left atrium. This is one reason arterial blood carries slightly less oxygen than the air in the alveoli.

## 2. Same flow, much lower pressure

The two sides of the heart are **in series**, so the right ventricle must pump the **same output** as the left: about 5 L/min at rest. Yet the pressures are very different:

| | **Pulmonary** | **Systemic** |
|---|---|---|
| Arterial pressure | About **25/8** mmHg (mean about 15) | About **120/80** mmHg (mean about 93) |
| Pressure at the end of the circuit | Left atrium, about 5 | Right atrium, about 2 |
| Pressure drop across the circuit | About **10** mmHg | About **90** mmHg |

The same flow is pushed through by a pressure drop about **one-ninth** as large, so **pulmonary resistance is only about one-tenth of systemic resistance**. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/hemodynamics">→ Haemodynamics</a>)*

**Why so low?** The pulmonary vessels are **short, wide, thin-walled and stretchy**, and the capillary network is huge, with countless paths in parallel.

**Exercise: more flow, little extra pressure.** When cardiac output rises, two things happen:

- **<span data-term="recruitment">Recruitment</span>:** capillaries that were closed or barely perfused **open up**.
- **<span data-term="distension">Distension</span>:** capillaries that were already open **widen**.

Both lower the resistance, so pulmonary flow can rise several-fold while pulmonary arterial pressure rises only a little. That protects the thin capillaries, and the right ventricle, from high pressure.

## 3. Why the alveoli stay dry

The alveolar wall must be thin for gas exchange, so the lung can't afford fluid leaking into it. Compare the two main forces across a pulmonary capillary *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/microcirculation">→ Microcirculation</a>)*:

- **Capillary hydrostatic pressure**, pushing fluid out: only about **10 mmHg**.
- **Plasma oncotic pressure**, pulling fluid in: about **25 mmHg**.

The outward push is unusually weak. So only a small amount of fluid filters into the lung's interstitium, and the **lymphatics** carry it away easily, long before it can reach the alveoli.

<aside class="source-note"><strong>Source note:</strong> The full balance also includes the lung's interstitial forces (a slightly negative interstitial pressure and a fairly high interstitial protein level), which leave a small net outward filtration that the lymphatics clear. The lecture gives the two main values, 10 and 25 mmHg; Guyton and Hall give the full balance.</aside>

**When does this safety margin fail?** In **left heart failure**, left atrial pressure rises and backs up into the pulmonary veins and capillaries. Once capillary hydrostatic pressure climbs past about **25 mmHg**, roughly the plasma oncotic pressure, filtration outruns lymph drainage. Fluid collects first in the interstitium, then floods the alveoli: **<span data-term="pulmonary-oedema">pulmonary oedema</span>**, which thickens the barrier and impairs gas exchange. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-failure?section=3-left-versus-right-failure-where-does-the-blood-back-up">→ Heart Failure, §3</a>)*

**Standing up:** the lung apex sits above the heart and the base below it. Gravity adds pressure to the blood in the lower vessels and takes it away from the upper ones. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/gravity">→ Gravity</a>)* So:

- At the **base**, vascular pressure is higher, more vessels are open and wider, and **blood flow is greatest**.
- At the **apex**, vascular pressure is lowest and **blood flow is least**.

**<span data-term="west-zones">West zones</span>: compare three pressures.** Whether a capillary is open depends on the pressure inside it versus the **alveolar air pressure** squeezing it from outside:

| Zone | Pressures | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone 1** | Alveolar > arterial > venous | Capillaries squeezed shut: **no flow**. Not present in healthy lungs; can appear after haemorrhage or with positive-pressure ventilation. |
| **Zone 2** (upper lung) | Arterial > alveolar > venous | Flow depends on **arterial − alveolar** pressure, so it is intermittent |
| **Zone 3** (lower lung) | Arterial > venous > alveolar | Capillaries always open: **continuous flow** |

West zones are **pressure relationships**, not fixed stripes of lung. They shift with posture, blood pressure and breathing.

**Lying down:** the whole lung is at about the level of the heart, so flow is spread more evenly. And about **400 mL** of blood moves from the body into the lungs. Standing up returns it to the circulation. So the pulmonary vessels act as a **blood reservoir**.

**Why this matters: <span data-term="orthopnoea">orthopnoea</span>.** In left heart failure, the pulmonary capillaries are already near their limit. Lying flat adds that extra blood to the lungs, capillary pressure rises further, and the patient becomes **breathless lying down**. Sitting up moves blood back to the lower body and eases it. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/heart-failure?section=3-left-versus-right-failure-where-does-the-blood-back-up">→ Heart Failure, §3</a>)*

## 5. Low oxygen constricts lung vessels

In most tissues, low oxygen **dilates** the arterioles, bringing in more blood. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/local-regulation">→ Local Regulation</a>)* The lung does the **opposite**:

**<span data-term="hpv">Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction</span>:** when an alveolus has low oxygen, the small pulmonary arteries supplying it **constrict**.

**Why the opposite?** In the lung, low oxygen in an alveolus means that alveolus is **poorly ventilated**. Sending blood there would waste it, because the blood can't pick up oxygen. Constricting those vessels **diverts blood to well-ventilated alveoli**, matching blood flow to air flow (the **<span data-term="vq-ratio">ventilation–perfusion ratio</span>**). A rise in CO₂ (and H⁺) also constricts pulmonary vessels, which helps in the same way.

**Local versus everywhere:**

- **One poorly ventilated region** (for example, a blocked airway): blood is redirected, and oxygenation improves.
- **Every alveolus hypoxic** (high altitude, chronic lung disease): there is nowhere better to send the blood. Constriction everywhere **raises total pulmonary resistance**. If it persists, the result is **<span data-term="pulmonary-hypertension">pulmonary hypertension</span>**, and the right ventricle strains against it.

**Before birth**, the same response keeps fetal pulmonary resistance high, so blood bypasses the fluid-filled lungs. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/fetal?section=1-why-does-the-fetus-need-shortcuts">→ Fetal Circulation, §1</a>)*

**Nerves and nitric oxide:** the pulmonary vessels have an extensive autonomic supply; sympathetic stimulation **constricts** them. **Nitric oxide** from the endothelium **dilates** them.

## 6. Chapter Essentials

1. **Two circulations:** pulmonary (gas exchange, low pressure) and bronchial (from the aorta, nourishes the airways, partly drains into pulmonary veins).
2. **Same output as the left side** at about one-tenth of the resistance: mean pulmonary arterial pressure about 15 mmHg.
3. **Exercise:** recruitment and distension let flow rise with little rise in pressure.
4. **Alveoli stay dry:** capillary hydrostatic pressure is about 10 mmHg against an oncotic pressure of about 25; lymphatics clear the small leak. Above about 25 mmHg capillary pressure (left heart failure), pulmonary oedema develops.
5. **Upright:** most flow at the base. **West zones** compare alveolar, arterial and venous pressures.
6. **Lying down** moves about 400 mL of blood into the lungs, which explains **orthopnoea** in heart failure.
7. **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** diverts blood to well-ventilated alveoli. When hypoxia is everywhere, it causes **pulmonary hypertension**.
