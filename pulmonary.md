The right ventricle delivers approximately the same minute output as the left ventricle, but the pulmonary circuit normally has **far lower vascular resistance and pressure**. Its thin vessels and capillaries permit gas exchange without excessive filtration. The **bronchial circulation** from systemic arteries supplies supporting lung tissues in addition to the low-pressure pulmonary circulation.

## Gravity and distribution
In an upright person, gravity changes hydrostatic pressures across lung regions. Regional blood flow varies from apex to base as vascular pressures, alveolar pressure and recruitment/distension vary; this is often conceptualized with **West lung zones**.

## The unusual response to low oxygen
Systemic resistance vessels commonly dilate in metabolically hypoxic tissue, but **small pulmonary arteries constrict in response to low alveolar PO₂**. This diverts blood toward better-ventilated alveoli and can improve ventilation–perfusion matching. Diffuse chronic alveolar hypoxia, however, can raise pulmonary vascular resistance and contribute to pulmonary hypertension.

## Oedema prevention
Normally low pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure and efficient lymphatic drainage oppose fluid accumulation in alveoli. Raised left atrial/pulmonary venous pressure in left-heart failure increases filtration and may produce pulmonary oedema.

**Local versus widespread hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction:** local constriction redistributes blood to better ventilated alveoli; diffuse constriction increases overall pulmonary vascular resistance.

## Pulmonary vs systemic circuits
Both receive nearly the same cardiac output, yet normal pulmonary arterial pressures are much lower because pulmonary vascular resistance is low. The lung has dual circulations: **pulmonary vessels** for gas exchange and **bronchial arteries** (from the systemic aortic circulation) nourishing supporting tissues. Part of bronchial venous drainage enters pulmonary veins, contributing to normal physiological venous admixture.

## Gravity and vascular recruitment
In an upright lung, blood flow tends to be greater toward dependent (basal) regions because of hydrostatic pressure and vascular recruitment/distension. The classic **West lung zones** compare pulmonary arterial, alveolar and venous pressures; zone relationships shift with posture, lung pressure and disease. At rest pulmonary capillaries normally have low hydrostatic pressure and barrier/lymphatic protection against alveolar flooding, although pulmonary oedema can develop when those safeguards fail.

## Contrast hypoxic responses in two circulations
When an alveolar region is poorly oxygenated, nearby pulmonary arterioles constrict (**hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction**) to redirect blood toward better-ventilated alveoli. This is the opposite of the typical **systemic local hypoxic dilation** that boosts oxygen delivery to active tissues. Diffuse sustained alveolar hypoxia can raise pulmonary vascular resistance and eventually cause pulmonary hypertension/right-ventricular strain.

**Practice:** Why is high-altitude hypoxia capable of raising pulmonary pressure? Global alveolar hypoxia stimulates widespread pulmonary arteriolar constriction instead of merely diverting flow within one small region.


## Work through the pulmonary pressure and flow mechanisms

### Why can the lungs handle full cardiac output at low pressure?

The pulmonary and systemic circuits are arranged **in series**, so their flow rates are nearly equal over time. Yet the pulmonary circuit has a much lower resistance because its vessels are relatively short, compliant and arranged in an extensive parallel network. As cardiac output rises during exercise, previously underperfused capillaries **recruit** and already open vessels **distend**. Both effects limit the rise in pulmonary vascular resistance. A rise in flow therefore need not produce an equally large rise in pulmonary arterial pressure.

### West zones: use the pressures, not just the names

Compare **alveolar pressure (P_A)** with pulmonary arterial pressure (P_a) and venous pressure (P_v):

- **Zone 1 (P_A > P_a > P_v):** alveolar pressure can compress capillaries so flow is absent or very low. It is usually absent in healthy resting lungs but can develop with low vascular pressure or high alveolar pressure.
- **Zone 2 (P_a > P_A > P_v):** flow depends more on the arterial-to-alveolar pressure difference, sometimes called the *waterfall* relationship.
- **Zone 3 (P_a > P_v > P_A):** vessels remain distended and flow depends predominantly on arterial-to-venous pressure difference.

With gravity, vascular pressures usually increase toward the lung bases, which is one reason perfusion generally rises from apex to base in an upright person. **West zones are pressure relationships**, not fixed anatomical stripes.

### Link perfusion to ventilation

Air does not necessarily reach every alveolus in the same proportion as blood. The **ventilation–perfusion ratio (V/Q)** compares air delivery to blood delivery. Both ventilation and perfusion generally increase toward the dependent lung, but perfusion usually increases more steeply, making V/Q relatively higher toward the apex. An alveolus receiving too little ventilation relative to blood has low alveolar oxygen. Nearby pulmonary arterioles then constrict, diverting some flow toward better-ventilated alveoli.

**Clinical reasoning:** If only one small region is hypoxic, redistribution can improve matching. If nearly every alveolus is hypoxic, there is nowhere well-ventilated to redirect flow; widespread constriction instead raises pulmonary resistance, loading the right ventricle.

### Pulmonary oedema: where the fluid goes

Left ventricular dysfunction can raise left atrial and pulmonary venous pressure. This increases pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure, encouraging excess filtration into lung interstitium. Once barrier and lymphatic clearance are overwhelmed, fluid may enter alveoli and impair gas exchange. This is different from the **normal** modest filtration controlled by the pulmonary capillary barrier.

**Understanding checkpoint:** Why can exercise raise pulmonary flow without a proportionate pressure increase? *Recruitment and distension create more effective pathways for flow and lower resistance.*

## Chapter Essentials

1. Right and left outputs are equal over time.
2. Low pulmonary resistance reflects recruitment and distension.
3. Gravity changes regional perfusion and West-zone relationships.
4. Low alveolar oxygen causes local pulmonary vasoconstriction.
5. Diffuse hypoxia and high venous pressure can impair lung function.
