### Q1
Chapter: Chapter 16 — Local Control of Blood Flow
Section: 1. Why does a tissue need to control its own blood flow?
Type: application
Stem: A resting muscle suddenly becomes active while systemic arterial pressure stays steady. What most directly increases its blood supply?
A. Its local capillaries develop stronger contractions.
B. Its arteries increase their driving pressure.
C. Its large veins reduce their compliance.
D. Its local arterioles reduce their resistance.
Answer: D
Why: Flow = pressure difference ÷ resistance. With arterial pressure unchanged, the only way to get more flow is lower resistance, and the resistance that tissues control is in their arterioles. Metabolites from the working muscle relax those arterioles.
Not A: Capillaries lack the smooth muscle needed for this response.
Not B: The stem states that systemic arterial pressure remains steady.
Not C: Vein compliance mainly affects stored volume, not the main resistance to flow.

B. It becomes sixteen times its original value.

Answer: B
Why: Vascular resistance varies inversely with the fourth power of radius; halving radius increases resistance by 2⁴ = 16.
Not A: Eightfold change would follow a cubic, not fourth-power relation.
Not C: Twofold change assumes a linear relation with radius.
Not D: Fourfold change would follow a square, not fourth-power relation.

### Q3
Chapter: Chapter 16 — Local Control of Blood Flow
Section: 2. Autoregulation: steady flow despite changing pressure
Type: mechanism
Stem: Perfusion pressure to a tissue falls moderately but remains in the autoregulatory range. What compensatory change is expected?
A. Arteriolar constriction that limits the fall in flow.
B. Arteriolar dilation that limits the fall in flow.
C. Venous dilation that increases upstream resistance.
D. Capillary contraction that restores the lost pressure.
Answer: B
Why: When perfusion pressure falls, the arteriolar wall is stretched less (myogenic) and metabolites build up (metabolic). Both relax the arterioles, lowering resistance so flow stays close to normal despite the lower pressure.
Not A: Constriction would worsen the reduction in flow.
Not C: Venous dilation does not directly lower arteriolar resistance.
Not D: Capillaries are not the contractile resistance vessels.

### Q7

Type: data-interpretation
Stem: Two cylindrical vessels have equal transmural pressure, but vessel X has twice the radius of vessel Y. How do their required wall tensions compare?
A. Both vessels require exactly the same wall tension.
B. Vessel X requires half the wall tension of Y.
C. Vessel X requires four times the tension of Y.
D. Vessel X requires twice the wall tension of Y.
Answer: D
Why: For a cylinder, wall tension T = P × r. With equal transmural pressure, the vessel with twice the radius needs twice the tension. This is why a capillary’s tiny radius lets a one-cell wall withstand blood pressure.
Not A: Equal pressure alone does not make tension equal.
Not B: Half the tension would apply to half the radius.
Not C: A fourfold difference does not follow the linear radius relation.
