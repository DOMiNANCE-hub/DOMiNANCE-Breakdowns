### Q1
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 2. Where does the blood come from?
Type: mechanism
Stem: Occlusion of a vertebral artery threatens which upstream source of the posterior cerebral circulation?
A. The external carotid pathway.
B. The basilar artery pathway.
C. The anterior communicating artery.
D. The superior sagittal sinus.
Answer: B
Why: The two vertebral arteries unite as the basilar artery, which supplies the posterior circulation.
Not A: The external carotid primarily serves extracranial tissues.
Not C: The anterior communicating artery links anterior cerebral arteries.
Not D: The sagittal sinus drains venous blood rather than supplying arteries.

### Q2
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 2. Where does the blood come from?
Type: application
Stem: A patient has a large infarct affecting the lateral surface of one cerebral hemisphere. Which arterial territory is most likely?
A. Anterior cerebral artery.
B. Posterior cerebral artery.
C. Middle cerebral artery.
D. Anterior communicating artery.
Answer: C
Why: The middle cerebral artery supplies most of the lateral cerebral hemisphere, making it the relevant territory.
Not A: The anterior cerebral artery mainly supplies medial frontal and parietal surfaces.
Not B: The posterior cerebral artery predominantly supplies occipital and inferior temporal territories.
Not D: The communicating artery is a collateral link rather than the main lateral supply.

### Q3
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 2. Where does the blood come from?
Type: mechanism
Stem: Why can a cerebral arterial branch occlusion cause a local infarct despite the Circle of Willis?
A. Small distal territories may have limited effective collateral flow.
B. Every cerebral artery has a complete set of parallel branches.
C. Cerebral veins normally replace arterial inflow after blockage.
D. The basilar artery can supply every distal cortical branch directly.
Answer: A
Why: The Circle of Willis provides proximal connections, but distal cerebral arterial territories have limited functional anastomoses.
Not B: Distal vessels are not completely interconnected.
Not C: Veins drain blood and cannot substitute for oxygenated arterial supply.
Not D: Basilar inflow does not directly replace all distal arterial territories.

A. The foramina of Luschka.

Not C: The aqueduct connects third and fourth ventricles.
Not D: Magendie is the median fourth-ventricle outlet.

### Q6
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 5. The coverings, the ventricles and CSF
Type: mechanism
Stem: A narrowed cerebral aqueduct obstructs CSF passage. Which change would be expected upstream?
A. Enlargement limited to the fourth ventricle.
B. Immediate excess flow into the subarachnoid space.
C. Expansion of lateral and third ventricles.
D. Isolated enlargement of the central canal.
Answer: C
Why: The aqueduct lies between the third and fourth ventricles, so upstream lateral and third ventricles enlarge.
Not A: The fourth ventricle lies downstream of the block.
Not B: Obstruction decreases onward drainage into subarachnoid space.
Not D: The central canal is downstream and not the primary dilated compartment.

### Q7
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 5. The coverings, the ventricles and CSF
Type: mechanism
Stem: Once CSF reaches the subarachnoid space, how is it returned mainly to the venous circulation?
A. Through arachnoid villi into dural venous sinuses.
B. Through pia mater directly into cerebral arteries.
C. Through the foramen of Monro into the veins.
D. Through the cerebral aqueduct into the aorta.
Answer: A
Why: Arachnoid villi and granulations return CSF to the dural venous sinuses.
Not B: Pia does not discharge CSF into cerebral arteries.
Not C: Monro connects ventricles and is not the absorption site.
Not D: The aqueduct joins ventricles, not the systemic arterial circuit.

Stem: The CSF volume is about 150 mL and production is 550 mL/day. Approximately how many complete turnovers occur per day?
A. About one.
B. About two.

Answer: D

Not A: One turnover would represent only about 150 mL/day.
Not B: Two turnovers would require about 300 mL/day.
Not C: Six turnovers would correspond to around 900 mL/day.

### Q9

Type: conceptual
Stem: How does CSF substantially reduce the effective weight of the brain?
A. It increases the brain's intrinsic density.
B. It provides buoyancy that offsets gravitational loading.
C. It directly reduces the brain's oxygen consumption.
D. It reduces blood volume to near zero.
Answer: B
Why: Immersion provides buoyancy, reducing the brain's effective supported weight from roughly 1400–1500 g to around 50 g.
Not A: The tissue density does not increase to create buoyancy.
Not C: Lower oxygen consumption does not explain mechanical support.
Not D: Brain blood volume remains substantial.

### Q10

Stem: A patient has hydrocephalus due to impaired absorption by arachnoid villi without an obstructed ventricular pathway. What type is this?
A. Noncommunicating hydrocephalus.
B. Obstructive aqueductal hydrocephalus.
C. Communicating hydrocephalus.
D. Isolated ventricular haemorrhage.
Answer: C
Why: Communicating hydrocephalus occurs when CSF reaches subarachnoid space but reabsorption is impaired.
Not A: Noncommunicating hydrocephalus results from blocked ventricular drainage.
Not B: Aqueductal stenosis is a specific obstruction.
Not D: Haemorrhage may contribute to hydrocephalus but is not the classification described.

### Q12

Type: anatomy
Stem: Which structure forms the key physical restriction to paracellular movement across the blood–brain barrier?
A. Astrocytic end-feet alone.
B. The choroid plexus epithelium.
C. Loose junctions of cerebral venules.
D. Tight junctions between brain capillary endothelial cells.
Answer: D
Why: Tight junctions in cerebral capillary endothelium restrict movement between cells; astrocytes support barrier function.
Not A: Astrocytes help maintain the barrier but are not the main sealed endothelial layer.
Not B: The choroid plexus forms a separate blood–CSF barrier.
Not C: Loose venular junctions would not account for selective restriction.

Stem: How does glucose enter the brain despite the restrictive blood–brain barrier?
A. By free movement through tight junctions.

Not C: Protein leakage is limited by the barrier.
Not D: Cerebral glucose transport is largely insulin independent.

### Q16
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 4. A rigid box: intracranial pressure and perfusion
Type: calculation
Stem: Intracranial pressure rises while mean arterial pressure remains constant. What happens to cerebral perfusion pressure?
A. It rises by the same amount.
B. It remains fixed by cardiac output.
C. It becomes equal to venous return.
D. It falls because ICP opposes arterial driving pressure.
Answer: D
Why: Cerebral perfusion pressure is approximately mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure; raising ICP reduces it.
Not A: Increased ICP subtracts from the arterial driving pressure.
Not B: Autoregulation does not make the pressure difference mathematically constant.
Not C: Venous return and cerebral perfusion pressure are different quantities.
Slides: 22–23; Chapter 22 §4

Stem: A severe rise in intracranial pressure produces systemic hypertension with reflex bradycardia. What is this response?
A. Bainbridge reflex.
B. Cushing reflex.
C. Atrial stretch reflex.
D. Pulmonary hypoxic vasoconstriction.
Answer: B
Why: The Cushing response reflects brainstem ischaemia driving systemic hypertension, with baroreflex-mediated bradycardia.
Not A: Bainbridge responds to increased atrial filling.
Not C: Atrial stretch does not explain raised ICP-driven hypertension.
Not D: Pulmonary hypoxic vasoconstriction is a local lung response.

### Q18
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 7. Stroke
Type: comparison
Stem: A cerebral vessel ruptures and blood enters brain tissue. What stroke mechanism is present?
A. Thrombotic ischaemia.
B. Embolic ischaemia.
C. Haemorrhagic stroke.
D. Transient cerebral hypoglycaemia.
Answer: C
Why: Haemorrhagic stroke results from rupture of a vessel, whereas ischaemic stroke results from interrupted arterial supply.
Not A: Thrombosis obstructs an artery instead of rupturing it.
Not B: Embolism blocks flow rather than directly representing vessel rupture.
Not D: Hypoglycaemia causes global dysfunction rather than vessel rupture.

### Q19
Chapter: Chapter 22 — Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier
Section: 1. Why the brain can't wait for blood
Type: application
Stem: Why can a brief complete interruption of cerebral blood flow cause unconsciousness within seconds?
A. The brain has very limited stores of oxygen and fuel.
B. The brain stores enough ATP for several minutes of activity.
C. The cerebral circulation can switch immediately to venous oxygen.
D. The brainstem normally stops all electrical activity first.
Answer: A
Why: Neural activity depends on continuous perfusion because energy and oxygen reserves are small; the lecture cites roughly 10 seconds.
Not B: Adequate stored energy does not sustain function for many minutes.
Not C: Venous blood cannot replace arterial oxygen delivery.
Not D: The lecture describes cortical vulnerability, with brainstem structures relatively more resistant.
