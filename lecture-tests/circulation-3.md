### Q1
Chapter: Chapter 19 — Gravity, Posture and Acceleration
Section: 1. Gravity adds pressure below the heart and takes it away above
Type: calculation
Stem: A vessel is 50 cm below heart level in an upright patient. Approximately how much hydrostatic pressure does gravity add?
A. About 19 mmHg.
B. About 39 mmHg.
C. About 77 mmHg.
D. About 100 mmHg.
Answer: B
Why: The approved chapter gives 0.77 mmHg per centimetre; 50 × 0.77 ≈ 39 mmHg.
Not A: Nineteen mmHg corresponds to roughly 25 cm.
Not C: Seventy-seven mmHg corresponds to roughly 100 cm.
Not D: One hundred mmHg is much larger than the calculated gradient.

### Q2
Chapter: Chapter 19 — Gravity, Posture and Acceleration
Section: 2. What standing up does to the circulation
Type: mechanism
Stem: A student stands abruptly. What links pooling in the leg veins to a lower stroke volume?
A. Reduced venous return lowers end-diastolic volume.
B. Increased afterload raises end-diastolic volume.
C. Higher leg pressure directly raises coronary flow.
D. Reduced venous pressure increases ventricular filling.
Answer: A
Why: Blood pooling lowers venous return and ventricular filling; Frank–Starling physiology then reduces stroke volume.
Not B: Afterload is not the primary cause of reduced initial filling.
Not C: Leg pressure does not directly explain the fall in stroke volume.
Not D: Venous return falls, rather than increasing, after pooling.
Slides: Circulation 3, slides 3–4; Chapter 19 §2

### Q3
Chapter: Chapter 19 — Gravity, Posture and Acceleration
Section: 3. How the body rescues blood pressure
Type: mechanism
Stem: Which reflex pattern helps restore arterial pressure during the first seconds of standing?
A. More baroreceptor firing and less sympathetic activity.
B. More baroreceptor firing and more vagal activity.
C. Less baroreceptor firing and more sympathetic activity.
D. Less baroreceptor firing and more vagal activity.
Answer: C
Why: Falling arterial stretch reduces baroreceptor firing, increasing sympathetic drive and reducing vagal influence.
Not A: Pressure falls, so baroreceptor firing does not increase.
Not B: Greater vagal activity would slow the heart.
Not D: Vagal activity falls rather than rising during compensation.
Slides: Circulation 3, slide 4; Chapter 19 §3

### Q4
Chapter: Chapter 19 — Gravity, Posture and Acceleration
Section: 6. Acceleration: when “gravity” gets stronger
Type: application
Stem: Why can vision fail before consciousness during strong positive acceleration?
A. The retina receives blood at higher pressure than the heart.
B. Positive acceleration shifts blood toward the head.
C. Intracranial pressure always exceeds arterial pressure.
D. Retinal perfusion falls as blood shifts toward the feet.
Answer: D
Why: Positive g causes leg pooling and reduced pressure at eye level, so retinal perfusion can fail before cerebral consciousness.
Not A: Pressure at eye level decreases during positive g.
Not B: Headward blood movement occurs during negative g.
Not C: The mechanism is reduced eye-level arterial perfusion, not an inevitable ICP excess.
Slides: Circulation 3, slide 9; Chapter 19 §6

### Q5
Chapter: Chapter 20 — Circulatory Shock
Section: 1. What is shock?
Type: conceptual
Stem: A bleeding patient has a nearly normal arterial pressure but cold skin and reduced urine output. What is the best conclusion?
A. Normal pressure excludes significant shock.
B. Low tissue perfusion can coexist with maintained pressure.
C. Cardiac output must be above its pre-bleeding level.
D. Blood flow to all organs must remain normal.
Answer: B
Why: Sympathetic arteriolar constriction can maintain arterial pressure despite reduced cardiac output and poor perfusion of some tissues.
Not A: Shock is defined by inadequate tissue perfusion, not one pressure value.
Not C: Haemorrhage reduces venous return and usually lowers output.
Not D: Flow can be redistributed away from skin and kidneys.
Slides: Circulation 3, slides 12–13; Chapter 20 §1

### Q6
Chapter: Chapter 20 — Circulatory Shock
Section: 2. Haemorrhage: the model for all shock
Type: mechanism
Stem: Which chain best describes the early circulatory consequences of severe haemorrhage?
A. Reduced blood volume → reduced venous return → reduced stroke volume.
B. Reduced blood volume → increased venous return → reduced stroke volume.
C. Reduced blood volume → increased ventricular filling → increased output.
D. Reduced blood volume → increased plasma volume → increased output.
Answer: A
Why: Blood loss reduces central venous filling, lowering end-diastolic volume, stroke volume and cardiac output.
Not B: Venous return falls rather than rises after blood loss.
Not C: Ventricular filling decreases with reduced circulating volume.
Not D: Haemorrhage does not immediately expand plasma volume.
Slides: Circulation 3, slide 18; Chapter 20 §2

### Q7
Chapter: Chapter 20 — Circulatory Shock
Section: 3. What you see, and why
Type: mechanism
Stem: Why is the pulse often fast but weak in compensated hypovolaemic shock?
A. Reduced sympathetic drive combines with increased stroke volume.
B. Increased vagal drive combines with reduced afterload.
C. Increased sympathetic drive combines with reduced stroke volume.
D. Reduced vagal drive combines with increased ventricular filling.
Answer: C
Why: Baroreceptor unloading raises sympathetic activity and heart rate, while low filling produces a small stroke volume and weak pulse.
Not A: Sympathetic drive rises, not falls, during compensation.
Not B: Vagal influence generally decreases, not increases.
Not D: Ventricular filling is reduced after haemorrhage.
Slides: Circulation 3, slides 19–20; Chapter 20 §3

### Q8
Chapter: Chapter 20 — Circulatory Shock
Section: 5. When shock feeds on itself: progressive and irreversible shock
Type: mechanism
Stem: In late shock, precapillary sphincters relax while venules remain constricted. What is the consequence?
A. Reduced capillary pressure and greater fluid absorption.
B. Increased venous drainage and restored circulating volume.
C. Reduced filtration and improved tissue oxygen delivery.
D. Capillary stasis, raised pressure and interstitial fluid loss.
Answer: D
Why: Blood enters capillaries but drains poorly; rising capillary hydrostatic pressure drives more fluid into tissues.
Not A: Impaired outflow raises, rather than reduces, capillary pressure.
Not B: Venular constriction obstructs drainage rather than improving it.
Not C: The lecture describes greater fluid loss, not reduced filtration.
Slides: Circulation 3, slides 27–28; Chapter 20 §5

### Q9
Chapter: Chapter 21 — Heart Failure Physiology
Section: 1. What is heart failure?
Type: conceptual
Stem: A patient has near-normal resting cardiac output but very high ventricular filling pressures and pulmonary congestion. Which statement fits best?
A. Normal resting output excludes heart failure.
B. Heart failure requires reduced ejection fraction.
C. High filling pressure proves adequate pumping.
D. Heart failure can exist despite near-normal resting output.
Answer: D
Why: Heart failure includes states where output is maintained only at abnormally high filling pressures, causing congestion.
Not A: A near-normal output can be maintained at harmful filling pressures.
Not B: Diastolic failure can occur with preserved ejection fraction.
Not C: High filling pressures can be evidence of dysfunction rather than normality.
Slides: Circulation 3, slide 32; Chapter 21 §1

### Q10
Chapter: Chapter 21 — Heart Failure Physiology
Section: 2. Systolic and diastolic failure: can't squeeze, or can't fill?
Type: comparison
Stem: A stiff ventricle has high filling pressure but a preserved ejection fraction. What is the primary defect?
A. Impaired relaxation and filling.
B. Increased contractility and ejection.
C. Reduced electrical activation of the SA node.
D. Excessive ventricular compliance.
Answer: A
Why: Diastolic failure is impaired relaxation or stiff filling; ejection fraction may remain preserved.
Not B: Increased contractility is not the primary defect.
Not C: SA-node dysfunction does not define diastolic heart failure.
Not D: A stiff ventricle has reduced, not excessive, compliance.

### Q11
Chapter: Chapter 21 — Heart Failure Physiology
Section: 3. Left versus right failure: where does the blood back up?
Type: mechanism
Stem: What directly promotes pulmonary oedema in left-sided backward heart failure?
A. Reduced systemic venous pressure.
B. Raised pulmonary venous and capillary pressure.
C. Reduced pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure.
D. Raised right atrial pressure without lung congestion.
Answer: B
Why: Left ventricular failure increases left atrial and pulmonary venous pressures, driving filtration into the lungs.
Not A: Systemic venous pressure is more closely linked to right-sided failure.
Not C: Lower capillary hydrostatic pressure would oppose filtration.
Not D: Right atrial pressure alone does not explain pulmonary oedema.
Slides: Circulation 3, slide 34; Chapter 21 §3

### Q12
Chapter: Chapter 21 — Heart Failure Physiology
Section: 4. What does the body do about it, and why does that make it worse?
Type: mechanism
Stem: Why can prolonged renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation worsen heart-failure oedema?
A. It causes sustained urinary sodium loss.
B. It decreases circulating volume and filling pressure.
C. It retains sodium and water, increasing venous congestion.
D. It prevents compensatory arterial vasoconstriction.
Answer: C
Why: RAAS initially supports blood pressure, but prolonged salt and water retention increases filling pressures and oedema.
Not A: Aldosterone promotes sodium retention rather than loss.
Not B: RAAS tends to expand, not deplete, extracellular volume.
Not D: Angiotensin II supports rather than blocks vasoconstriction.
