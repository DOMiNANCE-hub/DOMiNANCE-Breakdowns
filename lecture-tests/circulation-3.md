### Q1
Chapter: Chapter 19 — Gravity, Posture and Acceleration
Section: 1. Gravity adds pressure below the heart and takes it away above
Type: calculation
Stem: A subject stands upright without compensatory changes. The brain is 40 cm above heart level. By approximately how much does gravity lower arterial pressure at the brain?
A. 15 mmHg lower than at heart level.
B. 31 mmHg lower than at heart level.
C. 46 mmHg lower than at heart level.
D. 77 mmHg lower than at heart level.
Answer: B
Why: Blood produces a hydrostatic gradient of about 0.77 mmHg per centimetre. A 40-cm upward difference therefore subtracts approximately 40 × 0.77 = 30.8 mmHg.
Not A: This corresponds to about 0.38 mmHg/cm, which is not the stated gradient.
Not C: This assumes a gradient of about 1.15 mmHg/cm rather than 0.77.
Not D: This is the pressure difference for about 100 cm, not 40 cm.

C. Lower circulating volume reduces venous return and reverses all congestion.
D. Reduced arterial resistance eliminates myocardial work in the failing heart.
Answer: A
Why: Angiotensin II increases vascular resistance, while aldosterone promotes sodium and water retention. These help preserve pressure short term but load the failing ventricle and worsen fluid overload.
Not B: The RAAS responses here favour constriction and retention, not dilation and natriuresis.
Not C: RAAS expands rather than depletes volume in this setting.
Not D: Increased afterload means the ventricle must work against greater resistance.
