# Electrocardiography and Basic Rhythms

## What the tracing measures
The surface **electrocardiogram (ECG)** records voltage differences generated as cardiac electrical activation and recovery spread through the body. It does not directly measure contraction, stroke volume or blood pressure.

A standard 12-lead ECG uses electrodes to produce **12 views**: bipolar limb leads I, II, III; augmented limb leads aVR, aVL, aVF; and chest leads V₁–V₆. Each lead views depolarization along a different direction. A depolarization vector generally travelling toward a positive recording electrode gives a positive deflection.

## Waves and intervals
| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| P wave | Atrial depolarization |
| PR interval | Onset of atrial to onset of ventricular depolarization, normally about 0.12–0.20 s |
| QRS complex | Ventricular depolarization, typically less than 0.12 s |
| ST segment | Period when much of ventricular myocardium is depolarized; significant deviation may indicate injury/ischemia in context |
| T wave | Ventricular repolarization |
| QT interval | Ventricular depolarization through repolarization; varies with heart rate |

Atrial repolarization is usually obscured by the larger QRS complex. The occasional U wave has more than one proposed physiological origin.

## ECG paper and heart-rate calculation
At **25 mm/s**, each 1 mm horizontal square is **0.04 s**, each 5 mm large square **0.20 s**. Standard gain is **10 mm/mV**; thus 1 small vertical square is **0.1 mV**.

For a regular rhythm, heart rate ≈ **300 ÷ number of large squares between R waves**, or **1500 ÷ small squares**. If R–R separation is 4 large squares, HR ≈ 75 beats/min. Irregular rhythms require a longer sampled time window and appropriate averaging.

## Basic conduction problems
**First-degree AV block** has prolonged PR without dropped QRS complexes. **Second-degree** block intermittently fails to conduct atrial impulses. **Third-degree** block involves AV dissociation. These illustrate conduction principles and are supplementary to the supplied introductory ECG slides.

## Understand limb leads instead of memorising letter names
**Lead I = left arm (+) − right arm (−)**. **Lead II = left leg (+) − right arm (−)**. **Lead III = left leg (+) − left arm (−)**. This yields **Einthoven's law: II = I + III** for simultaneously recorded limb lead voltages. The augmented unipolar limb leads are **aVR** (views from right arm), **aVL** (left arm) and **aVF** (foot/left leg). The right-leg electrode ordinarily serves as a ground/reference, not a 12-lead recording viewpoint.

The six chest electrodes sample the horizontal plane: **V1** fourth right intercostal space at sternal border; **V2** fourth left intercostal space; **V4** fifth left intercostal space at midclavicular line; **V3** halfway between V2 and V4; **V5** anterior axillary line level with V4; **V6** midaxillary line level with V4. In practice position V4 before V3. These locations matter because placement errors can change the apparent tracing.

## Distinguish waves, segments and intervals
The **PR interval** spans P onset to QRS onset and includes atrial depolarisation plus AV transmission; PR prolongation may indicate slowed AV conduction. The **PR segment** is the relatively flat period after the P wave before QRS. **QRS** reflects ventricular depolarisation; simultaneous atrial repolarisation is usually concealed. The **ST segment** lies between the QRS and T wave and roughly corresponds to a period during which much ventricular myocardium remains depolarised. The **QT interval** includes ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation.

A **corrected QT (QTc)** adjusts for rate. The Bazett approximation is **QTc = QT/√RR**, with QT and RR expressed in seconds. It can overcorrect at high rates and undercorrect at low rates, so interpretation needs context; do not treat an uncorrected QT duration as universally normal at every HR. The **U wave**, when visible, has debated origins.

## Work a paper-speed example
At **25 mm/s**, each small horizontal square = **0.04 seconds**, each large square = **0.20 seconds**. At **10 mm/mV**, each small vertical square = **0.1 mV**. A regular rhythm with R waves separated by **22 small squares** has HR ≈ **1500/22 = 68 beats/min** (the lecturer's example). For irregular rhythms, counting complexes over a longer strip gives a more useful average than a single R–R interval.

**Practice:** Why is the ECG not equivalent to a pressure or contraction graph? It records **electrical potentials**, which precede and coordinate the mechanical contraction but are not the mechanical event itself.
