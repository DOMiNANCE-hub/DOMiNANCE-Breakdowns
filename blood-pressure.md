# Arterial Blood Pressure

## What the numbers mean
**Systolic blood pressure (SBP)** is peak arterial pressure during ventricular ejection. **Diastolic blood pressure (DBP)** is the lowest pressure before the next ejection. **Pulse pressure (PP) = SBP − DBP**; for 120/80 mmHg, PP = 40 mmHg.

Mean arterial pressure (**MAP**) is the cycle-average arterial pressure. At ordinary heart rates **MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓(PP)**, so for 120/80 it is about 93 mmHg. This approximation becomes less accurate with major changes in heart rate or waveform shape.

## Where pressure comes from
In the systemic circuit **MAP − right atrial pressure ≈ CO × systemic vascular resistance**. Thus BP depends not just on HR, but on SV/CO, vascular resistance, arterial compliance and blood volume. Aortic elasticity maintains pressure between ventricular beats.

## Measurement principles
A cuff is inflated above systolic pressure and then slowly deflated. The first characteristic **Korotkoff sound** is used for systolic pressure and disappearance of sounds (phase V) usually indicates diastolic pressure in adults. Use an appropriately sized cuff, a supported arm at heart level, and proper rest; wrong cuff size and arm position bias the reading.

## Clinical reasoning
An increased stroke volume or stiffer large arteries may widen pulse pressure. Strong arteriolar constriction raises resistance, potentially raising MAP even if cardiac output falls.

## Correct cuff technique and gravity effects
The reference BP is reported for the cuff **near heart level**. Hydrostatic pressure changes by roughly **0.77 mmHg per vertical centimetre** in a static blood column; keeping the arm much lower than the heart can give a falsely elevated reading. A cuff too **small** tends to overestimate BP, while a cuff too **large** can underestimate it. The patient should rest, avoid talking, support the arm and use the appropriate cuff size.

**Palpation** can estimate systolic pressure from reappearance of the distal pulse while deflating the cuff, but cannot directly establish diastolic pressure. With **auscultation**, the first clear Korotkoff sound estimates systolic, and disappearance (usually phase V in adults) estimates diastolic. Sounds arise when cuff pressure partly compresses the artery and causes disturbed/intermittent flow.

## Interpret arterial pressure through the circulation
Aortic pressure oscillates because the heart ejects periodically, but **elastic arterial recoil** maintains flow during diastole. MAP falls substantially across resistance vessels, particularly arterioles; the pressure profile in the lecturer's diagram (slide 83) illustrates why capillaries are protected from high arterial pressure. **Pulse pressure** depends strongly on stroke volume and arterial compliance. **MAP** is more strongly related to CO × peripheral resistance (with central venous pressure taken into account).

If a patient loses blood, the **baroreflex** can temporarily raise resistance enough to maintain MAP even with falling SV. This explains why a near-normal BP cannot exclude compensated shock.

**Practice:** Why could two people have the same MAP but different pulse pressures? Their arterial compliance and SV/wave reflection may differ even when the time-averaged pressure is similar.
