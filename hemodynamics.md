# Blood Flow and Hemodynamics

## Flow, velocity and pressure are not synonyms
**Volume flow (Q)** is volume per time (mL/s); **mean velocity (v)** is distance per time (cm/s). They relate through **Q = v × cross-sectional area**. Since total cross-sectional area is greatest in systemic capillaries, average blood velocity there is low even though the entire capillary network carries the cardiac output.

For a vascular segment **Q = ΔP/R**. Pressure falls along the circulation as energy is dissipated against resistance; arterioles account for a major pressure drop and are key resistance vessels.

## Why radius dominates resistance
For laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid in a rigid cylindrical tube, **Poiseuille's law** gives **R = 8ηL/(πr⁴)**, where η = viscosity, L = length and r = radius. Halving radius increases resistance about **16-fold** under these idealised assumptions. Real blood vessels are compliant and blood is non-Newtonian, so the equation illustrates the strong effect of arteriolar calibre rather than predicting every biological flow precisely.

## Laminar versus turbulent flow
Laminar flow has organised layers; axial flow is generally fastest near the centre. Turbulence becomes more likely with increased velocity, increased diameter or decreased viscosity and can generate murmurs or bruits. The **Reynolds number** compares inertial and viscous effects; it is a guide, not a universal cutoff in pulsatile vascular networks.

## Compliance and blood storage
**Compliance = change in volume/change in pressure**. Veins are highly compliant **capacitance vessels** and hold much of the circulating blood at rest. Large elastic arteries store energy during systole and release it during diastole, smoothing flow (Windkessel effect).

## Distinguish velocity from volumetric flow in one vascular tree
In the ideal series circulation, the **same mean volume of blood per minute** passes the aorta and combined systemic capillaries. The capillary network has an enormous **total cross-sectional area**, so its mean velocity is low despite unchanged total Q. Vessel branching does not automatically mean a smaller *total* flow. A pulsatile elastic aorta stores part of systolic stroke volume and recoils in diastole (**Windkessel effect**).

## Where turbulent sounds come from
For idealised tube flow, **Re = ρvD/η**, where ρ is fluid density, v mean velocity, D tube diameter, and η viscosity. A narrowing can make the local velocity rise enough to favour turbulence, and a fall in viscosity in anaemia can also favour turbulence. This is why turbulent **Korotkoff sounds** appear as an external cuff partly compresses a brachial artery. For a particular total flow, the local constricted segment can have high velocity even though its diameter has decreased.

**Hagen–Poiseuille:** **Q = (πr⁴ ΔP)/(8ηL)**, so **R = 8ηL/(πr⁴)** under laminar, rigid, cylindrical assumptions. If radius falls from 2 mm to 1 mm and ΔP is held constant, theoretical flow becomes **one-sixteenth**. Real vascular beds adapt through active tone and parallel branching.

## Blood is not a simple fluid
Apparent blood viscosity depends on **haematocrit**, plasma proteins, red-cell deformability, temperature and vessel size/flow conditions. **Polycythaemia** increases viscosity and may raise circulatory workload; anaemia lowers viscosity but reduces oxygen delivery, often eliciting a higher cardiac output. In small vessels, red-cell axial migration and the Fåhræus–Lindqvist phenomenon modify apparent viscosity. The lecturer includes artery–vein compliance contrasts (slide 74): a much larger increase in **venous volume** can occur with only a small pressure rise, whereas arteries are relatively stiff.

**Practice:** Why are capillaries the slow-flow region although each capillary is narrow? Because their **combined cross-sectional area** is much larger than the aortic area.
