# The Cardiac Cycle

## Track pressures and valves, not memorized lists
One heartbeat repeats phases of filling, pressure generation, ejection and relaxation. A valve opens only when pressure **upstream exceeds downstream**; it closes when the gradient reverses. Think of the Wiggers diagram as simultaneous traces of atrial, ventricular and aortic pressure, ventricular volume, ECG and heart sounds.

## Sequence of phases
1. **Ventricular filling:** AV valves open; semilunar valves closed. Most filling is passive in a resting healthy adult.
2. **Atrial systole:** the atria add the final volume (often around 10–30% at rest, variable with age, rate and ventricular stiffness).
3. **Isovolumetric contraction:** ventricular depolarization precedes contraction; ventricular pressure rises, AV valves close (**S₁**) and semilunar valves remain closed. Volume does not change because all valves are closed.
4. **Ejection:** when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic/pulmonary pressure, semilunar valves open. **Rapid ejection** is followed by reduced ejection; ventricular volume falls.
5. **Isovolumetric relaxation:** when arterial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure, semilunar valves close (**S₂**). All valves are shut while ventricular pressure drops at constant end-systolic volume.
6. **Filling resumes** once ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure.

## Volumes and relationships
**End-diastolic volume (EDV)** is the volume before ejection; **end-systolic volume (ESV)** remains afterward. **Stroke volume (SV) = EDV − ESV**. **Ejection fraction (EF) = SV/EDV × 100%**. For example, EDV 120 mL and ESV 50 mL produce SV 70 mL and EF about 58%.

Increasing heart rate shortens diastole disproportionately, so very rapid rhythms reduce filling and may compromise coronary perfusion.

## One useful clinical connection
During physiological inspiration, right ventricular filling/ejection can last longer, delaying pulmonary valve closure relative to aortic valve closure: **physiological splitting of S₂**.

**Check:** Ventricular pressure rises rapidly, but ventricular volume stays constant. Which phase is this, and why?

## Read the Wiggers diagram as events, not curves to memorise
The lecturer's **Wiggers diagram (Circulation 1, slide 21)** aligns ECG, aortic pressure, ventricular pressure, atrial pressure, ventricular volume and heart sounds. The P wave precedes atrial contraction. The QRS precedes rapid ventricular pressure rise. The **first heart sound (S1)** accompanies AV valve closure near the beginning of ventricular systole; the **second sound (S2)** accompanies semilunar closure at the end of ejection. The T wave occurs around late ventricular systole as ventricles repolarise.

**Isovolumetric contraction:** all four valves are closed; ventricular pressure rises while volume remains near end-diastolic volume (EDV). This begins when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure and closes the AV valves. **Rapid ejection** begins once ventricular pressure exceeds aortic/pulmonary arterial pressure, followed by **reduced ejection**. The lecture slide labelled ejection initially slow then rapid; the typical sequence is **rapid then reduced**.

**Isovolumetric relaxation:** both sets of valves again are closed; ventricular pressure falls without a volume change. Once ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, AV valves open and **rapid filling**, **diastasis** (reduced filling) and **atrial systole** follow. Atrial contraction ordinarily adds a **minority** of ventricular filling at rest, but its contribution grows in some states such as increased heart rate or impaired relaxation.

## Use the pressure–volume loop to reason through disease
Loop width is **stroke volume = EDV − ESV**. Loop area approximates external stroke work. Increased **preload** with preserved function increases EDV and usually SV through Frank–Starling. Increased **afterload** makes the ventricle eject against a higher pressure, tending to increase ESV and reduce SV at a fixed contractile state. Increased **contractility** decreases ESV and shifts the end-systolic relation up/left, increasing SV.

At high heart rates, **diastole shortens disproportionately**, reducing filling time and the time available for left coronary perfusion. Therefore extreme tachycardia can reduce SV even though HR increases.

**Practice:** At the instant just after AV valves close but before aortic opening, what changes? **Pressure rises, volume does not**: isovolumetric contraction.
