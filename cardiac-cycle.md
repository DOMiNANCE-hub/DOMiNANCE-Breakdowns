# The Cardiac Cycle

**Lecture:** CIRCULATION 1(4).pdf, slides 11–21. **Supplementary explanation:** detailed valve-pressure reasoning, Wiggers interpretation, and visual teaching aids.

## 1. Understanding the cardiac cycle

The **cardiac cycle** is the sequence of electrical and mechanical events in one heartbeat: myocardial contraction and relaxation, changes in chamber pressures and volumes, and movement of blood.

The central rule is that **heart valves open and close because of pressure differences across them**. Blood passes through an open valve from higher to lower pressure. The **mitral valve opens** when left atrial pressure exceeds left ventricular pressure; the **aortic valve opens** when left ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure. When the pressure difference reverses, a valve closes to prevent backward flow.

Valves are not individually opened by nerve impulses. Electrical activation changes myocardial contraction, which changes pressure and consequently controls the valves. To reason through each phase, ask: **What is the pressure difference? Which valves are open? Is ventricular volume changing?**

The cycle is continuous: **filling → pressure generation → ejection → relaxation → filling again.**

## 3. Ventricular filling and atrial systole

During ventricular diastole, the myocardium relaxes and ventricular pressure falls. When atrial pressure becomes greater than ventricular pressure, the **AV valves open** and blood enters the ventricles. The semilunar valves remain closed because pressure in the great arteries is still greater than pressure inside the ventricles.

### Rapid ventricular filling

As the AV valves first open, blood accumulated in the atria moves rapidly into the relaxed ventricles. **Ventricular volume increases quickly while ventricular pressure remains relatively low**, owing to the ventricles' ability to accommodate incoming blood.

### Reduced filling (<span data-term="diastasis">diastasis</span>)

As the atrioventricular pressure difference decreases, filling slows. This slower period is called **diastasis**.

### Atrial systole

Near the end of diastole, atrial contraction adds the final portion of blood to the ventricles—the **<span data-term="atrial-kick">atrial kick</span>**. Most ventricular filling normally occurs passively before this point. The lecturer illustrates the proportions as approximately **70% passive and 30% atrial**; these are not fixed. Atrial systole often contributes roughly 10–30% in healthy adults at rest, varying with heart rate, age and ventricular relaxation.

Atrial contraction may become more important when ventricular relaxation is impaired or the ventricle is stiff. On the Wiggers diagram, it produces the **a wave** of atrial pressure. At the end of filling, ventricular volume reaches the **<span data-term="edv">end-diastolic volume (EDV)</span>**.

## 4. Isovolumetric contraction: why does pressure rise without ejection?

The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarisation, which precedes ventricular contraction. As ventricular pressure increases above atrial pressure, the mitral and tricuspid valves close, contributing to the first heart sound (**S1**).

But the ventricles **cannot immediately eject blood**. In the left ventricle, pressure is now greater than left atrial pressure, so the mitral valve is closed; it has not yet exceeded aortic pressure, so the aortic valve remains closed.

**Both valves are closed.** The myocardium generates force, causing pressure to rise rapidly, but blood cannot yet leave or enter. **Pressure rises while volume stays at EDV.** This is **<span data-term="isovolumetric">isovolumetric contraction</span>**.

The phase ends when ventricular pressure exceeds outflow arterial pressure and opens the corresponding semilunar valve. The lecturer's illustrative threshold pressures are approximately **80 mmHg in the aorta** and **10 mmHg in the pulmonary artery** at the onset of ejection. These are typical examples, not universal opening pressures.

## 5. Ventricular ejection and ventricular volumes

When ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the corresponding artery, the aortic or pulmonary valve opens. Blood is ejected and ventricular volume falls.

**Rapid ejection:** Early in ejection, outflow is high and a large fraction of stroke volume leaves the ventricle.

**Reduced ejection:** Later, outflow slows as ventricular contraction and the pressure gradient change. The conventional physiological order is **rapid ejection followed by reduced ejection**. The lecture slide describes initially slow then rapid ejection; the sequence here follows the standard physiological interpretation.

| Measurement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **End-diastolic volume (EDV)** | Volume present in a ventricle just before ejection |
| **End-systolic volume (ESV)** | Volume remaining after ejection |
| **Stroke volume (SV)** | Volume ejected per contraction |
| **<span data-term="ejection-fraction">Ejection fraction (EF)</span>** | Percentage of EDV ejected |

**SV = EDV − ESV**

**EF = (SV ÷ EDV) × 100%**

For example, EDV **130 mL** and ESV **50 mL** give SV **80 mL** and EF **61.5%**. The lecturer gives illustrative values of EDV ≈130 mL, SV ≈70–90 mL and EF ≈65%.

**Important:** EF is the percentage of blood *inside a ventricle at the end of filling* that it ejects—not the percentage of blood in the entire body.

## 6. Isovolumetric relaxation: why doesn't filling begin immediately?

Towards the end of systole, ventricular pressure falls. When aortic pressure exceeds left ventricular pressure, the aortic valve closes; the corresponding event occurs at the pulmonary valve. Semilunar valve closure contributes to the second heart sound (**S2**).

**Filling still cannot begin.** The ventricular pressure remains above atrial pressure, so the AV valves are also closed. While the myocardium relaxes, pressure falls rapidly, but blood cannot enter or leave. **Pressure falls while volume stays at ESV**—this is **isovolumetric relaxation**.

Once ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the AV valves open and ventricular filling resumes.

## 7. Understanding the Wiggers diagram

### Five events to follow across the traces

1. **Atrial contraction:** The P wave represents atrial depolarisation. Atrial systole follows, raising atrial pressure (the **a wave**) and completing filling.
2. **Ventricular contraction begins:** QRS represents ventricular depolarisation. Ventricular pressure rises, AV valves close (S1), and pressure climbs during isovolumetric contraction.
3. **Ejection begins:** Ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, semilunar valves open and ventricular volume starts falling.
4. **Relaxation begins:** The T wave marks ventricular repolarisation during late systole. As ventricular pressure drops below arterial pressure, semilunar valves close (S2), followed by isovolumetric relaxation.
5. **Filling resumes:** When ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the AV valves reopen and ventricular volume rises.

### Why are there a, c and v waves on the atrial tracing?

| Wave | Main cause |
|---|---|
| **a wave** | Atrial contraction |
| **c wave** | Bulging of the closed AV valve towards the atrium early in ventricular systole |
| **v wave** | Atrial filling while the AV valve is closed |

These are different **pressure events**, not three separate atrial contractions. S1 mainly accompanies AV valve closure; S2 mainly accompanies semilunar valve closure. Their detailed mechanisms and splitting belong in Chapter 05.

## 8. What happens when heart rate increases?

As heart rate increases, each cardiac cycle shortens. Both systole and diastole can become shorter, but **diastole generally shortens proportionately more**.

This matters because ventricular filling and much of the left ventricle's coronary blood flow occur during diastole. At moderate rates, increased contractility and faster relaxation help maintain pumping. **Excessive tachycardia** may leave insufficient filling time, reducing EDV and potentially SV. Therefore a faster heartbeat does not automatically mean higher cardiac output.

## Understanding Check

**1. Left ventricular pressure rises rapidly while ventricular volume remains unchanged. Which phase is occurring, and why is blood not being ejected?**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p><strong>Isovolumetric contraction.</strong> The mitral valve has closed, but ventricular pressure has not yet exceeded aortic pressure. Both valves remain closed, so pressure rises without ejection.</p></details>

**2. Why doesn't filling begin immediately after the aortic valve closes?**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>Left ventricular pressure is still above left atrial pressure, keeping the mitral valve closed. Filling begins when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure.</p></details>

**3. Why can atrial contraction matter more when ventricular relaxation is impaired?**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>Impaired relaxation reduces efficient passive filling. Atrial contraction helps deliver additional blood into the less compliant ventricle.</p></details>

**4. If EDV is 140 mL and ESV is 60 mL, calculate stroke volume and ejection fraction.**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>SV = 140 − 60 = <strong>80 mL</strong>. EF = (80 ÷ 140) × 100 = <strong>57.1%</strong>.</p></details>

**5. Why is it incorrect to state that ventricular contraction always causes immediate ejection?**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>Early ventricular contraction occurs with AV and semilunar valves closed. Ejection begins only after ventricular pressure exceeds outflow arterial pressure and a semilunar valve opens.</p></details>

**6. Distinguish the two isovolumetric phases on a pressure–volume recording.**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>Both have constant ventricular volume. Pressure <strong>rises at EDV</strong> during contraction and <strong>falls at ESV</strong> during relaxation.</p></details>

**7. What produces the c wave in the atrial pressure tracing?**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>The closed AV valve bulges into the atrium during early ventricular contraction, slightly raising atrial pressure.</p></details>

**8. Why may extreme tachycardia reduce stroke volume?**
<details><summary>Reveal answer</summary><p>Markedly shortened diastole can reduce ventricular filling and EDV, reducing the volume available for ejection.</p></details>

## Chapter Essentials

1. **Valve movements depend on pressure differences:** AV valves open when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure; semilunar valves open when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure.
2. **Ventricular filling:** AV valves open, semilunar valves closed; most filling is passive.
3. **Atrial systole:** Completes filling and produces the atrial **a wave**.
4. **Isovolumetric contraction:** All valves closed; pressure rises while volume stays at **EDV**; AV closure contributes to **S1**.
5. **Ventricular ejection:** Semilunar valves open; ventricular volume falls during rapid then reduced ejection.
6. **Ventricular volumes:** **SV = EDV − ESV**.
7. **Ejection fraction:** **EF = (SV ÷ EDV) × 100%**.
8. **Isovolumetric relaxation:** All valves closed; pressure falls while volume stays at **ESV**; semilunar closure contributes to **S2**.
9. **Wiggers diagram:** Relates ECG, pressure changes, volume changes, valve events and heart sounds across the same heartbeat.
10. **Fast heart rates:** Diastole shortens disproportionately and extreme tachycardia may reduce filling and coronary perfusion.
