# Formation of <span data-term="oedema">Oedema</span>

## 1. What exactly is oedema?

**Oedema** is too much fluid in the spaces **between the cells** (the interstitium), enough to make tissue swell.

Fluid is always leaving the capillaries, and the lymphatics are always carrying it back. **Oedema happens when more leaves than can be carried away.**

<div class="edema-balance" role="group" aria-label="Fluid balance causes oedema"><span><strong>Filtration ↑</strong><small>More fluid entering tissue</small></span><b>+</b><span><strong>Drainage insufficient</strong><small>Less fluid leaving tissue</small></span><b>→</b><span><strong>Oedema</strong><small>Fluid accumulates</small></span></div>

So there are only two ways to get oedema: **too much filtration**, or **too little drainage**.

## 2. What physical forces govern filtration?

The four Starling forces decide how much fluid leaves the capillaries *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/microcirculation?section=5-why-does-fluid-leave-capillaries-even-when-protein-stays-in-blood">→ Microcirculation, §5</a>)*:

- **Pc**, capillary blood pressure: pushes out.
- **πc**, plasma protein (albumin) pull: holds water in.
- **Pi** and **πi**, the tissue's own pressure and protein pull: smaller.

<div class="equation-block"><span class="equation-label">Traditional Starling filtration model</span><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><msub><mi>J</mi><mi>v</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>K</mi><mi>f</mi></msub><mo>[</mo><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>P</mi><mi>c</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>P</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>)</mo><mo>−</mo><mi>σ</mi><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>c</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>)</mo><mo>]</mo></math></div>

Two extra terms:

- **<span data-term="kf">Kf</span>**, how leaky the wall is (and how much wall there is). A leaky, inflamed wall has a high Kf.
- **<span data-term="reflection-coefficient">σ</span>**, how well the wall holds back protein. **σ = 1** means proteins can't cross at all, so their full pull counts. In leaky, inflamed capillaries **σ falls**, proteins slip through, and their pull is lost.

## 3. Five routes to the same outcome: excess interstitial fluid

| What fails | What changes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Push too high** | Pc ↑ | Blocked veins, heart failure, standing still, leaky vein valves |
| **Pull too weak** | πc ↓ (low albumin) | Liver disease, kidney protein loss (<span data-term="nephrotic">nephrotic syndrome</span>), malnutrition |
| **Wall too leaky** | Kf ↑, σ ↓ | Inflammation, burns, allergy |
| **Drain blocked** | Lymph flow ↓ | Filariasis, lymph nodes removed in breast cancer surgery |
| **Too much fluid overall** | Salt and water retained | Kidney failure |

**The key question for any swelling: which part failed?** Different diseases can look alike on the outside but work in completely different ways.

## 4. Why does raised venous pressure cause oedema?

If blood can't drain out of the veins, it **backs up into the capillaries**, raising Pc and pushing more fluid out. The lecture lists:

- **Blocked veins** (a clot in a leg vein): one leg swells.
- **Leaky vein valves** (varicose veins): blood falls back and pools.
- **Standing still for long periods**: gravity raises leg venous pressure, and the muscle pump isn't working, so the ankles swell. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/venous-return?section=4-how-do-the-skeletal-muscle-pump-and-venous-valves-work">→ Venous Return, §4</a>)*
- **Heart failure**:
  - A failing **right** heart raises systemic venous pressure, causing swollen ankles.
  - A failing **left** heart raises pressure in the lung veins, causing **<span data-term="pulmonary-oedema">pulmonary oedema</span>**: fluid in the lungs, which stops oxygen getting into the blood and makes the patient breathless.

**Arteriole and venule tone matter too.** Widening the arterioles lets more of the arterial pressure through to the capillaries. Tightening the venules holds blood back in them. Both raise Pc, and the lecture lists both as causes.

## 5. Why can low plasma proteins cause oedema?

**Albumin is the main protein holding water in the blood.** If it falls, that pull weakens and fluid stays out in the tissues, often all over the body.

Albumin falls when:

- **The liver can't make enough:** liver failure (cirrhosis).
- **The kidneys leak it into the urine:** nephrotic syndrome.
- **The diet lacks protein:** severe malnutrition (kwashiorkor).

The lecture also mentions **osmotically active substances building up in the tissues**: they pull water out into the tissue from the other side.

## 6. How does inflammation increase oedema?

**Histamine, bradykinin and substance P** make the capillary wall **leaky**: the gaps between endothelial cells widen. Water and proteins pour out. The escaped proteins now pull water **outwards**, adding to the swelling.

This is why a sting or a sprain swells within minutes. It is **not a pressure problem; the wall itself has changed.**

## 7. Why does lymphatic obstruction cause persistent swelling?

Even with normal pressures, some fluid and protein leave the capillaries all the time, and **only the lymphatics can take the protein back**. If they are blocked, protein-rich fluid builds up. Over time it causes inflammation and scarring, and the swelling becomes firm and permanent: **<span data-term="lymphoedema">lymphoedema</span>**.

The lecture's examples:

- **Filariasis (elephantiasis):** parasitic worms block the lymph vessels, so the legs or scrotum swell enormously.
- **Radical mastectomy:** removing the armpit lymph nodes in breast cancer surgery leaves that arm swollen.

## 8. Why are pitting and non-pitting oedema different?

Press a thumb firmly into swollen skin for a few seconds.

- **<span data-term="pitting">Pitting oedema</span>:** a dent stays after you let go. The extra fluid is free to move and is pushed aside. This is typical of heart failure, low albumin and venous causes.
- **Non-pitting oedema:** no dent forms. The tissue is firm, because the fluid is bound up with protein and the tissue has become scarred.



**Lymphoedema** is usually non-pitting, but **only once it is long-standing**. Early lymphoedema can still pit, and it becomes firm as scarring develops.

<aside class="source-note"><strong>Source note:</strong> The lecture states that lymphoedema does not pit. That describes its usual late stage; early lymphoedema can pit.</aside>

## 9. Which location of oedema suggests which mechanism?

| Where the swelling is | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| **Both ankles, worse at the end of the day** | Gravity plus raised venous pressure (standing, right heart failure) |
| **The lungs** (breathlessness, crackles) | Left heart failure, or leaky lung capillaries |
| **All over, including around the eyes** | Low albumin (kidney or liver disease, malnutrition) |
| **One arm or leg, firm** | Blocked lymphatics, or a blocked vein |

These are clues, not diagnoses. Heart and kidney disease often cause oedema by **more than one route at once**.

## 10. Chapter Essentials

1. **Oedema** = too much fluid between the cells. More leaves the capillaries than the lymphatics can carry away.
2. **Only two ways:** too much filtration, or too little drainage.
3. **Five routes:** push too high (Pc ↑), pull too weak (albumin ↓), wall too leaky, drain blocked, or too much fluid overall.
4. **Raised venous pressure:** blocked veins, leaky valves, standing still, heart failure. Right failure → swollen ankles; left failure → pulmonary oedema.
5. **Low albumin:** liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition → swelling everywhere.
6. **Inflammation:** histamine, bradykinin and substance P make the wall leaky; escaped proteins pull more water out.
7. **Lymphatic blockage:** filariasis, removal of lymph nodes → protein-rich, firm lymphoedema.
8. **Pitting** = mobile fluid. **Non-pitting** = firm, scarred tissue (late lymphoedema).
9. **Ask where the swelling is**: it points to the mechanism that failed.
