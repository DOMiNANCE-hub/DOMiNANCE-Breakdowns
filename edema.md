# Formation of Oedema

**Primary lecture:** CIRCULATION 1(4).pdf, slides 89–95. **Supplementary clarification:** revised Starling physiology, endothelial glycocalyx, mechanisms and clinical interpretation of pitting versus non-pitting swelling.

## 1. What exactly is oedema?

**Oedema** is the **abnormal accumulation of excess fluid in tissue interstitial spaces**. It is not simply the presence of fluid outside blood vessels: cells normally live surrounded by a regulated volume of interstitial fluid.

The important balance is between:

- **Microvascular filtration:** Fluid leaving blood vessels into tissue.
- **Lymphatic drainage:** Fluid and proteins being returned from tissues to the venous circulation.

When filtration exceeds the removal capacity of lymphatic vessels, interstitial fluid accumulates. Oedema may therefore occur even if the heart is still beating and blood is continuing to circulate.

## 2. What physical forces govern filtration?

The lecturer identifies **capillary hydrostatic pressure, interstitial hydrostatic pressure, plasma oncotic pressure, interstitial oncotic pressure, filtration coefficient, pre-/postcapillary resistance and capillary surface area**.

A conventional form of the Starling equation summarises these:

| Symbol | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Jv** | Net fluid flux | Positive value represents net outward filtration |
| **Kf** | Filtration coefficient | Reflects permeability and filtering surface area |
| **Pc** | Capillary hydrostatic pressure | Drives fluid out of capillaries |
| **Pi** | Interstitial hydrostatic pressure | Can oppose outward filtration |
| **πc** | Capillary plasma oncotic pressure | Opposes outward filtration |
| **πi** | Interstitial oncotic pressure | Can promote outward filtration |
| **σ** | Protein reflection coefficient | How effectively the barrier restricts protein passage |

**Why is albumin important?** Albumin contributes strongly to plasma oncotic pressure. If plasma protein concentration falls, its normal opposition to fluid loss becomes weaker.

### Important refinement: endothelial glycocalyx

The lecturer's classic model assumes a simple balance between capillary and bulk interstitial proteins. Modern research shows the **endothelial glycocalyx** affects the relevant oncotic gradient immediately beneath the endothelial surface.

In many tissues, steady-state filtration persists along much of the capillary bed; a large universal reabsorption at the venous end is **not** expected. Lymphatics provide the main ongoing return path for filtered fluid.

The lecture's **24 L/day, 85% capillary reabsorption and 15% lymphatic return** is a **historical illustrative model**, not an exact modern description for all tissues. Some transient reabsorption and specialised tissue exceptions remain possible.

## 3. Five routes to the same outcome: excess interstitial fluid

| Mechanism | Physiological change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher Pc** | More hydrostatic pressure pushes water outward | Venous obstruction, heart failure |
| **Lower πc** | Weaker plasma oncotic opposition to water loss | Liver disease, renal protein loss, malnutrition |
| **Higher permeability/Kf** | Vessel barrier allows more fluid and protein escape | Inflammation, burns, allergy |
| **Impaired lymph drainage** | Filtered fluid and proteins cannot return adequately | Filariasis, lymph node removal |
| **Salt and water retention** | More ECF volume and often higher microvascular pressure | Renal dysfunction |

**Key reasoning:** Different diseases can all create swelling, but through **different failed parts of the fluid-balance system**.

## 4. Why does raised venous pressure cause oedema?

The lecturer lists increased venous pressure, venous obstruction, venous valve incompetence, prolonged standing and heart failure.

If pressure at the venous end of a capillary bed rises, the pressure gradient across its resistance vessels changes, often raising **capillary hydrostatic pressure Pc**. More fluid is pushed into the interstitium.

**Example — standing still:** Gravity increases hydrostatic pressure in dependent leg veins. Without the calf-muscle pump, venous pooling persists and capillary filtration rises. Ankles can swell even though the heart's intrinsic muscle is normal.

**Example — cardiac failure:** Elevated systemic venous pressure may produce dependent peripheral oedema; elevated pulmonary venous pressure may produce pulmonary oedema, impairing lung gas exchange.

### Why can arteriolar dilation or venular constriction affect filtration?

A simplified microvascular pressure system has resistance **before** and **after** the capillary bed. Reduced precapillary resistance or increased postcapillary resistance can raise capillary pressure relative to surrounding tissue.

The lecturer explicitly lists **arteriolar dilation and venular constriction** as factors that can increase filtration. Their effect depends on the wider microvascular context.

## 5. Why can low plasma proteins cause oedema?

Plasma proteins help maintain **plasma oncotic pressure**. If albumin falls markedly, the oncotic pressure opposing capillary filtration decreases.

The lecturer gives three important examples:

- **Liver disease:** Reduced albumin synthesis in significant hepatic dysfunction.
- **Kidney disease:** Excess urinary protein loss in conditions such as nephrotic syndrome.
- **Severe malnutrition:** Inadequate protein availability and related metabolic effects.

**Mechanism:** Albumin ↓ → plasma oncotic pressure ↓ → filtration easier relative to retention → interstitial fluid may accumulate if lymphatic return is exceeded.

The lecture also mentions **osmotically active substances accumulating in tissues**. Increased effective interstitial oncotic forces can draw or retain more water in the tissue environment.

## 6. How does inflammation increase oedema?

The lecture names **histamine, bradykinin and substance P** among agents that increase permeability.

Inflammatory signals change endothelial barrier properties, allowing greater fluid movement and, in some vascular beds, more protein escape. This can increase **Kf** and alter the effective oncotic balance, promoting local tissue swelling.

This is not identical to swelling caused purely by raised venous pressure: the **barrier itself** has changed.

## 7. Why does lymphatic obstruction cause persistent swelling?

Even when capillary pressure is fairly normal, some fluid and proteins continually reach the interstitium. Lymphatic vessels normally return these materials to the venous circulation.

If lymphatic channels are damaged or obstructed, drainage cannot match filtration. Protein-rich fluid accumulates and chronic inflammation and tissue remodelling can follow.

The lecturer identifies **filarial infection (elephantiasis)** and **radical mastectomy** affecting lymph drainage as examples. Details are developed in Chapter 15.

## 8. Why are pitting and non-pitting oedema different?

**Pitting oedema** means sustained finger pressure produces a temporary indentation. Mobile interstitial fluid is displaced by the pressure.

**Non-pitting oedema** may involve tissue changes such as fibrosis, protein deposition or altered connective-tissue properties, so an indentation is less easily produced.

**Important qualification:** Early lymphoedema can **pit**. Longstanding lymphoedema may become firm and non-pitting as fibrosis develops. Therefore, the lecturer's short “lymphoedema — not pitting” statement describes a common **late pattern**, not every stage.

Clinical signs alone should not be used to determine a diagnosis without considering the full presentation.

## 9. Which location of oedema suggests which mechanism?

| Pattern | A physiological possibility |
|---|---|
| **Dependent ankle swelling after standing** | Gravity-related venous and capillary hydrostatic pressure rise |
| **Pulmonary oedema** | Raised pulmonary microvascular pressure and/or permeability, impairing gas exchange |
| **Generalised swelling with low albumin** | Reduced plasma oncotic opposition to filtration |
| **Chronic swelling after lymph-node removal** | Impaired regional lymphatic drainage |

These are **mechanistic associations**, not exclusive diagnoses. For example, more than one oedema mechanism can coexist in heart or kidney disease.

**Central takeaway:** The visible swelling is the outcome. The important question is **which fluid-balance mechanism failed?**
