# Formation of Oedema

## 1. What exactly is oedema?

**Oedema** is the **abnormal accumulation of excess fluid in tissue interstitial spaces**. It is not simply the presence of fluid outside blood vessels: cells normally live surrounded by a regulated volume of interstitial fluid.

The important balance is between:

- **Microvascular filtration:** Fluid leaving blood vessels into tissue.
- **Lymphatic drainage:** Fluid and proteins being returned from tissues to the venous circulation.

<div class="edema-balance" role="group" aria-label="Fluid balance causes oedema"><span><strong>Filtration ↑</strong><small>More fluid entering tissue</small></span><b>+</b><span><strong>Drainage insufficient</strong><small>Less fluid leaving tissue</small></span><b>→</b><span><strong>Oedema</strong><small>Fluid accumulates</small></span></div>

When filtration exceeds the removal capacity of lymphatic vessels, interstitial fluid accumulates. Oedema may therefore occur even if the heart is still beating and blood is continuing to circulate.

## 2. What physical forces govern filtration?

The principal factors include **capillary hydrostatic pressure, interstitial hydrostatic pressure, plasma oncotic pressure, interstitial oncotic pressure, filtration coefficient, pre-/postcapillary resistance and capillary surface area**.

A conventional form of the Starling equation summarises these:

<div class="equation-block"><span class="equation-label">Traditional Starling filtration model</span><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><msub><mi>J</mi><mi>v</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>K</mi><mi>f</mi></msub><mo>[</mo><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>P</mi><mi>c</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>P</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>)</mo><mo>−</mo><mi>σ</mi><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>c</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>)</mo><mo>]</mo></math></div>

| Symbol | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Jv** | Net fluid flux | Positive value represents net outward filtration |
| **Kf** | Filtration coefficient | Reflects permeability and filtering surface area |
| **Pc** | Capillary hydrostatic pressure | Drives fluid out of capillaries |
| **Pi** | Interstitial hydrostatic pressure | Can oppose outward filtration |
| **πc** | Capillary plasma oncotic pressure | Opposes outward filtration |
| **πi** | Interstitial oncotic pressure | Can promote outward filtration |
| **σ** | Protein reflection coefficient | How effectively the barrier restricts protein passage |

**Why is albumin important?** Albumin contributes strongly to plasma oncotic pressure. If plasma protein concentration falls, its normal opposition to fluid loss becomes weaker.

### Important refinement: endothelial glycocalyx

The classic model assumes a simple balance between capillary and bulk interstitial proteins. Modern research shows the **endothelial glycocalyx** affects the relevant oncotic gradient immediately beneath the endothelial surface.

In many tissues, steady-state filtration persists along much of the capillary bed; a large universal reabsorption at the venous end is **not** expected. Lymphatics provide the main ongoing return path for filtered fluid.

The lecture's **24 L/day, 85% capillary reabsorption and 15% lymphatic return** is a **historical illustrative model**, not an exact modern description for all tissues. Some transient reabsorption and specialised tissue exceptions remain possible.

## 3. Five routes to the same outcome: excess interstitial fluid

| Mechanism | Physiological change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher Pc** | More hydrostatic pressure pushes water outward | Venous obstruction, heart failure |
| **Lower πc** | Weaker plasma oncotic opposition to water loss | Liver disease, renal protein loss, malnutrition |
| **Higher permeability/Kf** | Vessel barrier allows more fluid and protein escape | Inflammation, burns, allergy |
| **Impaired lymph drainage** | Filtered fluid and proteins cannot return adequately | Filariasis, lymph node removal |
| **Salt and water retention** | More ECF volume and often higher microvascular pressure | Renal dysfunction |

**Key reasoning:** Different diseases can all create swelling, but through **different failed parts of the fluid-balance system**.

## 4. Why does raised venous pressure cause oedema?

The lecturer lists increased venous pressure, venous obstruction, venous valve incompetence, prolonged standing and heart failure.

If pressure at the venous end of a capillary bed rises, the pressure gradient across its resistance vessels changes, often raising **capillary hydrostatic pressure Pc**. More fluid is pushed into the interstitium.

**Example — standing still:** Gravity increases hydrostatic pressure in dependent leg veins. Without the calf-muscle pump, venous pooling persists and capillary filtration rises. Ankles can swell even though the heart's intrinsic muscle is normal.

**Example — cardiac failure:** Elevated systemic venous pressure may produce dependent peripheral oedema; elevated pulmonary venous pressure may produce pulmonary oedema, impairing lung gas exchange.

### Why can arteriolar dilation or venular constriction affect filtration?

A simplified microvascular pressure system has resistance **before** and **after** the capillary bed. Reduced precapillary resistance or increased postcapillary resistance can raise capillary pressure relative to surrounding tissue.

The lecturer explicitly lists **arteriolar dilation and venular constriction** as factors that can increase filtration. Their effect depends on the wider microvascular context.

## 5. Why can low plasma proteins cause oedema?

Plasma proteins help maintain **plasma oncotic pressure**. If albumin falls markedly, the oncotic pressure opposing capillary filtration decreases.

Three important examples:

- **Liver disease:** Reduced albumin synthesis in significant hepatic dysfunction.
- **Kidney disease:** Excess urinary protein loss in conditions such as nephrotic syndrome.
- **Severe malnutrition:** Inadequate protein availability and related metabolic effects.

**Mechanism:** Albumin ↓ → plasma oncotic pressure ↓ → filtration easier relative to retention → interstitial fluid may accumulate if lymphatic return is exceeded.

The lecture also mentions **osmotically active substances accumulating in tissues**. Increased effective interstitial oncotic forces can draw or retain more water in the tissue environment.

## 6. How does inflammation increase oedema?

The lecture names **histamine, bradykinin and substance P** among agents that increase permeability.

Inflammatory signals change endothelial barrier properties, allowing greater fluid movement and, in some vascular beds, more protein escape. This can increase **Kf** and alter the effective oncotic balance, promoting local tissue swelling.

This is not identical to swelling caused purely by raised venous pressure: the **barrier itself** has changed.

## 7. Why does lymphatic obstruction cause persistent swelling?

Even when capillary pressure is fairly normal, some fluid and proteins continually reach the interstitium. Lymphatic vessels normally return these materials to the venous circulation.

If lymphatic channels are damaged or obstructed, drainage cannot match filtration. Protein-rich fluid accumulates and chronic inflammation and tissue remodelling can follow.

The principal factors include **filarial infection (elephantiasis)** and **radical mastectomy** affecting lymph drainage as examples. Details are developed in Chapter 15.

## 8. Why are pitting and non-pitting oedema different?

**Pitting oedema** means sustained finger pressure produces a temporary indentation. Mobile interstitial fluid is displaced by the pressure.

**Non-pitting oedema** may involve tissue changes such as fibrosis, protein deposition or altered connective-tissue properties, so an indentation is less easily produced.

**Important qualification:** Early lymphoedema can **pit**. Longstanding lymphoedema may become firm and non-pitting as fibrosis develops. Therefore, the lecturer's short “lymphoedema — not pitting” statement describes a common **late pattern**, not every stage.

Clinical signs alone should not be used to determine a diagnosis without considering the full presentation.

## 9. Which location of oedema suggests which mechanism?

| Pattern | A physiological possibility |
|---|---|
| **Dependent ankle swelling after standing** | Gravity-related venous and capillary hydrostatic pressure rise |
| **Pulmonary oedema** | Raised pulmonary microvascular pressure and/or permeability, impairing gas exchange |
| **Generalised swelling with low albumin** | Reduced plasma oncotic opposition to filtration |
| **Chronic swelling after lymph-node removal** | Impaired regional lymphatic drainage |

These are **mechanistic associations**, not exclusive diagnoses. For example, more than one oedema mechanism can coexist in heart or kidney disease.

**Central takeaway:** The visible swelling is the outcome. The important question is **which fluid-balance mechanism failed?**


## Understanding Check

**1. Why doesn't a healthy person normally develop continually increasing tissue swelling?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Microvascular filtration supplies some interstitial fluid, but lymphatic vessels return the excess fluid and escaped proteins to the venous circulation. Normally drainage matches ongoing net filtration sufficiently to prevent progressive interstitial accumulation.</p></details>

**2. A patient has venous obstruction in one leg. Which Starling force is most likely increased?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc).</strong> Obstruction increases pressure upstream in venules and capillaries, pushing more fluid into tissue. Swelling develops when the increase in filtration exceeds local lymphatic capacity.</p></details>

**3. Why might prolonged motionless standing produce ankle oedema?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Gravity raises venous pressure in dependent legs. This elevates capillary hydrostatic pressure and filtration. Lack of calf-muscle pumping also reduces venous emptying, favouring interstitial fluid accumulation at the ankles.</p></details>

**4. Why can advanced liver disease cause oedema through a different mechanism from venous obstruction?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Severe liver disease may reduce albumin synthesis, lowering <strong>plasma oncotic pressure</strong>. Fluid is then less strongly retained within capillaries. Venous obstruction, by contrast, chiefly increases hydrostatic pressure; either may cause excess filtration and oedema.</p></details>

**5. How can nephrotic protein loss produce swelling despite apparently normal arterial pressure?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Heavy urinary protein loss can reduce circulating albumin, decreasing plasma colloid osmotic pressure. This favours greater net fluid escape from microvessels; additional renal sodium and water retention may worsen the swelling.</p></details>

**6. A patient develops red, warm swelling around an inflamed area. What barrier mechanism can produce this?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Inflammatory mediators such as histamine and bradykinin can increase endothelial permeability. More water and some proteins enter local tissues, raising filtration and changing the effective oncotic conditions. This is <strong>increased permeability</strong>, not merely pressure loading.</p></details>

**7. What does the capillary filtration coefficient Kf represent?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p><strong>Kf</strong> represents the combined effect of exchange surface area and hydraulic permeability of the microvascular barrier. A larger Kf permits more volume movement for a given effective pressure difference, potentially increasing filtration and swelling.</p></details>

**8. Why is the classic statement that 85% of filtered fluid is always reabsorbed into venous capillaries misleading?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>The revised Starling principle accounts for the endothelial glycocalyx and local protein gradients. In many continuous capillary beds, low net filtration persists at steady state instead of large sustained venous-end reabsorption. <strong>Lymphatic return</strong> is central to fluid balance. The percentage is a historical illustrative model, not universal physiology.</p></details>

**9. Why might early lymphoedema be pitting while longstanding lymphoedema is often non-pitting?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Early obstruction produces relatively mobile accumulated tissue fluid that may be displaced by finger pressure. Chronic lymphatic failure can cause tissue inflammation, fibrosis and skin thickening, making the swollen tissue firmer and less readily indented.</p></details>

**10. Compare oedema caused by heart failure with oedema caused by lymph-node removal.**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Heart failure may elevate systemic or pulmonary venous pressure, increasing capillary hydrostatic filtration. Lymph-node removal can damage lymphatic return pathways, reducing clearance even if filtration initially remains near normal. Both ultimately produce excess interstitial fluid through different primary mechanisms.</p></details>

**11. Why can left-sided cardiac failure interfere with breathing by producing oedema?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Impaired left-heart forward function may elevate pulmonary venous and capillary pressures, raising fluid filtration into the lung interstitium and potentially alveoli. This increases diffusion distance and disrupts gas exchange, contributing to breathlessness.</p></details>

**12. Why is renal sodium/water retention able to worsen oedema in more than one disease?**
<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Retention expands extracellular fluid volume and can increase vascular filling and capillary hydrostatic pressure. If plasma oncotic pressure is low, venous pressure is high or lymph drainage is impaired, the additional retained fluid can further exceed tissue-fluid clearance capacity.</p></details>

## Chapter Essentials

1. **Oedema:** Abnormal excess interstitial fluid caused by filtration exceeding clearance or reduced lymph drainage.
2. **Traditional Starling model:** Jv = Kf[(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)].
3. **Pc:** Capillary hydrostatic pressure favours outward fluid movement.
4. **Plasma πc:** Mainly albumin-derived oncotic pressure opposes outward filtration.
5. **Kf:** Hydraulic permeability × filtering surface; inflammatory changes can raise fluid leakage.
6. **Raised venous pressure:** From obstruction, heart failure or standing increases capillary filtration.
7. **Reduced plasma albumin:** May follow liver disease, kidney protein loss or malnutrition.
8. **Inflammation:** Histamine, bradykinin and other mediators can increase endothelial permeability.
9. **Lymphatic dysfunction:** Impaired fluid/protein return causes local or regional swelling.
10. **Revised Starling model:** Glycocalyx-mediated gradients and lymphatic return revise universal venous-end reabsorption assumptions.
11. **Pitting:** Early fluid-rich swelling can pit; advanced lymphoedema may become non-pitting with fibrosis.
12. **Distribution:** Dependent, pulmonary, generalised and post-surgical patterns suggest different mechanisms.
13. **Excess salt/water:** Can amplify swelling through expanded ECF and increased microvascular pressures.
