# Coronary Circulation

## 1. Why does the heart need a separate blood supply?

The blood inside the chambers can't nourish the thick ventricular walls: oxygen can only diffuse a fraction of a millimetre. So the heart has its own arteries, the **right and left coronary arteries**. They arise from the **aortic sinuses**, the small pouches just above the aortic valve cusps.

**The heart already takes out most of its oxygen at rest** (about 70%, compared with about 25% for the body as a whole). So when it works harder, it can't simply extract more. **Coronary blood flow has to rise.** *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/co-measurement?section=7-why-does-pressure-loading-increase-the-heart-s-oxygen-demand">→ Measuring Cardiac Output, §7</a>)*

**Oxygen delivered = coronary blood flow × oxygen content of the blood.** If either falls, the heart muscle runs short.

  - the **left anterior descending (LAD)**, running down the front, which supplies the **front of the left ventricle and most of the septum**;
  - the **left circumflex (LCx)**, curving round the left side, which supplies the **side wall of the left ventricle**.
- **Right coronary artery (RCA)** supplies the **right ventricle** and, usually, the **bottom (inferior) wall** of the heart.

**Dominance** just means which artery gives off the **posterior descending artery**, which supplies the back of the septum. It is the RCA in most people ("right dominant"). It does **not** mean that artery supplies one whole side.

## 3. Why is left ventricular coronary perfusion greatest during diastole?

**In systole, the left ventricle squeezes its own arteries shut.** The coronary branches run through the muscle wall. When the muscle contracts hard, it **compresses** them, and left ventricular coronary flow almost stops.

**In diastole, the muscle relaxes and the arteries open.** Aortic pressure is still high, so blood **rushes in**.

So **the left ventricle is fed mainly in diastole.** The right ventricle, which squeezes far less hard, is fed in both <span data-term="systole-diastole">systole and diastole</span>.

**The pressure that drives left coronary flow** ≈ **aortic diastolic pressure − left ventricular diastolic pressure.** Anything that lowers aortic diastolic pressure (shock, aortic regurgitation) or raises left ventricular filling pressure (heart failure) cuts this driving pressure.

### Why is the inner layer (subendocardium) most at risk?

The **inner layer** of the left ventricle is squeezed hardest, because it is closest to the high-pressure cavity. So it depends **most** on diastole, and it is the **first to become ischaemic** when perfusion falls. In **aortic stenosis**, for example, very high ventricular pressure both raises oxygen demand and squeezes the inner-layer vessels harder.

## 4. How does coronary flow match changing myocardial work?

The heart's oxygen demand rises with **heart rate**, **contractility** and **<span data-term="wall-tension">wall tension</span>**. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/co-measurement?section=7-why-does-pressure-loading-increase-the-heart-s-oxygen-demand">→ Measuring Cardiac Output, §7</a>)*

**Working heart muscle opens its own arteries.** As it works harder, it releases local signals, chiefly **adenosine** and also K⁺, low O₂ and **<span data-term="nitric-oxide">nitric oxide</span>** from the endothelium. These relax the coronary arterioles, so flow rises to match the work. This is **active hyperaemia**. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/local-regulation">→ Local Control</a>)*

**Sympathetic nerves: two effects, one winner.** They can constrict coronary arteries directly (α receptors). But they also make the heart beat faster and harder (β₁), which raises its metabolism and releases dilating metabolites. **The dilation wins**, so in exercise coronary flow rises.

## 5. Why can tachycardia cause ischaemia?

A fast heart hits the heart muscle from **both** sides:

1. **Demand rises:** more beats per minute means more work.
2. **Supply time falls:** as heart rate rises, **diastole shortens much more than systole**. Diastole is when the left ventricle is perfused.

In a healthy heart, the arterioles dilate enough to cope. But with a **narrowed coronary artery**, a **low diastolic pressure** or a **high filling pressure**, the shrinking diastole can tip the balance into **ischaemia**.

## 6. Coronary flow reserve, angina and myocardial infarction

<figure class="teaching-illustration regional-anatomy-figure"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Coronary_Artery_Disease.png" alt="Medical comparison of normal coronary artery flow and an artery narrowed by atherosclerotic plaque."><figcaption><strong>Normal coronary artery versus a narrowed vessel.</strong><details class="figure-attribution"><summary>Image credit</summary><span class="figure-credit">BruceBlaus, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Coronary_Artery_Disease.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wikimedia Commons</a> · <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a> · unmodified.</span></details></figcaption></figure>

**Coronary flow reserve** is how much coronary flow can rise above its resting level when the arterioles fully dilate. Normally, it can rise about 4–5 times.

**Why angina comes on with exertion, not at rest:** behind a narrowed artery, the arterioles **dilate at rest** to keep flow normal. They have **used up their reserve**. When exercise raises demand, there is nothing left to give, so supply falls short and **angina** (ischaemic chest pain) appears. Resting cures it.

| | **Angina** | **Myocardial infarction (heart attack)** |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | Temporary **ischaemia**: supply falls short of demand | Prolonged, severe ischaemia: **heart muscle dies** |
| Reversible? | **Yes**, when demand falls or flow returns | **No**: dead muscle becomes scar |
| Usual cause | A fixed narrowing plus higher demand | A plaque ruptures and a clot blocks the artery |

## 7. Chapter Essentials

1. **Coronary arteries** arise from the aortic sinuses. The heart extracts about 70% of its oxygen at rest, so more demand needs **more flow**.
2. **LAD** = front of the LV and the septum; **circumflex** = side of the LV; **RCA** = right ventricle and inferior wall. **Dominance** = which artery gives the posterior descending artery.
3. **Venous blood** drains via the **coronary sinus** to the right atrium.
4. **The left ventricle is fed mainly in diastole**, because contraction squeezes its arteries. Driving pressure ≈ aortic diastolic − LV diastolic pressure.
5. **The <span data-term="subendocardium">subendocardium</span>** is the most vulnerable layer.
6. **Active hyperaemia:** adenosine and other metabolites dilate the arterioles. Sympathetic β₁ effects make the net result dilation.
7. **Tachycardia** raises demand and **shortens diastole**, so it can cause ischaemia in diseased arteries.
8. **Narrowed artery** → reserve used up at rest → **angina on exertion**. **Infarction** = muscle death from prolonged ischaemia.
