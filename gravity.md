# Gravity and Orthostatic Homeostasis

**Primary lecture:** CIRCULATION 3 Homeostasis in CVS.pdf, slides 2–11. **Supplementary clarification:** mechanism-level physiology and clinical distinctions are labelled where they extend beyond introductory lecture diagrams.

## 1. Standing changes the hydrostatic column

In the upright body, pressure at points **below** heart level rises because of the hydrostatic blood column, while pressure **above** heart level is relatively lower. Hydrostatic pressure depends on density, gravitational acceleration and vertical height difference (**ΔP = ρgh**). This is distinct from the pressure generated by the heart itself.

## 2. Why venous pooling lowers cardiac output

Standing suddenly shifts blood toward compliant lower-limb and splanchnic veins. Central blood volume and right atrial filling decline, lowering ventricular **end-diastolic volume → stroke volume** by the Frank–Starling mechanism. CO can transiently fall, endangering blood pressure at head level and cerebral blood flow.

## 3. The seconds-long baroreflex rescue

Reduced arterial stretch causes lower carotid/aortic baroreceptor firing. Vagal restraint decreases and sympathetic drive increases: **HR rises, contractility rises, arterioles constrict and veins constrict**. This response raises systemic resistance and supports venous return. A strong heartbeat alone cannot compensate if preload is critically low.

## 4. Skeletal-muscle pump and standing at attention

Contracting calf muscles compress deep leg veins; one-way valves direct flow centrally instead of permitting backward movement. Walking and subtle **isometric calf contraction** can reduce dependent pooling. Soldiers standing still may perform small muscular contractions to support venous return. The muscle pump complements—not replaces—the baroreflex.

## 5. Orthostatic hypotension and its clinical definition

Classical **orthostatic hypotension** is a sustained drop of at least **20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes of standing** from supine, using standard clinical measurement conditions. Dehydration, medications, autonomic neuropathy, haemorrhage and prolonged bed rest can contribute. Symptoms include dizziness, visual darkening and syncope from impaired cerebral perfusion. Some forms occur later than three minutes or immediately; formal definitions vary by timing.

## 6. Vasovagal syncope is not simply normal orthostatic compensation

Prolonged standing, heat, fear or pain can provoke a reflex with inappropriate **vasodilation and/or bradycardia**, leading to transient hypotension and fainting. Contrast this with the usual immediate compensation to standing, which produces **sympathetic activation and tachycardia**. A patient can also have mixed mechanisms; clinical history matters.

## 7. Gravity also explains dependent oedema

Higher dependent venous and capillary hydrostatic pressures favour filtration into ankle interstitium during prolonged standing. Lymphatic return and muscular movement limit swelling; long immobility can overcome these defences. This connects Chapter 19 directly to capillary exchange and oedema.

## 8. Acceleration, aviation and clinical consequences

Under positive **+Gz** (head-to-foot acceleration), blood shifts toward legs and brain perfusion may fall, causing grey-out, blackout or loss of consciousness. Anti-G manoeuvres and suits reduce dependent pooling. Under **−Gz**, blood shifts toward the head, contributing to congestion and visual disturbances. Effects depend on direction, intensity and duration. Supine positioning reduces the head-heart hydrostatic gradient during a simple fainting episode, but persistent or unexplained loss of consciousness needs medical assessment.
