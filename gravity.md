# Gravity and Orthostatic Homeostasis

## What changes when someone stands?
When a person rises from lying to standing, gravity increases hydrostatic pressure in dependent veins. Blood transiently pools in the legs and splanchnic circulation. This reduces central venous volume, venous return, ventricular end-diastolic volume, stroke volume and potentially cerebral perfusion.

## The rapid compensation
Reduced carotid sinus/aortic arch stretch decreases baroreceptor firing. Brainstem circuits increase sympathetic activity and reduce vagal activity, producing **tachycardia, venoconstriction and arteriolar constriction**. The skeletal-muscle pump supports venous return when standing or walking. Over longer time periods, renal salt/water regulation contributes.

## Why standing motionless can cause fainting
Without muscle pumping, pooling can continue. If compensatory mechanisms fail, cerebral perfusion falls, causing dizziness, visual dimming or syncope. Lying flat restores the head nearer the heart and reduces the gravitational pressure difference.

**Orthostatic hypotension** is a sustained excessive fall in arterial pressure on standing; autonomic neuropathy, medications and volume depletion are potential causes. Vasovagal syncope has an additional reflex pattern of vasodilatation and often bradycardia, and should not be equated with all postural hypotension.

## Acceleration and g-forces
Positive head-to-foot acceleration promotes blood displacement toward the legs and can impair vision and consciousness; anti-g suits counter dependent pooling. Negative acceleration drives blood toward the head and may cause congestion or visual disturbances. Effects depend on direction, magnitude, duration and individual compensation.

## Separate orthostatic hypotension and vasovagal syncope
**Orthostatic hypotension** is commonly defined as a sustained fall of **at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes of standing** (standard clinical criterion). Mechanistically, standing pools blood in dependent vessels, reduces preload and challenges cerebral perfusion. An intact baroreflex usually responds with **tachycardia and vasoconstriction**; autonomic failure, certain drugs, dehydration or prolonged pooling can overwhelm compensation. The lecturer's 'postural syncope = reflex bradycardia' should not be taken as the usual response to standing.

**Vasovagal syncope** often involves inappropriate reflex vasodilatation and/or bradycardia after triggers such as pain, heat, emotion or prolonged standing. It can also cause fainting but has a different autonomic pattern. Returning to the horizontal position restores a more favourable head-to-heart hydrostatic relationship.

## Think in pressure columns
Arterial and venous pressure at the ankle when standing are higher than at heart level because of hydrostatic load. At the head, the opposite tendency occurs. Dependent capillary hydrostatic pressure rises with prolonged standing, encouraging **ankle oedema**. **Leg-muscle isometric contractions** compress deep veins: venous valves channel blood centrally and reduce pooling, an everyday explanation for the soldier-at-attention question.

Under high positive head-to-foot **+Gz**, blood shifts toward legs and cerebral perfusion can fail, producing **grey-out/blackout**; anti-G suits increase external pressure over legs/abdomen. With negative **−Gz**, head/neck vascular congestion and 'red-out' symptoms may occur. Magnitude, direction and duration are crucial; the slide thresholds are illustrative rather than guarantees.

**Practice:** A person is dizzy after standing motionless, but feels better while walking. Walking activates the venous muscle pump and helps maintain venous return.
