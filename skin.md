# Cutaneous Circulation and the Triple Response

## 1. Skin blood flow is mainly about heat, not nutrition

Most tissues get the blood flow they need **to feed their cells**. The skin is different: its flow is controlled mainly to **regulate body temperature**. It can carry far more blood than its cells need.

- **Hot:** skin vessels **dilate**, so warm blood flows near the surface and heat is lost (flushed skin).
- **Cold:** sympathetic nerves **constrict** skin vessels, so blood stays deep and heat is kept (pale, cool skin).

**<span data-term="ava">Arteriovenous anastomoses (AVAs)</span>** are direct shortcuts from small arteries to veins, found in the fingers, toes, palms, ears and nose. When they open, a large volume of warm blood passes just under the skin, ideal for losing heat. They are controlled almost entirely by **sympathetic** nerves.

## 2. Skin blood flow in shock

In haemorrhage, sympathetic nerves **shut down skin blood flow**, diverting blood to the brain and heart. That is why a bleeding patient has **cold, pale, clammy skin**, often **before** blood pressure falls much. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/shock">→ Shock</a>)*

**Septic shock** can be different early on: toxins dilate the vessels, so the skin may be **warm and flushed**. **Skin temperature alone can't diagnose shock**; it must be read with the rest of the patient.

## 3. The triple response of Lewis

Draw a blunt point **firmly** across the skin of the forearm. Within a minute, three things appear, in this order:

| Sign | What you see | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Red line** | A red line exactly along the stroke, within seconds | **Local capillaries dilate** where the skin was injured |
| **2. <span data-term="flare">Flare</span>** | A wider, blotchy red area spreading out around the line | **<span data-term="axon-reflex">Axon reflex</span>**: sensory nerve endings send signals along their own branches, dilating nearby arterioles |
| **3. <span data-term="wheal">Wheal</span>** | A raised, pale swelling along the line | **Histamine** makes the capillaries **leaky**, so fluid leaks into the tissue (local oedema) |

### Why does the flare spread? The axon reflex, step by step

A single sensory nerve fibre in the skin doesn't end at one point. Near the skin it **splits into many branches**, like the fingers of a hand, and together they cover a patch of skin a few centimetres across.

1. **The stroke stimulates one branch.** The firm stroke damages a few cells and stimulates the sensory nerve endings in the line of the stroke.
2. **The signal travels up that branch.** It travels towards the spinal cord, as any sensory signal would.
3. **At the branch point, it also travels back down the other branches.** An impulse travels in both directions along a nerve fibre. So when it reaches the fork, it carries on up towards the spinal cord **and also runs back down every other branch**, into skin that was never touched.
4. **The endings of those branches release vasodilators.** They release **<span data-term="substance-p">substance P</span>** and **<span data-term="cgrp">CGRP</span>** (calcitonin gene-related peptide). These dilate the nearby arterioles directly, and substance P also makes nearby **<span data-term="mast-cell">mast cells</span> release histamine**, which dilates them further.
5. **The arterioles across the whole patch dilate, giving the flare.** The redness spreads as far as that fibre's branches reach, which is why the flare is **wider than the stroke but stops a few centimetres out**.

**Why "axon" reflex?** The whole loop happens **within the branches of one nerve fibre**. There is no synapse, and the spinal cord and brain are not needed. That makes it different from a true reflex arc, which passes through the spinal cord.

**How do we know?**

- A **local anaesthetic** that blocks the nerve abolishes the flare. The red line and the wheal still appear, because they come from local injury and histamine, not from the nerve.
- After the sensory nerve has been **cut and has degenerated**, the flare disappears.
- Cutting the nerve **above** the branch point, closer to the spinal cord, does **not** stop the flare, because the loop doesn't need the spinal cord.

## 4. Chapter Essentials

1. **Skin blood flow regulates heat**, not just nutrition. It can far exceed what the skin needs.
2. **Hot → dilate** (lose heat); **cold → sympathetic constriction** (keep heat).
3. **Arteriovenous anastomoses** in the fingers, toes, ears and nose shunt warm blood near the surface to lose heat.
4. **Haemorrhage → cold, pale skin** (sympathetic shutdown); early septic shock may give warm skin.
5. **Triple response:** **red line** (local dilation) → **flare** (axon reflex) → **wheal** (histamine, leaky capillaries, oedema).
6. **Flare = axon reflex:** a stroked branch sends impulses back down the fibre's other branches, which release substance P and CGRP to dilate arterioles. No spinal cord needed.
7. A light stroke gives only a brief **white line** (local constriction), which is not part of the triple response.
