Skin has a rich vascular network, including **arteriovenous anastomoses** in some regions. Increasing cutaneous perfusion promotes heat loss, whereas sympathetic vasoconstriction limits heat transfer and helps preserve central blood volume during haemorrhage.

## Local vascular reactions after stroking the skin
A mild stroke can initially produce a transient pale line through local vasoconstriction (the **white reaction**). Firmer stroking may produce the **triple response of Lewis**:
1. **Red line** near the stimulated area, reflecting local vasodilatation.
2. **Flare**, a wider surrounding erythema mediated partly by sensory axon reflexes and vasodilators.
3. **Wheal**, a raised swelling caused by increased vascular permeability and fluid leakage.

Histamine contributes importantly to these responses; their timing and expression vary.

## Shock and temperature
In haemorrhagic shock, intense skin vasoconstriction often produces cool, pale extremities. In warm environments, competing requirements for heat loss and blood-pressure maintenance influence vascular tone; skin circulation is regulated for **thermoregulation**, not simply nutrient demand.

**Wheal versus flare:** a wheal is a raised area of oedema from microvascular permeability; a flare is spreading erythema from local vasodilation involving sensory axon reflexes.

## Distinguish blood-flow functions
Skin circulation is unusual because its flow can greatly exceed local nutrient needs when the body must **lose heat**. Sympathetic-mediated cutaneous constriction conserves heat in cold conditions, whereas vasodilator mechanisms and opening of thermoregulatory arteriovenous anastomoses help increase heat loss in warmth. During haemorrhagic shock, strong skin vasoconstriction can contribute to pallor and coolness.

A firm, blunt stroke produces a **red line** at the site (local capillary/arteriolar dilation), a surrounding **flare** (wider arteriolar dilation partly due to local sensory-nerve **axon reflex**) and a raised **wheal** (local oedema as permeability rises). Histamine contributes to the reaction. A brief **white line response** with gentler stroking, traditionally attributed to local constrictor response; distinguish it from the red line/flare/wheal response.

**Practice:** Which component of the triple response is chiefly an oedematous swelling? The **wheal**; the flare is spreading redness.


## Understand the skin's unusual circulation

### Why isn't skin blood flow controlled only by oxygen demand?

Skin has two jobs: nourish its tissues and control body heat. When cold, narrowing cutaneous vessels conserves heat and can make the hands pale. During heat exposure, greater skin flow moves warm blood toward the surface, allowing heat loss. Special **arteriovenous anastomoses** in regions such as fingers and palms can carry blood between arterial and venous pathways while bypassing some nutritional capillaries.

Some cutaneous vessels are under sympathetic **noradrenergic vasoconstrictor** control; active vasodilator mechanisms also contribute to heat stress responses in humans. Therefore, simply saying *sympathetic stimulation always constricts every skin vessel* misses the physiological context.

### Understand the three components of Lewis's response

Imagine gently drawing a blunt object along the forearm, then applying a firmer stroke.

1. A brief **white line** may appear after light stroking and is distinct from the triple response.
2. A firmer stimulus produces a **red line** at the contact site due to local vasodilatation.
3. A wider surrounding **flare** develops because sensory nerve endings activate nearby branches through an **axon reflex**, encouraging arteriolar dilatation beyond the stroke.
4. A raised **wheal** forms where increased vascular permeability allows plasma fluid to move into surrounding tissue, producing local swelling.

Histamine can contribute importantly to redness, flare and swelling. **Red line = local redness; flare = spreading redness; wheal = raised oedema**. These signs are related but not interchangeable.

### Why is cool skin important in shock?

During haemorrhage, the body prioritizes blood flow to organs such as the brain and heart. Sympathetic vasoconstriction reduces skin flow, which may produce cool, pale extremities **even before a large fall in measured arterial pressure**. Skin findings alone cannot diagnose the type or severity of shock.

**Understanding checkpoint:** A patient has a raised mark after a skin stroke but no large surrounding red area. Which component is raised, and what caused it? *The wheal, from locally increased microvascular permeability and fluid entry into tissue.*

## Comprehensive Understanding Checks

**01. What makes skin perfusion different from most tissue perfusion?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>Cutaneous vessels do supply nutrients, but their flow can change far more than local metabolic demand requires. Increased surface flow supports heat loss, while reduced flow conserves heat during cold exposure.</details>

**02. Where are specialized cutaneous arteriovenous shunts especially important?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>Special arteriovenous anastomoses are especially important in non-hairy skin of fingers, palms and similar areas. They can route blood past superficial nutritional capillaries to adjust heat transfer quickly.</details>

**03. Why does cold generally reduce skin blood flow?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>In the cold, sympathetic vasoconstrictor activity narrows skin vessels and reduces warm blood reaching the surface, limiting heat loss. This does not mean skin circulation is regulated only by oxygen demand.</details>

**04. Why does skin often become pale and cool in haemorrhage?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>When blood volume falls, sympathetic activation narrows skin vessels to help preserve central pressure. Less warm blood reaches the surface, so extremities may feel cool and look pale even while blood pressure is initially maintained.</details>

**05. What is the white reaction after gentle skin stroking?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>A light stroke can produce a brief pale streak, traditionally linked to local reduction in superficial vessel filling. It is separate from the stronger red line, flare and wheal of Lewis's triple response.</details>

**06. Name the Lewis triple response components.**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>The red line is narrow redness at the site; the flare is wider surrounding redness mediated partly by a local sensory axon reflex; the wheal is raised swelling caused by increased vascular permeability.</details>

**07. What produces the red line?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>The red line occurs at the stroke itself when local vessels dilate and more blood fills the superficial microcirculation. It differs from the surrounding flare, which spreads beyond the stimulated line.</details>

**08. Why does flare extend beyond the initial stroke?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>Sensory nerve endings activated at the stimulus spread signals along nearby branches of the same peripheral neuron. Released mediators cause wider arteriolar dilation, explaining why the flare extends beyond the contact line.</details>

**09. Does an axon reflex require a brainstem integration centre?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>No. A local axon reflex travels within branches of a peripheral sensory neuron and can cause nearby mediator release without requiring a spinal cord or brainstem integration centre.</details>

**10. How does a wheal differ from flare?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>A wheal is raised because plasma fluid passes into the tissue when local microvascular permeability rises. A flare is broader redness from increased vessel diameter and blood flow, not the swelling itself.</details>

**11. Why can early septic shock present with warm skin?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>Early distributive or septic shock can involve widespread loss of vascular tone, leaving skin warm despite inadequate tissue perfusion. Warm skin therefore does not rule out shock, and later stages may look different.</details>

**12. Why is skin temperature alone insufficient to diagnose shock?**

<details><summary>Show explanation</summary>Skin temperature depends on environmental temperature, local vasomotor tone, sweating and heat transfer, not only the amount of blood reaching vital organs. Cool skin can support a concern about reduced perfusion in context, but it cannot by itself diagnose shock.</details>

## Chapter Essentials

1. Cutaneous perfusion has a strong thermoregulatory role.
2. Sympathetic constriction reduces skin flow in cold and bleeding.
3. Lewis triple response comprises red line, flare and wheal.
4. Flare involves a peripheral sensory axon reflex.
5. Wheal reflects increased capillary permeability and oedema.
