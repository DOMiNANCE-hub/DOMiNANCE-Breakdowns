# Cardiac Muscle Physiology

## What makes cardiac muscle different?
The myocardium contains short, branched, striated cells linked by **intercalated discs**. **Fascia adherens** and **desmosomes** transmit mechanical force and resist separation; **gap junctions** allow ionic current to pass between cells. Thus, the myocardium behaves as a coordinated functional syncytium, while individual cells remain separate.

Cardiac contractile cells require an electrical signal before contracting, whereas nodal pacemaker cells can depolarize spontaneously. The atria and ventricles are electrically insulated by the fibrous skeleton except through the normal AV conduction route, ensuring sequence rather than simultaneous contraction.

## Ventricular muscle action potential
- **Phase 0:** rapid depolarization through voltage-gated fast Na⁺ channels.
- **Phase 1:** brief initial repolarization, partly due to transient K⁺ outward current.
- **Phase 2 (plateau):** inward L-type Ca²⁺ current is balanced by outward K⁺ current.
- **Phase 3:** Ca²⁺ entry declines and K⁺ efflux drives repolarization.
- **Phase 4:** relatively stable resting membrane potential, maintained chiefly by K⁺ permeability.

The plateau contributes to the prolonged refractory period. **Cardiac muscle cannot normally develop tetanus** because refractoriness overlaps most of the mechanical contraction; relaxation and refilling remain possible between beats.

## Excitation–contraction coupling: electrical signal becomes force
1. The action potential opens **L-type Ca²⁺ channels** in the T-tubule membrane.
2. Entering Ca²⁺ triggers larger Ca²⁺ release from sarcoplasmic reticulum through **ryanodine receptors (RyR2)**: calcium-induced calcium release.
3. Cytosolic Ca²⁺ binds **troponin C**, permitting actin–myosin cycling and contraction.
4. **SERCA2a** pumps Ca²⁺ back into sarcoplasmic reticulum; the Na⁺–Ca²⁺ exchanger and membrane Ca²⁺ pumps assist removal, allowing relaxation.

Sympathetic β₁ stimulation increases cAMP/PKA signalling, improving Ca²⁺ entry, release, reuptake and therefore contraction and relaxation. **Positive inotropy** means stronger contraction at a given preload and afterload; **positive lusitropy** means faster relaxation.

## Why this matters
A prolonged refractory period prevents the heart from remaining continuously contracted. Reduced SERCA activity impairs relaxation and can compromise ventricular filling. Blocking RyR2-mediated SR release reduces available activator Ca²⁺ and contractile strength.

## Cells, intercalated discs and functional syncytia
Cardiac muscle cells are short, branched, striated cells connected end to end by **intercalated discs**. **Fascia adherens** anchor actin and transmit contractile force; **desmosomes** resist separation; **gap junctions** allow ions to pass between neighbouring cardiomyocytes and support electrical spread. Atria and ventricles behave as separate functional electrical syncytia, ordinarily linked by the specialised AV conduction system.

The **T-tubule–sarcoplasmic reticulum system** couples membrane depolarisation to calcium release. Cardiac muscle depends on both extracellular Ca²⁺ entry and SR Ca²⁺ release, unlike skeletal muscle's primarily mechanical DHPR–RyR coupling.

## Contrast pacemaker and ventricular action potentials
Working ventricular cells have a relatively stable resting membrane potential near **−90 mV**. Phase 0 is rapid Na⁺ entry; phase 1 brief initial repolarisation; phase 2 plateau reflects L-type Ca²⁺ entry balanced against K⁺ exit; phase 3 mainly K⁺-mediated repolarisation; phase 4 is a stable resting period. **Fast inward Na⁺ channels** account for the steep working-myocyte upstroke, not for the SA nodal upstroke.

SA and AV nodal pacemaker cells lack a stable resting plateau: their phase 4 gradually depolarises through the **funny current (If)**, declining outward K⁺ current and other inward currents; phase 0 relies primarily on **L-type Ca²⁺ channels**. They repolarise through K⁺ efflux. Thus the SA node initiates impulses without a preceding external nerve impulse.

## Explain relaxation as well as contraction
Ca²⁺ entering through L-type channels triggers ryanodine receptors (**RyR2**) to release more Ca²⁺ from SR: **calcium-induced calcium release**. Ca²⁺ binding to troponin C permits actin–myosin cycling. Relaxation requires lowering cytosolic Ca²⁺: **SERCA2a** returns most Ca²⁺ to SR, while the Na⁺–Ca²⁺ exchanger expels substantial Ca²⁺ from the cell. Phospholamban normally inhibits SERCA; phosphorylation reduces this inhibition and accelerates relaxation.

The long cardiac action potential creates a refractory period overlapping most of the twitch. This prevents normal tetanic summation so the heart can relax and refill. A shorter refractory period can increase susceptibility to premature excitation or re-entry, but does **not** mean a normal heart can undergo useful skeletal-type tetanus.

**Practice:** If the RyR2 channels failed to open after L-type Ca²⁺ influx, force would fall because the larger secondary SR release would be absent. If SERCA were impaired, relaxation would slow and SR Ca²⁺ loading for subsequent beats could decrease.
