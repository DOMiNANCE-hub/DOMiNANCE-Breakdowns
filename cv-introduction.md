# Introduction to the Heart and Circulation

## 1. Why do we need a circulatory system?

Every cell in the body requires oxygen and nutrients to function. At the same time, cells produce metabolic waste products, including carbon dioxide, which must be removed.

The **circulatory system** makes this continuous transport possible.

It consists of three major components:

- **Heart:** Pumps blood through the circulation.
- **Blood vessels:** Provide pathways through which blood travels.
- **Blood:** Transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones, waste products and heat.

For example, during exercise, skeletal muscles require more oxygen and nutrients to meet their increased energy demands. The circulatory system helps deliver these substances while transporting carbon dioxide and other metabolic products away.

## 2. The two major circulatory pathways

Blood circulates through two connected pathways:

**Pulmonary circulation:** Carries blood from the right side of the heart to the lungs, where carbon dioxide is released and oxygen is taken up. The oxygenated blood then returns to the left side of the heart.

**Systemic circulation:** Carries oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to tissues throughout the body. After delivering oxygen and collecting carbon dioxide, the blood returns to the right side of the heart.

These two circuits are arranged **in series**, meaning blood passes through one circuit and then the other in a continuous pathway.

Consequently, under stable conditions, the right and left ventricles must pump approximately equal volumes of blood per minute. Otherwise, blood would progressively accumulate in one circulation.

## 3. The heart and its four chambers

The heart is a muscular organ that functions as two coordinated pumps.

- **Right atrium:** Receives blood returning from the body through the superior and inferior venae cavae.
- **Right ventricle:** Pumps blood towards the lungs through the pulmonary trunk and pulmonary arteries.
- **Left atrium:** Receives oxygenated blood returning from the lungs through the pulmonary veins.
- **Left ventricle:** Pumps oxygenated blood into the aorta for distribution throughout the body.

The **atria primarily receive blood and assist ventricular filling**, while the **ventricles generate the pressure needed to pump blood into the arteries**.

### How does blood flow through the heart?

Follow the pathway:

**Body tissues → Venae cavae → Right atrium → Tricuspid valve → Right ventricle → Pulmonary valve → Pulmonary trunk and arteries → Lungs → Pulmonary veins → Left atrium → Mitral valve → Left ventricle → Aortic valve → Aorta → Body tissues.**

The cardiac valves help maintain one-way blood flow by preventing blood from flowing backwards.

Notice that the pulmonary arteries carry relatively deoxygenated blood, while the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.

This is because **arteries carry blood away from the heart, whereas veins carry blood towards the heart**, regardless of oxygen content.

## 4. Why is the left ventricular wall thicker than the right?

Although both ventricles pump approximately the same volume of blood per minute, they must generate different pressures.

To understand why, consider **vascular resistance**, which refers to the opposition that blood vessels offer to blood flow.

Imagine water flowing through a pipe. When the pipe narrows, it becomes harder to maintain the same flow unless a greater pressure difference is applied.

A similar principle applies to blood vessels.

The systemic circulation contains many small arteries and arterioles whose narrow lumens and muscular walls help regulate blood flow to different organs. Contraction or relaxation of their vascular smooth muscle changes the vessel diameter and therefore its resistance to blood flow.

Collectively, the systemic vascular network offers substantially more resistance than the pulmonary vascular network.

The pulmonary circulation, by comparison, has a relatively low-resistance network of vessels that allows blood to pass through the lungs at much lower pressures.

**What does this mean for the ventricles?**

The **left ventricle** must generate higher pressure to maintain blood flow through the systemic circulation. Its muscular wall is therefore thicker.

The **right ventricle** pumps blood through the lower-resistance pulmonary circulation, so it normally requires much less pressure and has a thinner muscular wall.

The lower pressure in the pulmonary circulation is also beneficial because the lungs contain delicate capillaries surrounding the alveoli. Excessively high pressure in these capillaries can force more fluid into the surrounding lung tissue, potentially causing **pulmonary oedema (fluid accumulation in the lungs)** and interfering with gas exchange.

**Key distinction:** The left ventricle normally generates greater pressure, not a greater volume of blood per minute.

*The factors determining vascular resistance will be explored further in Blood Flow and Hemodynamics.*

## 5. Systole and diastole

The pumping activity of the heart involves alternating periods of contraction and relaxation.

**Systole** refers to the period of contraction of a heart chamber, while **diastole** refers to its period of relaxation.

- **Atrial systole:** The atria contract, contributing the final portion of blood needed to complete ventricular filling.
- **Atrial diastole:** The atria relax and receive blood returning from the body and lungs. During ventricular filling, blood can also pass from the relaxed atria into the ventricles.
- **Ventricular systole:** The ventricles contract and generate pressure, allowing blood to be ejected into the pulmonary trunk (right ventricle) and aorta (left ventricle).
- **Ventricular diastole:** The ventricles relax and fill with blood in preparation for the next contraction.

Importantly, **atrial systole normally occurs towards the end of ventricular diastole**. The atria complete their contribution to ventricular filling before the ventricles contract.

*The detailed sequence of pressure changes, valve movements and phases of the heartbeat will be explained in the Cardiac Cycle chapter.*

## 6. Cardiac muscle and the conducting system

For the heart to pump blood effectively, it must generate sufficient contractile force, and its chambers must contract in a coordinated sequence.

Two important components make this possible:

- **Cardiac muscle (myocardium):** Produces the force required for contraction and blood ejection.
- **Specialised cardiac conducting system:** Generates and distributes electrical impulses that coordinate the heartbeat.

Electrical activation normally precedes contraction, allowing the atria and ventricles to pump in an organised sequence.

## Understanding Check

**1. Why must the right and left ventricles pump approximately equal volumes of blood per minute?**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>The pulmonary and systemic circulations are connected in series. Blood pumped by the right ventricle passes through the lungs before reaching the left ventricle. If their outputs remained unequal, blood would progressively accumulate in one circulation rather than maintaining a stable distribution.</p></details>

**2. Why does the left ventricle have a thicker muscular wall than the right ventricle?**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>The systemic circulation offers greater resistance to blood flow than the pulmonary circulation. Consequently, the left ventricle must generate higher pressure to maintain blood flow through the body, requiring a thicker muscular wall. The right ventricle pumps blood through the lower-resistance pulmonary circulation and therefore operates at much lower pressure.</p></details>

**3. A blood vessel carries oxygenated blood towards the heart. Is it an artery or a vein? Explain.**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>It is a vein. Blood vessels are classified by the direction in which they carry blood relative to the heart, not by oxygen content. Pulmonary veins, for example, carry oxygenated blood from the lungs towards the left atrium.</p></details>

**4. Does ventricular contraction always mean blood is being ejected?**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>No. At the beginning of ventricular systole, the ventricles first develop pressure while their outflow valves remain closed. Blood is ejected only when ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the corresponding outflow artery. This initial period is called <em>isovolumetric contraction</em> and will be discussed in the Cardiac Cycle chapter.</p></details>

**5. During atrial systole, are the ventricles also in systole?**

<details><summary>Reveal explanation</summary><p>Normally, no. Atrial systole occurs near the end of ventricular diastole, allowing atrial contraction to contribute to ventricular filling before ventricular systole begins.</p></details>
