# Cardiac Output and the Frank–Starling Mechanism

## Definitions and calculations
**Cardiac output (CO)** is blood pumped by one ventricle per minute: **CO = heart rate × stroke volume**. At HR 70/min and SV 70 mL, CO ≈ 4.9 L/min. The **cardiac index** expresses output per body-surface area. Over time the outputs of the right and left ventricles are essentially equal.

## What determines stroke volume?
- **Preload**: initial ventricular fibre stretch before contraction, approximated clinically by end-diastolic volume/pressure (with limitations).
- **Contractility (inotropy)**: intrinsic ability to develop force independent of changes in preload and afterload.
- **Afterload**: load opposing ejection, related to arterial pressure, vascular impedance and ventricular wall stress.

## Frank–Starling: why filling affects ejection
Within physiological limits, more ventricular filling stretches cardiac fibres, improving force generation and stroke volume. This helps the heart match output to venous return. On a plot of stroke volume versus end-diastolic volume, increased contractility shifts the curve **upward**; reduced contractility shifts it **downward**. Do not treat changes in contractility as simply the same thing as more preload.

## Sympathetic stimulation
β₁-adrenoceptor signalling increases pacemaker activity and intracellular Ca²⁺ cycling, producing positive **chronotropy** (higher HR) and positive **inotropy** (stronger contraction). Extreme tachycardia, however, shortens filling time and can **reduce** SV enough to offset the rise in HR.

Exercise often increases CO via both HR and SV. Heart failure may blunt the ability to increase SV despite high filling pressures.

**Check:** Venous return falls sharply but HR rises. Must cardiac output increase?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. CO = HR × SV. The fall in preload can markedly lower SV; compensatory tachycardia may be insufficient and very high HR can further shorten filling.</details>

## Frank–Starling curves are families, not one fixed line
The diagrams in Circulation 1 **slides 39–41** plot ventricular performance against filling (often EDV or atrial pressure). As filling increases within a physiological range, increased sarcomere length improves force development, so the heart ejects more blood. **Higher contractility** shifts the ventricular function curve upward: for the same filling, SV/CO is greater. **Heart failure** usually places performance on a lower curve. Moving **along** a curve by changing preload is different from shifting the curve by changing inotropic state.

The steep ascending limb is most useful for ordinary clinical reasoning. The descending portion sometimes drawn at extreme stretch should not be assumed to occur routinely in an intact human heart.

## What changes EDV and what changes contractility?
EDV rises with increased circulating volume, venoconstriction, enhanced skeletal-muscle or respiratory pumping and longer filling time, provided the ventricle can relax and expand. It falls with blood loss, standing-related venous pooling, increased intrapericardial pressure (e.g. tamponade) or reduced ventricular compliance.

**β₁-adrenoceptor** stimulation activates Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA, increasing L-type Ca²⁺ current and SR Ca²⁺ cycling. This produces **positive inotropy** and often faster relaxation (**positive lusitropy**). Hypoxia, marked acidosis and myocardial damage may depress contractility. *Inotropy* (force at matched loading), *chronotropy* (rate) and *dromotropy* (conduction speed) are different effects.

A rise in afterload generally makes LV ejection more difficult; cardiac oxygen demand rises with wall stress. During exercise, HR, contractility and venous return increase, and active-muscle arterioles dilate; the combination can markedly increase CO.

**Practice:** If two ventricles have the same EDV but one produces a larger SV, a difference in **contractility or afterload**, rather than filling alone, may explain it.
