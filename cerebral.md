# Cerebral Circulation, CSF and the Blood–Brain Barrier

## 1. Why the brain can't wait for blood

The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about **15% of the cardiac output** and about **20% of the body's oxygen** at rest. Yet it stores almost no oxygen and very little glucose. So it depends on a **continuous** blood supply:

- About **10 seconds** without blood flow causes **unconsciousness**.
- A few minutes causes permanent damage.

**Not every part is equally fragile.** The cerebral cortex, **basal ganglia** and **thalamus** are the most sensitive to low oxygen. The **brainstem**, which runs breathing and circulation, is more resistant. That is why a patient can survive a severe hypoxic injury with brainstem functions intact.

**Fuel:** glucose supplies about **90%** of the brain's energy. Brain cells take it up **without needing insulin**. So **hypoglycaemia** hits the brain fast: confusion, then **convulsions** and **coma**. In **prolonged starvation**, the brain switches partly to **ketone bodies**, made by the liver from fat.

<aside class="source-note"><strong>Source note:</strong> The lecture says that in starvation the brain uses "other substances (amino acids, lipids and proteins)". More precisely, the main alternative fuel is ketone bodies, which the liver makes from fatty acids; ordinary fatty acids are not a major brain fuel.</aside>

## 2. Where does the blood come from?

<figure class="teaching-illustration regional-anatomy-figure"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Circle_of_Willis_en.svg" alt="Detailed inferior view of cerebral arterial anatomy showing vertebral and basilar arteries, internal carotids, anterior communicating arteries, posterior communicating arteries and branches of the Circle of Willis."><figcaption><strong>The real anatomical arrangement of the Circle of Willis.</strong><details class="figure-attribution"><summary>Image credit</summary><span class="figure-credit">Rhcastilhos, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Circle_of_Willis_en.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wikimedia Commons</a> · public domain · unmodified.</span></details></figcaption></figure>

Two pairs of arteries supply the brain:

- The two **internal carotid arteries** supply most of the front and middle of the brain.
- The two **vertebral arteries** join to form the **basilar artery**, which supplies the back of the brain and the brainstem.

At the base of the brain, these connect in a ring: the **Circle of Willis**. In principle, if one feeding artery narrows slowly, blood can come round the ring from the others. In practice, the ring is often incomplete or has thin segments, so it can't always compensate.

**Beyond the ring, there is little backup.** The cerebral arteries that leave the circle have **few connections (anastomoses)** with each other. So when one is blocked suddenly, the tissue it supplies has no other source, and it dies.

| Artery | Main territory | Blockage typically affects |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior cerebral (ACA)** | Inner (medial) surface of the frontal and parietal lobes | The opposite **leg** |
| **Middle cerebral (MCA)** | Most of the outer (lateral) surface of the hemisphere | The opposite **face and arm**; **speech**, if the dominant side |
| **Posterior cerebral (PCA)** | Occipital lobe | **Vision** |

The **middle cerebral artery** is a branch of the internal carotid. It is **not** part of the ring itself.

## 3. How does the brain keep its blood flow steady?

**Autoregulation.** When perfusion pressure rises, the cerebral arterioles **constrict**; when it falls, they **dilate**. So cerebral blood flow stays nearly constant across a wide range of blood pressures (classically quoted as a <span data-term="map">mean arterial pressure</span> of about 60–150 mmHg; the limits vary between people). Outside that range, or after a brain injury, flow simply follows pressure. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/local-regulation">→ Local Regulation</a>)*

**Carbon dioxide: the strongest controller.** A rise in arterial CO₂ raises H⁺ around the vessels, and the arterioles **dilate**, so flow rises. A fall in CO₂ **constricts** them. That is why **overbreathing** (which blows off CO₂) can make you **dizzy**: cerebral blood flow falls. Severe hypoxia also dilates cerebral vessels.

**Active areas get more blood.** When a brain region works harder, local signals dilate its arterioles, so flow rises **just in that region**. This is what brain-activity scans detect.

**Nerves play only a small part.** Cerebral vessels are supplied by **sympathetic (adrenergic)**, **cholinergic** and **sensory** nerves. The sensory nerves explain why the **vessels and meninges feel pain** (a cause of headache), although brain tissue itself does not.

## 4. A rigid box: intracranial pressure and perfusion

**The Monro–Kellie doctrine.** The skull is a closed, rigid box. It holds three things whose total volume stays nearly constant:

- **Brain**, about 1400 g
- **Blood**, about 75 mL
- **CSF**, about 75 mL

If one increases (a bleed, a tumour, swelling), something else must leave. At first, CSF and venous blood are squeezed out, so the pressure barely rises. Once that reserve is used up, even a small extra volume makes the **intracranial pressure (ICP)** shoot up, and the rising pressure **compresses the cerebral vessels**.

**Cerebral perfusion pressure: the pressure that actually drives blood through the brain.**

**CPP = MAP − ICP**

- Normal: MAP 90, ICP 10 → **CPP 80 mmHg**.
- ICP rises to 35, MAP still 90 → **CPP 55 mmHg**.

The arm blood pressure hasn't changed, yet the brain is getting less blood.

**The Cushing reflex: a last-ditch defence.** When ICP climbs very high (the lecture gives more than about **33 mmHg**), the brainstem itself becomes ischaemic. Its <span data-term="vasomotor-centre">vasomotor centre</span> fires a massive **sympathetic** discharge that **raises arterial pressure**, trying to force blood back in. The <span data-term="baroreceptors">baroreceptors</span> detect the high pressure and **slow the heart**. **Irregular breathing** may follow.

So the **Cushing triad** is **high blood pressure, slow heart rate and irregular breathing**. It is a **late, dangerous sign**: the brain is about to be compressed past its limit. *(<a href="#/physiology/cvs/reflexes">→ Reflexes</a>)*

## 5. The coverings, the ventricles and CSF

<figure class="teaching-illustration regional-anatomy-figure"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Meninges-en.svg" alt="Cross-sectional anatomical illustration identifying dura mater, arachnoid mater, subarachnoid space and pia mater"><figcaption><strong>Meninges and the subarachnoid compartment.</strong><details class="figure-attribution"><summary>Image credit</summary><span class="figure-credit">Mysid and Wikimedia contributors, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Meninges-en.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wikimedia Commons</a> · <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a> · unmodified.</span></details></figcaption></figure>

**The meninges**, from outside in:

1. **Dura mater:** tough, attached to the skull.
2. **Arachnoid mater:** thin, joined to the brain surface by fine strands (trabeculae).
3. **Subarachnoid space:** contains **CSF** and the cerebral blood vessels.
4. **Pia mater:** clings to the brain surface, following every fold.

<aside class="source-note"><strong>Source note:</strong> The lecture says the subdural space also contains CSF. In normal anatomy, the subdural space is only a potential space; CSF lies in the subarachnoid space.</aside>

**Where CSF is made and where it goes.** CSF is made mainly by the **choroid plexus** in the lateral, third and fourth ventricles. It then flows:

1. **Lateral ventricles** → through the **foramen of Monro** (interventricular foramen) →
2. **Third ventricle** → through the **aqueduct of Sylvius** (cerebral aqueduct) →
3. **Fourth ventricle** → out through the **foramen of Magendie** (middle) and the **foramina of Luschka** (sides) →
4. **Subarachnoid space**, around the brain and spinal cord →
5. Absorbed through the **arachnoid villi** into the venous sinuses, and so into the blood.

| CSF value | Typical adult |
|---|---|
| Volume | About **150 mL** |
| Production | About **550 mL/day**, so it is replaced several times a day |
| Pressure (lumbar, lying down) | **70–180 mm CSF** |
| Protein | About **20 mg/dL**, far less than plasma |
| Glucose | About **64 mg/dL**, roughly two-thirds of blood glucose |

**What CSF does:**

- **Cushions** the brain against blows to the head.
- **Buoyancy:** a brain weighing about 1400 g in air weighs only about **50 g** in CSF, so it doesn't crush its own base.
- **Removes waste:** the one-way flow into the blood carries metabolites and drugs away.
- **Carries hormones** to distant parts of the brain.

**Hydrocephalus: when CSF builds up.**

- **Non-communicating (obstructive):** something **blocks the pathway** inside the ventricles, for example at the narrow aqueduct. The ventricles upstream of the block swell.
- **Communicating:** the ventricles connect normally, but the **arachnoid villi can't absorb** CSF fast enough.

**Lumbar puncture** samples CSF from the **subarachnoid space in the lower back**, below where the spinal cord ends. If <span data-term="icp">ICP</span> may be raised by a mass, it must be checked first, because releasing pressure from below can pull the brain downwards.

## 6. The blood–brain barrier

<figure class="teaching-illustration regional-anatomy-figure"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Blood_brain_barrier.png" alt="Comparison of brain capillary endothelial barrier with the more permeable capillaries of other tissues"><figcaption><strong>What makes cerebral capillaries different?</strong><details class="figure-attribution"><summary>Image credit</summary><span class="figure-credit">David Nascari and Alan Sved, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Blood_brain_barrier.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wikimedia Commons</a> · <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a> · unmodified.</span></details></figcaption></figure>

In most capillaries, small molecules slip through gaps between the endothelial cells. In the brain, the endothelial cells are sealed together by **tight junctions**, and there are no gaps or pores. So anything leaving the blood must pass **through** the cells. This is the **blood–brain barrier**, and it lies in the **capillary endothelium**. Surrounding <span data-term="pericytes">pericytes</span> and astrocyte endfeet help maintain it.

| Crosses easily | Crosses poorly |
|---|---|
| **Water, O₂, CO₂** | **Proteins** |
| **Lipid-soluble** substances (e.g. steroid hormones) | Substances **bound to plasma proteins** |
| **Glucose**, quickly, but only by a **carrier** (GLUT1) | Most **ions and water-soluble** molecules |

**Why have it?** It keeps the neurons' environment **constant**, despite swings in blood ions and hormones, and **shields** them from toxins and circulating antigens.

**Clinical points:**

- A drug acting on the brain must be able to **cross** the barrier.
- The barrier **breaks down** in infection, injury, tumours and inflammation.

The **<span data-term="choroid-plexus">choroid plexus</span>** has its own barrier between blood and CSF, but there the seal is formed by the **epithelial cells**, not the capillaries.

## 7. Stroke

A **stroke** is a sudden loss of blood supply to part of the brain, causing ischaemia and cell death. The signs depend on which artery is affected (<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cerebral?section=2-where-does-the-blood-come-from">§2</a>).

| | **Ischaemic stroke** | **Haemorrhagic stroke** |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | An artery is **blocked** | An artery **ruptures** and bleeds |
| Causes | **Atherosclerotic plaque**, **thrombus** or **embolus** | Often a ruptured **aneurysm** |
| Extra danger | — | The blood adds volume, so **ICP rises** (<a href="#/physiology/cvs/cerebral?section=4-a-rigid-box-intracranial-pressure-and-perfusion">§4</a>) |

## 8. Chapter Essentials

1. **The brain can't store oxygen:** about 10 seconds without flow causes unconsciousness. The cortex, basal ganglia and thalamus are most sensitive; the brainstem is more resistant.
2. **Fuel:** glucose (about 90%), taken up without insulin. Prolonged starvation: ketone bodies.
3. **Supply:** internal carotids + vertebrals → basilar, joined in the **Circle of Willis**. Beyond the ring, **little anastomosis**.
4. **Autoregulation** keeps flow steady across a wide pressure range. **CO₂** is the strongest controller: high CO₂ dilates, low CO₂ constricts.
5. **Monro–Kellie:** brain + blood + CSF in a rigid skull. **CPP = MAP − ICP.**
6. **Cushing reflex:** very high ICP → brainstem ischaemia → hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing. A late sign.
7. **CSF:** made by the choroid plexus; lateral → Monro → third → Sylvius → fourth → Magendie/Luschka → subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi. About 150 mL, about 550 mL/day.
8. **Hydrocephalus:** non-communicating (pathway blocked) or communicating (absorption fails).
9. **Blood–brain barrier:** tight junctions in the capillary endothelium. Water, gases and lipid-soluble substances cross; glucose crosses by carrier; proteins don't.
10. **Stroke:** ischaemic (blocked) or haemorrhagic (ruptured, often an aneurysm).
