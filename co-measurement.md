# Measurement of Cardiac Output and Cardiac Work

## Fick principle
The amount of oxygen removed from blood each minute equals blood flow times the arterial–mixed venous oxygen content difference: **CO = VO₂ ÷ (CaO₂ − CvO₂)**. Use consistent units: if oxygen consumption is 250 mL/min and the content difference is 50 mL/L, CO = **5 L/min**.

The venous sample for systemic Fick cardiac output must approximate **mixed venous blood**, traditionally drawn from the pulmonary artery—not merely any peripheral vein. Whole-body oxygen consumption and paired oxygen content are required.

## Indicator dilution and thermodilution
An indicator injected upstream appears downstream as a concentration–time curve. After correcting for dose and units, **more rapid dilution** generally reflects greater flow. **Thermodilution** uses a temperature change instead of dye and can estimate CO through pulmonary-artery catheter measurements. Measurements have limitations in settings such as major tricuspid regurgitation or shunts.

**Echocardiography** can estimate SV from left ventricular outflow tract cross-sectional area × velocity–time integral; then CO = SV × HR.

## Work performed by the heart
Ventricular **stroke work** is approximately the area of the pressure–volume loop (∮P dV). A useful approximation is ejected volume times the pressure against which it ejects, though true work depends on the changing pressure throughout the beat. Myocardial oxygen demand rises with heart rate, contractility and wall stress.

## Distinguish output measurement from local blood-flow measurement
**Fick's principle** is a mass-balance relation: oxygen consumption per unit time equals blood flow × the difference between entering and leaving oxygen content. To estimate whole-body CO, the oxygen content difference must use **systemic arterial and mixed venous** samples. The pulmonary artery conveniently contains mixed systemic venous blood.

For **indicator dilution**, known indicator quantity injected upstream is related to the integrated downstream concentration–time curve; with constant dose and proper correction, a smaller integrated concentration area corresponds to larger flow. **Thermodilution** substitutes a temperature-change curve, but mixing, valve regurgitation and shunts can introduce error.

The lecturer also names three methods for **regional blood flow** (Circulation 1, slide 62): an **electromagnetic flowmeter** senses induced voltage when conducting blood moves in a magnetic field; **Doppler ultrasound** estimates velocity from reflected frequency shift; and **plethysmography** detects volume changes in a tissue or limb as an indirect index of blood flow. They are not interchangeable with measuring whole-body cardiac output.

## Understand cardiac work and oxygen demand
Approximate external **stroke work ≈ mean ejection pressure × stroke volume**, whereas actual LV pressure–volume loop area integrates the varying pressure through contraction/ejection. Raising **afterload** tends to increase pressure work and wall stress; pressure-overload lesions such as aortic stenosis can strongly increase myocardial oxygen demand. Coronary venous blood already has low oxygen content because myocardium extracts a large fraction at rest, so increased oxygen demand is met substantially by **increased coronary flow**.

**Practice:** A patient has the same SV but a much higher aortic pressure. Pressure work and myocardial oxygen requirements generally rise, even though the amount of blood ejected per beat is unchanged.
