# Circulatory Shock

## What shock actually means
**Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery/utilisation relative to metabolic needs**, producing cellular dysfunction. A low measured BP is common in advanced shock but not required early. Compensatory vasoconstriction can maintain MAP temporarily while tissue perfusion remains inadequate.

## Four mechanistic categories
- **Hypovolaemic:** reduced effective intravascular volume—haemorrhage, substantial fluid loss, burns.
- **Cardiogenic:** inadequate pump performance—large myocardial infarction, severe myocardial dysfunction or arrhythmia.
- **Distributive:** pathologically reduced vascular tone and maldistributed flow—sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic causes.
- **Obstructive:** mechanical impediment to filling or flow—cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive pulmonary embolism.

## Trace haemorrhagic shock in sequence
Blood loss → reduced circulating volume → reduced venous return → reduced preload → reduced stroke volume → reduced CO → inadequate tissue oxygen delivery.

The baroreflex stimulates tachycardia and arteriolar/venous constriction; RAAS and ADH promote retention of fluid. Skin may become cool/pale due to vasoconstriction; renal perfusion may fall, reducing urine output. Respiratory rate may increase. When oxygen delivery is inadequate, anaerobic metabolism contributes to **lactate accumulation and metabolic acidosis**.

## Why compensation eventually fails
Persistent ischaemia and acidosis impair myocardial contraction and vascular responsiveness, while endothelial/microcirculatory injury can worsen capillary leakage and cellular injury. This creates reinforcing feedback loops toward progressive shock and multiorgan dysfunction.

## The blood test trap
Immediately after whole-blood loss, **Hb concentration and haematocrit can remain near initial values**, because plasma and red cells are lost together. Later interstitial fluid entry or fluid treatment dilutes remaining red cells and measured Hb/Hct decrease.

**Check:** A patient is tachycardic with cold extremities but near-normal MAP after bleeding. Can they still be in shock?

## Link individual signs to compensation and perfusion
In **early compensated haemorrhage**, sympathetic discharge increases HR, contractility, arteriolar resistance and venous tone. This supports central BP at the expense of reduced cutaneous and sometimes renal perfusion, producing **cool pale skin**, a fast pulse and reduced urine production. Respiratory drive may increase because of stress, acidosis and chemoreceptor signals. In **septic distributive shock**, however, extremities can initially be warm: avoid treating 'cold skin' as a universal sign of every shock category.

Low cellular oxygen delivery can increase **anaerobic glycolytic flux**, **lactate** and metabolic acidosis. Prolonged hypoperfusion damages vascular endothelium, impairs myocardial performance and weakens responsiveness to constrictor signals, creating **positive feedback** toward worsening shock. 'Irreversible shock' is a descriptive endpoint when injury has advanced beyond successful rescue; exact timing is not predictable from a single diagram.

## Explain fluid shifts and the haemoglobin trap over time
When whole blood is lost, **RBCs and plasma are removed together**, so Hb concentration and haematocrit may be little changed **immediately**. Over ensuing hours, movement of interstitial fluid into vessels and IV fluids dilute the remaining red cells, lowering measured Hb/Hct. **RAAS, ADH and thirst** help replace circulating volume, while **erythropoietin-driven erythropoiesis** restores red-cell mass over weeks, not immediately. The lecturer's 12–72 hour volume recovery and several-week red-cell recovery are broad illustrative time frames, not promises for severe untreated bleeding.

## Classify causes by mechanism, not only symptom
A large **myocardial infarction** reduces pumping ability (cardiogenic); **tamponade, massive pulmonary embolism or tension pneumothorax** obstruct filling/flow (obstructive); **anaphylaxis and severe sepsis** reduce effective vascular tone and can increase capillary leak (distributive); **haemorrhage, burns and severe gastrointestinal fluid loss** reduce effective volume (hypovolaemic). Multiple categories can overlap in one patient.

**Practice:** Why is a normal Hb immediately after bleeding not reassuring? It measures **concentration**, which may stay similar when red cells and plasma are lost together; volume and oxygen delivery may already be dangerously reduced.
